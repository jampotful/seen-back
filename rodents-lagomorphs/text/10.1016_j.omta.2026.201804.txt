Optimization of AAV tools to target Müller glial cells for retinal gene therapy.

# Optimization of AAV tools to target Müller glial cells for retinal gene therapy

## Abstract
Reprogramming of Müller glial (MG) cells into retinal neurons has the potential to treat vision loss by regenerating the retina. Development of efficient gene delivery systems to target the MG cells is critical for this approach. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) serotypes and promoter specificity are important factors that influence AAV transduction profile in the retina. However, studies optimizing these parameters to specifically target MG cells are limited, particularly in rats which are widely used for eye research. In this study, we tested 4 AAV serotypes and 13 promoters to optimize gene delivery to human MG cells in vitro and/or rat MG cells in vivo. We showed that the combinatorial use of MG-specific serotypes and promoters achieved high specificity for MG cell targeting, with ShH10Y serotype and the GFAP (gfaABC1D) promoter as the best performing tool to target rat MG cells in vivo. We developed AAV vectors using known and novel MG-specific promoters and engineered short promoter variants to improve the cargo capacity of AAV delivery. Our results highlighted a panel of promoters that can target MG cells in vitro or in vivo. This study further expands the AAV toolbox for MG-targeted gene delivery, which has important implications for retinal gene therapy.

## Introduction
Retinal degeneration is one of the major causes of irreversible vision loss worldwide, affecting millions of people with conditions such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD), glaucoma, inherited retinal diseases (IRDs), and diabetic retinopathy. Gene therapy provides a promising strategy to treat retinal diseases that are currently incurable, and adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) have emerged as one of the most widely used vectors to deliver gene therapies to the retina. Notably, Luxturna (Voretigene neparvovec) became the first AAV-based retinal gene therapy to be approved by therapeutic regulatory organizations in many countries. Luxturna is a treatment for retinal degeneration caused by RPE65 mutation including IRDs such as Leber’s congenital amaurosis, which uses the AAV2 serotype to deliver a healthy copy of the gene RPE65 through subretinal injections.1
Recombinant AAVs have a favorable safety profile in the retina, with minimal risk of genome integration2 and relatively low immunogenicity.3 Recent advances in AAV engineering allow the targeting of particular retinal cells with unprecedented specificity. In particular, the capsid of different AAV serotypes determines their immunogenicity, stability, and transduction profiles.4 AAV2 is the most commonly studied serotype for gene transfer in the retina, since it has shown effective transduction of retinal neurons, the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), and glial cells.5,6 Other AAV serotypes exhibit different transduction profiles in the retina, and numerous studies have shown that the route of delivery plays a critical role in determining the transduction pattern.7,8 Notably, anatomical barriers within the eye can affect the transduction profile of AAVs, which include the vitreous, inner limiting membrane (ILM), retinal cell density, and the subretinal space. To address this, AAV capsids have been engineered to improve transduction properties, such as increased transduction yield through intravitreal (IVT) injections (e.g., 7m89 and GL10) or higher specificity for retinal cell types (ShH1011 and Anc8012).
In recent years, the Müller glial (MG) cells have emerged as an interesting target for regenerative therapies. Interestingly, MG cells in some vertebrates like teleost fish can dedifferentiate into retinal progenitors upon injury and regenerate the lost retinal cells.13 Similar regenerative ability is also observed in amphibians and chicken to some extent.14 However, in mammals the regenerative capacity of MG cells is limited. Notably, cellular reprogramming studies in rodents have highlighted the potential of using reprogramming genes to stimulate MG cells to become retinal neurons. For instance, the transcription factor Ascl1 has been extensively studied in regenerative strategies harnessing the therapeutic potential of MG reprogramming.15,16 The in vivo reprogramming of MG cells offers the remarkable possibility of regenerating retinal cells in situ, which allows regenerative treatments to potentially overcome some of the limitations associated with cell-based therapies that require transplantation.
To achieve this promising therapeutic potential, the development of gene delivery methods that can specifically target MG cells has become a critical factor to ensure the efficacy and safety of these regenerative treatments. Directed evolution studies using libraries of AAV capsids have identified new AAV serotypes that have higher affinity for MG transduction, such as the AAV6 variant ShH1011 and the AAV2 variant M4.17 However, these engineered serotypes can still target other retinal cell types, albeit less efficiently compared to MG cells. Therefore, the combinatorial use of MG-specific promoters and AAV serotypes can further improve the specificity of targeted gene expression in MG cells. While previous research has extensively studied the transduction efficacy of different AAV tools in mouse MG cells,18,19 the transduction profiles of some serotype and promoter combinations in rat models remain largely understudied. Rats represent a valuable model for retinal research offering highly characterized pre-clinical lines for IRDs such as the transgenic mutant rhodopsin lines (P23H and S334ter) and the Royal College of Surgeons (RCS) rat.20,21
In this study, we performed a head-to-head comparison of the specificity for MG cell transduction in rats using four AAV serotypes: AAV2, M4, ShH10,11 and its variant ShH10Y.22 We tested 13 human promoters in their ability to drive gene expression in human and rat MG cells, including GFAP, GLAST, HES1, TRDN, and CP and their engineered variants. Promoter specificity was studied in vitro using a human MG model and in vivo delivering AAVs with IVT injections to the rat retina. Our results provide a valuable benchmark for pre-clinical studies targeting rat MG cells. Furthermore, our engineered promoter variants represent the foundation for future synthetic promoter engineering, thus expanding the tool kit of AAV systems tailored for MG-targeted therapies.

## Results

## Müller glia transduction using different AAV serotypes in rats
To perform a head-to-head comparison of AAV tools for targeted gene delivery to rat MG, we investigated the transduction profile of four AAV serotypes, AAV2, ShH10, ShH10Y, and M4. First, we paired the serotypes AAV2, ShH10Y, and M4 with the ubiquitous promoter CB7. Derived from the classic CAG promoter, CB7 is a promoter optimized to drive strong ubiquitous gene expression using AAV vectors.23 Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats received IVT injections to deliver these AAV systems to the retina (Figure 1A). To increase the specificity of transgene expression in MG cells, we paired these AAV serotypes with the short glial-specific promoter gfaABC1D, which is a variation from the GFAP promoter (Figures S1A–S1E). Following IVT delivery, immunohistochemistry analysis showed that gene transfer to MG cells was increased using these capsid promoter combinations (Figures S1F–S1I). Assessment of GFP co-localization with the MG marker CRALBP confirmed that all four capsids AAV2, ShH10, ShH10Y, and M4 were capable of driving gene expression MG cells (Figures 1E–1G; Figure S2A). However, GFP expression was also detected in some CRALBP negative cells between the ganglion cell (GCL) and inner nuclear (INL) layers, indicating varying degrees of non-specific activity among the tested AAV vectors.
AAV specificity analysis showed that ShH10Y achieved the highest specificity (Figures 1H and 1I) for targeting rat MG cells (95% ± 1.6%). The parental capsid ShH10 demonstrated comparable specificity for targeting MG (91% ± 1%), but the transduction efficiency was lower than ShH10Y (Figures S2B–S2C). Therefore, we selected ShH10Y over ShH10 for subsequent experiments.
AAV2.GFAP was significantly the least specific combination with 75% ± 1.9%. Notably, the AAV2 variant M4 showed a significant increase in specificity for rat MG transduction compared to the parental AAV2 capsid (97% ± 2.1%), rarely targeting other retinal cells when it was paired with the GFAP promoter. However, the transduction efficiency of M4 was lower compared to AAV2 and ShH10Y, with only a small number of GFP+ MG cells detected in vivo (Figure 1I).
Additionally, negative controls using sham (saline) injection and IgG isotype control were clean confirming the specificity of our assays (Figures S3A–S3F). Overall, the results of our head-to-head comparison for AAV tools in rat retina expand on previous reports from rodent studies,11,17,22 confirming that all four serotypes AAV2, ShH10, ShH10Y, and M4 can target rat MG cells in vivo using IVT injections. Notably, the combinations of engineered capsids ShH10, ShH10Y, and M4 paired with the GFAP promoter demonstrated significantly higher specificity for targeting rat MG cells compared to AAV2, with ShH10Y.GFAP the most effective combination.

## Exploring GLAST and HES1 as alternative promoters to target MG cells in rats
Our capsid comparison assays confirmed that ShH10Y with the short gfaABC1D GFAP promoter is an effective combination for targeting rat MG cells. Nevertheless, the identification of novel promoter sequences to drive MG-specific gene expression represents a valuable resource for optimizing AAV tools for broader applications in MG-targeted gene therapies. Therefore, we investigated two other glia specific promoters, GLAST24 and HES1,25 which could be coupled to the ShH10Y serotype to specifically target MG cells. To facilitate clinical translation, we tested the human sequences of the GLAST and HES1 promoters. We compared the transduction specificity for rat MG using GLAST, HES1, and the CB7 promoters paired with the ShH10Y capsid. Following IVT injections, histological analysis showed that CB7 and GLAST promoters drove strong GFP expression in MG cells (Figures 2A and 2B). ShH10Y.GLAST effectively drove GFP expression in CRALBP+ MG cells. Nevertheless, GFP was also detected in CRALBP negative cells between the GCL and INL layers (Figure 2B, yellow arrows). Quantification analysis confirmed that the GLAST promoter was significantly more specific in targeting MG cells compared to the ubiquitous promoter CB7 (Figure 2D), with a 76% ± 3.8% specificity for GLAST promoter compared to 47.4% ± 7.9% specificity for CB7 promoter. Nevertheless, the transduction efficiency between these two promoters was comparable (Figure 2E). In comparison, the HES1 promoter only targeted a small population of retinal cells including MG, exhibiting a specificity of 49.1% ± 5.8% and the lowest efficiency from these three promoters (Figures 2D and 2E). These results indicate that the human GLAST promoter can be coupled with ShH10Y to specifically target rat MG cells, providing an interesting promoter alternative and a starting point for developing novel synthetic promoters for MG-targeted gene therapy.

## Development of short GLAST and HES1 promoters to improve AAV packaging capacity
Given that the size of the human GLAST promoter tested is over 2 kb, we engineered shorter variants to improve the packaging capacity of AAVs carrying this regulatory element. Promoter composition such as enhancers, transcription factor binding sites, and other cis-regulatory elements are key factors that dictate promoter activity and tissue specificity. We performed in silico analysis considering features such as FANTOM5 cap analysis of gene expression (CAGE) peaks, transcription factor binding sites, chromatin topology, and predicted cis-elements to identify five candidate regulatory regions for the GLAST promoter (marked as A–E, Figure 3A). We employed the human MG cell line MIO-M1 to test the functionality of these short GLAST promoters in vitro. Notably, transfection of the AAV plasmids showed that all five short GLAST promoters could drive GFP expression in human MG cells, with the GLAST(EABCD) promoter being the largest and least efficient, thus it was excluded from further studies (Figures S4A–S4G). We further validated these results by packaging the GLAST vectors with the ShH10Y capsid. Transduction of MIO-M1 cells confirmed that pairing these promoters with ShH10Y could deliver expression cassettes with all four short promoters and drive GFP expression, with GLAST(ABC) and GLAST(ABCD) promoters driving the strongest expression (Figures 3C–3G).
Subsequently, AAVs carrying the short promoters GLAST(ABC), GLAST(ABCD), GLAST(BCD), and GLAST(ECD) were delivered to rat retinae through IVT injections. Histological analysis showed that the transduction efficiency of all the AAVs injected was low, with only a few GFP positive cells identified per tissue section analyzed (Figures 3H–3K). Immunohistochemistry analysis using CRALBP expression confirmed that GLAST(ABC), GLAST(ABCD), and GLAST(BCD) could label MG cells in vivo with low efficiency (Figures 3H–3J). Furthermore, we could not detect GFP expression in CRALBP+ MG cells using the GLAST(ECD) promoter in vivo (Figure 3K).
Using a similar design strategy, we constructed vectors with two short human HES1 promoters: HES1(AB) of 626 bp and HES1(C) of 703 bp (Figures 4A and 4B). In vitro analysis of promoter activity by plasmid transfection showed that both short HES1 promoters could drive GFP expression in MIO-M1 cells, albeit only a very small number of GFP+ cells was detected (Figures S5A–S5D). This low efficiency was also observed using the full-length HES1 promoter (Figure S5A). We subsequently validated the full-length and short HES1 promoters in vitro using AAVs, pairing these expression cassettes with the ShH10Y capsid and obtained similar results. We detected a small number of GFP+ cells using full-length HES1 and HES1(AB) promoters, while no GFP+ cells were detected using the HES1(C) promoter, further indicating low efficiency (Figures 4C–4E). Consistent with our in vitro results, in vivo testing of these AAVs through IVT delivery showed a limited number of GFP+ cells in the rat retina. The HES1(C) promoter was able to drive GFP expression in a very small number of CRALBP+ MG cells, but we could not detect GFP in MG cells using the HES1(AB) promoter (Figures 4F and 4G). Altogether, these results suggest that the short GLAST promoters, particularly GLAST(ABC), GLAST(ABCD), and GLAST(BCD), can be used to target human MG cells in vitro, but are mostly inefficient for targeting rat MG cells in vivo by IVT injection. Similarly, the short HES1(AB) promoter showed limited activity in human MG cells in vitro, while AAVs paired with the HES1(C) promoter showed low efficiency for gene delivery in rat MG cells in vivo. Thus, some of these short regulatory sequences represent interesting candidates that could be further optimized into more efficient synthetic promoters to develop novel gene therapy tools to target MG cells.

## Identification of novel candidates for Müller glia-specific promoters
To expand our study beyond known MG-specific promoters, we used a single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset of the human retina we previously reported26 to identify novel promoter candidates that could specifically drive gene expression in MG cells. Differential gene expression analysis identified two genes that were enriched in MG cells within the human retina: TRDN, which encodes for Triadin, a protein involved in calcium release27 and CP, which encodes for a ferroxidase involved in iron oxidation28 (Figures 5A and 5B). The putative human TRDN and CP promoter regions were cloned into our AAV expression cassette for subsequent testing (1.4 and 1.9 kb, respectively). In vitro analysis of promoter activity using MIO-M1 cells confirmed that both TRDN and CP promoters could drive GFP expression in human MG cells following plasmid transfection (Figures S6A and S6B). We performed IVT injections in rats with ShH10Y.TRDN-EGFP and ShH10Y.CP-EGFP AAVs to test the ability of these AAVs to target MG cells. Histological analysis showed that the TRDN promoter could target MG cells and some other retinal cells located in the GCL (Figure 5E). In contrast, AAVs carrying the CP promoter predominantly targeted cells that did not express CRALBP and were usually located in the GCL (Figure 5F). Due to the low efficiency of the CP promoter in targeting MG cells both in vitro and in vivo, this promoter was excluded from subsequent tests. These results suggest that the human TRDN promoter has the potential to target human MG cells and to some extent for rat MG cells in vivo.
To optimize the TRDN promoter length and potentially improve its efficiency, we designed a shorter variant. Three candidate regulatory elements near the transcription start site of TRDN were assembled to form a 702 bp TRDN(ABC) promoter (Figure 5G). In vitro testing showed that the TRDN(ABC) promoter can drive GFP expression in MIO-M1 cells after plasmid transfection and upon transduction with ShH10Y AAVs (Figure 5H; Figures S6C and S6D). Subsequent in vivo testing in rats showed that TRDN(ABC) promoter could drive GFP expression in a small number of CRALBP+ MG cells following IVT delivery (Figure 5I). These results indicate that the full-length and short TRDN promoters could be used for gene delivery in human MG cells in vitro, but further optimization is needed for in vivo applications in rats.

## Discussion
The unique properties of AAV vectors have accelerated gene therapy research, with multiple strategies harnessing the transduction profile of engineered capsids to develop treatments for IRDs.29 For instance, the AAV2 variant 7m8 has improved ability to penetrate and transduce all retinal layers upon IVT delivery in mouse and non-human primates (NHPs),30 which makes this serotype an attractive tool for treatment seeking pan-retinal or photoreceptor transduction. Indeed, the anti-VEGF therapy Ixo-vec uses 7m8 AAVs to deliver the drug aflibercept to the retina as a treatment for neovascular AMD.31
In the context of retinal degenerative diseases, the reprogramming of MG cells into retinal neurons is a promising gene agnostic strategy to regenerate the retina and treat vision loss.32 Thus, development of AAV tools that can specifically target MG cells is critical to boost the efficacy of this approach. This study provides a head-to-head comparison of different AAV tools to specifically target MG in rats, which are one of the most widely used pre-clinical models for retinal research. We compared the transduction specificity of four AAV capsids with demonstrated ability to target retinal neurons and glial cells, AAV2, ShH10, ShH10Y, and M4. Additionally, we assessed 13 human promoters in their ability to drive gene expression in human and rat MG cells, including GFAP, GLAST, HES1, TRDN, and CP and their engineered variants. Our results build on previous studies that have assessed MG transduction specificity in the rodent retina and provide a valuable benchmark for pre-clinical studies in the rat retina.
Notably, the multilayered organization of the retina represents a physical barrier for AAV transduction; structures like the ILM and retinal neuronal processes can restrict AAV penetration.33 ShH10 is a variant of AAV6, for which the primary target on the cell surface is sialic acid.34 In the retina, polysialic acid can be found as a post-translational modification of the neural adhesion molecule NCAM, but it is restricted to MG cells in adult rats.35 Additionally, AAV’s ability to bind heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) influences the transduction efficiency by IVT delivery, as a diverse range of HSPGs can be found in the ILM.36 Although AAV6 has low affinity for HSPG, capsid engineering to increase the affinity for HSPG led to ShH10 with improved ability to transduce MG upon IVT injection in rodents.11 Interestingly, Pellissier et al. reported some species difference in MG cell targeting specificity for ShH10 and ShH10Y viruses in mice compared to rats.22 Our results highlight ShH10Y as the best-performing serotype to target MG cells, which is consistent with this previous study. Given that the selectivity of the ShH10 serotype to transduce glial cells was initially identified using human and rat tissue,11,37 this capsid represents an important resource for targeting MG cells in clinical and pre-clinical studies.
We also analyzed the transduction profile of a recently reported AAV2 variant called M4 that was shown to target human MG cells in combination with the CMV promoter.17 This capsid demonstrated high specificity for MG transduction in human retina explants and retinal organoids. In this study, we identified that the M4 serotype can transduce rat MG cells through IVT delivery with high specificity in combination with the GFAP promoter. However, a low transduction efficiency was observed indicating a reduced ability to transduce the rodent retina. In contrast, we observed high transduction efficiency in human MG cells in vitro, which suggests that the translational utility of this serotype may lie in applications targeting human or NHP MG rather than rodent cells.
Our results identified the GFAP promoter (gfaABC1D) as the best promoter coupled with ShH10Y to target MG cells in rats in vivo. Studies evaluating AAV specificity for MG-targeted gene delivery have largely focused on mice. Consequently, head-to-head comparisons of AAV transduction profiles remain understudied in rats, despite their widespread use as rodent models of retinal degeneration.20 Regenerative therapy research in mice has previously used the ShH10 serotype together with GFAP promoter for MG reprogramming.38,39 Interestingly, research in mice suggested that in some cases the specific transgenes can alter the specificity of GFAP promoter, with ectopic expression observed in a portion of retinal neurons using the 7m8, PHP.eB, and ShH10 serotypes.18,19 Although this has not been shown in rats, these reports highlight the importance of pairing MG-specific promoters and serotypes to enhance the AAV specificity to target MG cells.
Our study analyzed the specificity of alternative glial-specific promoters such as GLAST, HES1, TRDN, and CP for MG-targeted gene delivery, thereby expanding the promoter toolkit available for AAV systems targeting this cells. To facilitate the clinical translation of these AAV tools, we selected the human versions of these promoters for our study. The Glast promoter has been extensively employed to label MG cells in lineage tracing mice,18,19,40 and the HES1 promoter was recently used in vitro in reprogramming studies with human MG cells.41 Our in vivo study with rat retinae further expands these findings. We showed that the IVT delivery of a combination of the GLAST promoter with ShH10Y was able to specifically target rat MG cells. However, the specificity achieved with this combination was still lower compared to the GFAP promoter. Future experiments to analyze the specificity of the human GLAST promoter together with ShH10Y in other clinically relevant species, such as mouse, NHPs, and human retinal explants, would allow a better understanding of the clinical value of this AAV tool for the transduction of MG cells.
In contrast, the other AAV vectors developed in this study carrying the human promoters for HES1, TRDN, and CP were not very effective for targeting rat MG cells in vivo. Notably, the three promoters showed activity in human MG cells in vitro, though the 2.5 kb HES1 promoter tested in this study yielded low efficiency. Of note, during preparation of this manuscript, another study reported that the HES1 promoter was effective in human retinal explants.41 This discrepancy with our results is possibly due to a difference in the selected HES1 promoter sequence, as we tested a 2.5 kb fragment that includes upstream region and the transcription start site for this study. Interestingly, a 310 bp fragment located at the 3′ region of our human HES1 promoter shares 97.5% and 96.8% of homology with the mouse and rat Hes1 promoter regions respectively, which also includes the predicted transcription start site. Future studies to characterize the functionality of the regulatory sequences in the human HES1 promoter could provide valuable data for the generation of more effective synthetic AAV tools to target MG cells.
Additionally, we tested two novel MG-specific promoters CP and TRDN. CP encodes for the protein Ceruloplasmin, which is a multicopper ferroxidase that contributes to the regulation of retinal iron and protects the cells from degeneration caused by iron overload. Notably, it has been shown that MG cells can increase the levels of CP expression after photopic injury.28 On the other hand, TRDN is expressed in the retina (www.proteinatlas.org) and brain,42 though its role in MG cells remains unknown. Our results showed that while the CP and TRDN promoters can drive transgene expression in human MG cells, they were not efficient in targeting the rat MG cells in vivo by IVT injection of ShH10Y AAVs. Future studies to investigate the specificity and efficiency of these promoters in human retinal explants could provide more information about the potential applications of these novel promoters.
The small packaging capacity of AAVs represents a major limitation for AAV-based treatments. In particular, the reprogramming of MG cells into retinal neurons often requires multiple transcription factors.16,38 To improve AAV packaging capacity, we engineered short variants of human GLAST, HES1, and TRDN promoters to target MG cells. Our testing identified at least two short GLAST promoters that could drive gene expression in human MG cells in vitro. However, their ability to target rat MG cells in vivo is limited following IVT injection, suggesting that further optimization is needed. Significant efforts have been made to engineer short promoters for AAV-based retinal gene therapy.43,44,45,46 However, many of the promoters developed for targeting MG cells are larger than 1 kb, which complicates the delivery of multiple transgenes in a single AAV. Notably, our GLAST(ABC) promoter is only 839 bp, which leaves enough space within the AAV vector to potentially fit multiple transgenes. Our short promoter design strategy focused on the identification of sequences in silico using information from CAGE sequence, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq), RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), and Hi-C datasets. A similar approach was employed by Korecki et al. to design a short NR2E1 promoter (1,675 bp) to target mouse MG cells by IVT delivery.43 Future studies to test the activity, specificity of the GLAST(ABC) promoter across different species would be interesting, such as mouse models, NHP, and human retinal explants. Overall, our study has identified potential human promoter candidates that represent a good starting point for the development of synthetic promoters to expand the AAV toolsets to target MG cells.
This study provided a systematic testing of 4 AAV serotypes and 13 promoters to target MG cells both in vitro and in vivo. This head-to-head comparison provides a valuable benchmark for retinal research using rats as a pre-clinical model. Consistent with previous reports, we show that the ShH10Y serotype coupled with a GFAP promoter is an optimal tool to target MG cells with IVT delivery in rats. We have engineered promoter candidates that can be paired with the ShH10Y capsid to target human MG cells in vitro and in vivo, thereby providing new resources for development of optimized tools to target MG cells.

## Materials and methods

## Ethics
The study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee at St Vincent’s Hospital Melbourne (024/21). Wild type SD rats were obtained from the Animal Resources Center and Ozgene (Perth, Australia). The animals were housed at the Biological Research Center (The University of Melbourne, Melbourne, Australia) or EMSU animal facility (St. Vincent’s Hospital, Melbourne, Australia).

## AAV vector design and construction
AAV plasmids were generated through Gibson assembly using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). In short, the AAV expression cassette was generated with restriction enzyme digestion of the plasmid pAAV-GFAP-EGFP (a gift from Bryan Roth, Addgene # 50473). The full-length human promoters were amplified using PCR with human gDNA HES1 (chr3:194,133,991–194,136,531), TRDN (chr6:123,636,303–123,637,799), CP (chr3:149,221,630–149,224,237), and from the plasmid GLASTp-DsRed2 (a gift from Nicholas Gaiano, Addgene #17706) for GLAST (chr5:36,604,634–36,606,688), using a high-fidelity KOD Hot Start DNA Polymerase (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany).
The short promoters were designed by assembling putative regulatory elements for MG-specific genes. The regulatory sequences were selected using the UCSC genome browser (https://genome-euro.ucsc.edu), taking into consideration FANTOM5 CAGE peaks, the eukaryotic promoter database (EPDnew), transcription factor binding sites (JASPAR), open chromatin marks, ChIP-seq data and topologically associating domains (Hi-C). The short promoters were synthesized as gBlock DNA fragments (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA) and assembled into the AAV expression cassette vectors with EGFP reporter.

## Identification of novel MG-specific promoters
To identify MG-specific promoter usage in the human retina, we analyzed our previously reported scRNA-seq dataset.26 The dataset was analyzed using Seurat (v.4.0). MG marker genes were identified using the FindMarkers function by comparing MG cells to all other annotated retinal cell types. Genes with log2 fold change >0.25, pct.1 (MG cells) > 0.5, and pct.2 (all other retina cell types) < 0.05 were retained to yield high-confidence MG-specific candidates including TRDN and CP. Violin plot was used to visualize the expression of TRDN and CP across retinal cell types.

## AAV production
AAVs were generated with a triple transfection method using adherent HEK293D cells. On day 0, 2 × 107 cells were co-transfected with 20 μg of helper plasmid (pXX6), 10 μg of RepCap plasmid, and 10 μg of transfer plasmid using polyethylenimine hydrochloride (PEI; Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA). Three days after transfection, the AAVs were purified and concentrated using the AAVpro Purification Kit Midi (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The RepCap plasmids ShH10 and ShH10Y were gifts from John Flannery and Jan Wijnholds (Addgene #64867). The CB7 plasmid pENN.AAV.CB7.CI.eGFP.WPRE.rBG was a gift from James M. Wilson (Addgene # 105542).

## In vitro testing of promoter activity
In vitro testing of promoter activity was performed by plasmid transfection and AAV transduction. For plasmid transfection tests, AAV plasmids carrying the specific promoter were transfected into the human MG cell line MIO-M1. In brief, the cells were maintained with DMEM high glucose supplemented with GlutaMAX and 10% fetal calf serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). On day 0, 2.4 × 104 cells/well were transfected with 1 μg of AAV plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. On day 1, fresh medium was added, and the samples were processed for immunocytochemistry on day 4–6. Images were taken with an Axio inverted microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) and processed with ZEN 3.2 Blue edition (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).
For AAV transduction, 4 × 103 MIO-M1 cells were treated with AAVs with at least MOI of 1,000,000 on day 0. Promoter activity was analyzed on day 6 post-transduction by immunocytochemistry to monitor EGFP expression. Images were taken with an Axio inverted microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) and processed with ZEN 3.2 Blue edition (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Promoter efficiency was calculated by normalizing the number of GFP positive cells to the surface culture area in mm.2 At least 4 field images per condition across 2 independent experiments were used to quantify GFP positive cells.

## In vivo testing of promoter activity
SD rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 75 mg/kg of Ketamine hydrochloride (Ceva, Hills District, Australia) and 10 mg/kg of Xylazil-20 (Ilium, Blacktown, Australia). The pupils were dilated with one drop of 1% w/v tropicamide (Bausch & Lomb, Bridgewater, NJ, USA) and 2.5% w/v phenylephrine hydrochloride eye drops (Bausch & Lomb, Bridgewater, NJ, USA). Intravitreal injections of AAVs were performed with a surgical microscope with the help of an intravitreal lens (OcuScience, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) placed on the cornea. GenTeal eye lubricant gel was applied to prevent corneal desiccation. A Hamilton syringe with a 34-gauge removable needle was used to inject 3 μL of AAVs (1 × 109–7 × 1010 vg) into the vitreous space of the eye. Two weeks post-injection, the eyes were processed for histology. The animals were euthanized with an overdose of sodium pentobarbitone (Lethabarb, Virbac, Canterbury-Bankstown, Australia). The eyes were enucleated and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) prior to dissection of the anterior eyecup (cornea, iris, and lens). The posterior eyecups were processed for sucrose gradient incubation (10%, 20%, and 30%) and left overnight in 30% (w/v) sucrose at 4°C. Subsequently, the tissue was embedded and snap-frozen in OCT medium (Scigen, Paramount, CA, USA) with an isopentane bath in liquid nitrogen. The frozen tissue samples were cryosectioned at 20 μm thickness using a CryoStar NX70 cryostat (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

## Immunohistochemistry
For immunohistochemistry analysis, the samples were permeabilized with 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 and blocked with 10% (w/v) normal goat serum (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany). Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4°C using the following dilutions: GFP (1/2,000; Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) and CRALBP (1/1,000; Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom). The next day, the samples were washed with PBS and incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies (1/400, Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) for 30 min at room temperature, followed by 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) counterstain to label the nuclei. Coverslips were mounted on the tissue sections using ProLong glass antifade mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Images were taken with an Axio Imager.M2 upright microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) and processed with ZEN 3.2 Blue edition (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

## AAV specificity analysis
AAV specificity was analyzed by quantifying the GFP signal that co-localized with the MG marker CRALBP using the Cell Counter plugin for ImageJ. AAV specificity was calculated as a ratio of the GFP signal that co-localized with MG markers and the total GFP signal quantified. To calculate gene transfer efficiency, the GPF signal was quantified and normalized per mm2. More than 10 field images corresponding to >30 sections were analyzed across 2–3 eyes treated with the AAVs, and more than 200 GFP signals were quantified per condition (only 33 GFP signals for M4 as it showed low efficiency). The statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism using one-way ANOVA and p < 0.05 was used to establish statistical significance.
