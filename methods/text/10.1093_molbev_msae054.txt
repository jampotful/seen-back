Genetic Basis and Evolutionary Forces of Sexually Dimorphic Color Variation in a Toad-Headed Agamid Lizard.

# Genetic Basis and Evolutionary Forces of Sexually Dimorphic Color Variation in a Toad-Headed Agamid Lizard

## Abstract
AbstractIn the animal kingdom, sexually dimorphic color variation is a widespread phenomenon that significantly influences survival and reproductive success. However, the genetic underpinnings of this variation remain inadequately understood. Our investigation into sexually dimorphic color variation in the desert-dwelling Guinan population of the toad-headed agamid lizard (Phrynocephalus putjatai) utilized a multidisciplinary approach, encompassing phenotypic, ultrastructural, biochemical, genomic analyses, and behavioral experiments. Our findings unveil the association between distinct skin colorations and varying levels of carotenoid and pteridine pigments. The red coloration in males is determined by a genomic region on chromosome 14, housing four pigmentation genes: BCO2 and three 6-pyruvoyltetrahydropterin synthases. A Guinan population-specific nonsynonymous single nucleotide polymorphism in BCO2 is predicted to alter the electrostatic potential within the binding domain of the BCO2-β-carotene complex, influencing their interaction. Additionally, the gene MAP7 on chromosome 2 emerges as a potential contributor to the blue coloration in subadults and adult females. Sex-specific expression patterns point to steroid hormone-associated genes (SULT2B1 and SRD5A2) as potential upstream regulators influencing sexually dimorphic coloration. Visual modeling and field experiments support the potential selective advantages of vibrant coloration in desert environments. This implies that natural selection, potentially coupled with assortative mating, might have played a role in fixing color alleles, contributing to prevalence in the local desert habitat. This study provides novel insights into the genetic basis of carotenoid and pteridine-based color variation, shedding light on the evolution of sexually dimorphic coloration in animals. Moreover, it advances our understanding of the driving forces behind such intricate coloration patterns.Keywords: color variation, sexual dimorphism, genetic basis, evolutionary driving forces, lizard

## Introduction
Animal coloration plays a crucial role in adaptation and social communication (Stevens 2013; Caro et al. 2017). Due to the complexity of living environments and social communication contexts, animals often exhibit color variation (Tibbetts et al. 2017; Stuart-Fox et al. 2021), which likely aids in sex recognition, individual identity, and signaling quality or behavioral strategies (Sinervo and Lively 1996; Tibbetts and Dale 2007; i de Lanuza et al. 2014). Lizards, characterized by a remarkable diversity of colors, stand out as one of the most vibrantly colored vertebrate groups. This striking variation, evident within and across species and populations, as well as spanning both sexes, underscores the dynamic interplay between lizards and their intricate ecological niches (Stuart-Fox et al. 2020). The color of lizards, a multifaceted trait, is influenced by various factors, including habitat adaptation, anti-predation strategies (such as camouflage or predator avoidance), and social interactions (for mate attraction or resource defense; Sinervo and Lively 1996; Rosenblum 2006; Corl et al. 2018). The interplay of these selective pressures has likely sculpted the remarkable color variation observed even among different lizard populations today. Understanding the genetic basis of this variation, as lizard color plays a pivotal role in their survival and reproduction, can provide valuable insights into the ecology and evolution of these fascinating creatures. By delving into the genetic mechanisms that control color patterns, we can gain a deeper appreciation for the intricate relationship between lizards and their environment.
While the mechanisms underlying the black pigments (melanin) in lizards are relatively well established (Rosenblum et al. 2010; Jin et al. 2020), the genetic underpinnings of red, orange, and blue colors remain largely enigmatic, particularly in the context of sexually dimorphic coloration. Despite potential explanations have been proposed based on a limited number of loci for the painted dragon lizard (Olsson et al. 2007) and swift dragon lizard (Rankin et al. 2016), and transcriptomic data from the tawny dragon lizard (McLean et al. 2017) and frill-neck lizard (McLean et al. 2019), a comprehensive understanding of the genetic mechanisms governing these diverse colorations remains elusive. Notably, a recent study has identified two small regulatory regions near genes linked to pterin (SPR) and carotenoid (BCO2) metabolism in the common wall lizard (Podarcis muralis), shedding light on a color polymorphism and representing the most detailed study in lizards to date (Andrade et al. 2019).
The toad-headed agamid lizard, Phrynocephalus putjatai, presents an ideal system for unraveling the genetic basis of sexually dimorphic coloration in lizards. Notably, it lacks heteromorphic sex chromosomes, and its body coloration exhibits variability across different geographical regions (Li et al. 2016). Specifically, populations in the Mugetan desert in Guinan County (often referred to as P. guinanensis, GN hereafter) exhibit striking sexual dimorphism and age-related color variation. Adult males exhibit a red coloration, while subadults and adult females display a blue coloration, primarily on their body flanks (Fig. 1a). In contrast, populations in Guide County and the nearby area (GD hereafter) and their close relatives (e.g. P. vlangalii) generally exhibit a subdued coloration. Given their parapatric distribution and the longstanding debate regarding the species status of GN and GD, attributed to their similar genetic background (Jin et al. 2008; Ji et al. 2009; Jin et al. 2014), we hypothesize that the color variation in GN likely arose recently through a limited number of genetic alterations. Additionally, populations residing in Guomaying town (hereafter GM), located between the Mugetan desert and Guide County, display an intermediate phenotype (Fig. 1a), hinting at potential introgression between GN and GD populations. To elucidate the processes governing the formation and maintenance of color variation, we incorporate GM populations into our analyses. The primary objectives of this study are to elucidate the genetic underpinnings of sex-specific color variation in GN populations and to unveil the potential evolutionary forces that have led to the fixation of genes associated with color. Our approach involved a comprehensive investigation across multiple facets. Initially, we quantified color variation across different populations and between sexes. Subsequently, we delved into the biochemical and cellular foundations underlying pigmentation differences. To explore the genetic basis of color variation, we conducted whole genome sequencing on three toad-headed agama individuals (GN, GD, and P. vlangalii). Leveraging the GN chromosome-level assembly as reference, we used population genomics by re-sequencing 100 individuals from diverse color groups. Finally, we integrated field experiments with visual modeling techniques to scrutinize the possible selection pressures influencing the evolution of color variation in GN populations. This multifaceted approach aimed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the genetic, biochemical, and ecological factors contributing to the observed color variations in toad-headed agamas.

## Results

## The Phenotypic, Cellular, and Biochemical Basis of Color Variation
To verify noticeable color differences among GD, GM, and GN populations, as well as sexually dimorphic coloration in the GN populations, we utilized spectrometry to quantify body color. A total of 268 individuals from six populations were examined, comprising GD1 and GD2 from Guide County, GN1 and GN2 from Guinan County, and GM1 and GM2 from Guomaying town (Fig. 1a). The spectral curves (Fig. 1b), the cluster analysis (Fig. 1c and supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online), and the linear model (Fig. 1d and supplementary table S1, Supplementary Material online) based on hue (wavelength of peak reflectance) and chroma (wavelength bandwidth of peak reflectance) collectively affirmed the color distinctions among individuals of GD, GM, and GN populations, as well as the presence of sexually dimorphic coloration in GN populations. Due to the evident color variation, we categorized GD, GM, and GN as distinct color groups to facilitate subsequent comparisons.
To investigate the ultrastructure of skin tissues with different colors, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed. The selected skin patches for TEM analysis were red/blue in color for GN lizards and brown for GD lizards. The lizard skin features a well-known layered system comprising three distinct chromatophore cells arranged from the upper to lower layers: xanthophores, iridophores, and melanophores (Stuart-Fox et al. 2020). Xantophores, known to contain carotenoid and pteridine pigments (Grether et al. 2004; Olsson et al. 2008; Andrade et al. 2019), was successfully identified in the skin of GN males. However, in GN females and GD skin, the xanthophore layer posed challenges in reliable identification and distinction from collagen fiber bundles (Fig. 1e and supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online). Iridophores, containing light-scattering guanine crystals (GCs) known for their potential to absorb light and influence ultraviolet and blue intensity (Morrison et al. 1995; Macedonia et al. 2000; McLean et al. 2017), were found to be abundant in the lateral skin of GN. Specifically, in GN males, iridophores appeared to exhibit an irregular distribution, while in GN females, they presented a strip-shaped, tile-covered arrangement and were densely packed with GCs (Fig. 1e and supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online). In contrast, melanophores, housing melanin pigments, were notably concentrated in the lateral skin of GD (Fig. 1e and supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online), consistent with the darker and duller coloration observed in GD. Further investigation involving a larger sample size and exploration of group-level differences is needed to draw robust conclusions regarding the observed patterns.
To elucidate the biochemical basis of color variation, we employed liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS) to quantify the carotenoid and pterin contents in the skins of both sexes within the GD and GN populations. The outcomes revealed distinct concentrations of specific metabolites corresponding to different skin colors (Fig. 1f). Red skin samples exhibited low levels of yellow pigments, including pteridine xanthopterin, carotenoid β-cryptoxanthin, β-carotene, and lutein (Fig. 1f). Concentrations of β-carotene and xanthopterin were significantly lower in the skin of GN compared to GD (Wilcoxon test P = 0.022 for β-carotene; P = 0.045 for xanthopterin), particularly in GN males for β-carotene (Wilcoxon test P = 0.009; Fig. 1f). Two colorless pteridine pigments, 6-biopterin and isoxanthopterin, potentially serving as precursors to colored pigments, were more abundant in red skin compared to blue and dull skin (Fig. 1f). Therefore, the distinction among red, blue, and dull skin likely stems from varying concentrations of yellow carotenoids and colored pteridines. Nevertheless, the existence of instances showing nonsignificant differences in pigment content, particularly between GN males and females, may be attributed to a few individual outliers and the relatively modest sample size. Generally, the red skin exhibits a comparatively lower concentration of carotenoids and a relatively higher concentration of pteridines compared to the blue and dull skin.
Our results provide insights into the structural and biochemical basis of color variation in lizard populations, highlighting the role of carotenoids and pteridines in shaping the observed differences.

## Chromosome-Level Genome Construction and Phylogenetic Inference
As a resource for studying the genetic underpinnings of color variation in the toad-headed agamid lizards, we generated a chromosome-level reference genome using long-read sequences derived from DNA extracted from a single female GN lizard. Utilizing Hi-C methodology and guided by the established karyotype study (2n = 48; Li et al. 2016), contigs of GN were organized into 24 pseudo-chromosomes, yielding an assembly totaling 1.81 Gb, closely matching the estimated genome size (1.83 Gb) derived from short-read sequences. The assembly exhibited a scaffold N50 of 132.7 Mb, a L50 of 6, and 3.4% gaps (supplementary table S2 and fig. S3, Supplementary Material online). For comparative analysis, we also sequenced and assembled the genomes of GD lizard and P. vlangalii using PacBio long-read sequencing (80× to 105× coverage). A high mapping efficiency of at least 91% of long reads and 98.08% of short reads was observed, with 91.4–92.2% recovery of universal vertebrate single-copy genes (complete BUSCOs; see supplementary fig. S4, Supplementary Material online), affirming the integrity, accuracy, and completeness of the genome assemblies. Chromosomal synteny analysis revealed that toad-headed agamid lizard chromosomes originated from a serious of fission events from Anolis carolinensis chromosomes (supplementary fig. S5, Supplementary Material online).
Our phylogenetic inference (Fig. 2a; supplementary table S3, Supplementary Material online) recovered a sister group relationship between GN and GD lizards. The estimated split time between GN and GD was approximately 2.7 million years ago (mya; Fig. 2a). This estimation was derived from multiple calibration points (supplementary table S4, Supplementary Material online) and aligns with the end of the Pliocene epoch. P. vlangalii diverged from the common ancestor of GN and GD around 6.95 mya. Notably, the Asian toad-headed agamid clade exhibited a relatively high neutral evolution substitution rate (∼1.7 × 10−03 substitutions per site per million years) compared to other squamate species. Interestingly, the bright color GN lizard exhibited a discernible reduction in chemoreception-related (CR) genes, including olfactory receptors (ORs) and vomeronasal receptors type-2 (V2Rs) families (supplementary tables S5, S6 and figs. S6 and S7, Supplementary Material online). This finding prompts inquiries into the potential interplay between visual signaling and chemosensory adaptations.

## Genetic Composition and Evolutionary Scenario of the Three Different Color Groups
To lay the groundwork for unraveling the genetic basis of color variation, our investigation delved into the genetic composition and evolutionary history of the three distinct color groups—GN, GD, and GM. Leveraging population genomic data from 100 individuals, with an average sequencing depth of approximately 11×, we employed principal components analysis (PCA) and ADMIXTURE (Alexander et al. 2009) to delineate clear genetic clusters corresponding to each color group (Fig. 2b and c). Notably, a certain degree of genetic mixing was observed, particularly between GM and the other two groups (Fig. 2c), suggesting potential scenarios of recent shared ancestry, introgressive hybridization, or a combination of both. The individual-based maximum likelihood (ML) tree further illuminated the relationships, revealing that individuals from GM formed a paraphyletic group at the base of the GN clade (Fig. 2d). This pattern aligned with the higher proportion of GN genetic component in the GM populations as evidenced by the ADMIXTURE analysis. This intriguing assignment pattern prompted further exploration into the evolutionary dynamics of these groups.
Examining the pairwise sequentially Markovian coalescent (PSMC) plot, we observed a consistent trend in effective population size (Ne) curves for GN and GD populations (Fig. 2e). Both groups experienced an increase in Ne since their initial divergence at the end of the Pliocene epoch, reaching a peak at the end of the Early Pleistocene (∼0.8 mya; Tian et al. 2018), and subsequently undergoing a sustained decline, with temporary recovery during the early stage of the last glacial period.
The coalescent simulations, encompassing 11 alternative evolutionary scenarios (supplementary fig. S8, Supplementary Material online), pointed toward a secondary contact with admixture as the best-fitting model (model 2; supplementary table S7, Supplementary Material online). This model suggested an ancient isolation and a recently formed hybrid pattern with a southwest-to-northeast migration route. GN and GD diverged without gene flow around 2.6 mya, closely aligning with the molecular clock estimation (∼2.7 mya), later hybridizing at approximately 0.3 mya to give rise to GM (Fig. 2f). The hybrid parameter (α) estimation indicated that around 80% of the GM genome originated from GN, while only approximately 20% came from GD (Fig. 2f). The unequal ancestry contribution was also corroborated by the ancestry assignment based on the ADMIXTURE result (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, since the initial hybridization event between GD and GN, there has been a continuous and asymmetric gene flow, with the predominant migration direction being from GN to GD. This is indicated by the higher rates of gene flow from GN to GD (M13 = 1.5 × 10−5) and GM (M23 = 3.7 × 10−5) compared to the opposite direction (M31 = 2 × 10−6 and M32 = 1.5 × 10−7, respectively; Fig. 2f). Given the restricted distribution of GN, the Pliocene end origin, and its initial isolation without migration pattern, evidence suggests that the Mugetan sandy land where GN lived likely emerged at least 2.6 mya, corresponding primarily to the second phase of the uplift of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (Yuan et al. 2004).
Our evolutionary analyses shed light on the intricate genetic relationships and evolutionary history of these distinct color groups, setting the stage for a deeper understanding of the genetic basis underlying color variation in these populations.

## Genomic Signatures of Selection and Candidates
To pinpoint the genomic region responsible for the color variation, we initially conducted a genome scan focusing on local regions exhibiting extreme divergence in allele frequency (FST) and a reduction in genetic diversity (π). This analysis was performed in non-overlapping 20-kb windows across all chromosomes, utilizing high-depth population sequence data. Global FST was found to be merely 0.07 between GN and GM, 0.11 between GM and GD, and 0.15 between GN and GD, indicating a shallow level of genetic differentiation. To identify loci under selection specifically in the GN populations, we utilized a recently developed FST-based statistic called population branch excess (PBE; Yassin et al. 2016). PBE exhibits heightened sensitivity to increased genetic differentiation solely between the focal population and others. Serving as a refined derivative of population branch statistics (PBS; Shriver et al. 2004; Yi et al. 2010), PBE addresses the constraint of elevated PBS values in scenarios where all populations have high genetic differentiation. Instead, PBE assesses the disparity between observed and expected PBS values. Given the shallow genetic differentiation yet remarkable color variation observed between GN and GM as well as GD, PBE aligns with our objective of detecting color-related loci while minimizing the risk of false positives. Recognizing evident differences in body color between sexes, we conducted separate PBE scans for males and females, as well as for the entire cohort of individuals.
Genome-wide comparisons revealed a limited number of strongly differentiated loci, with top outliers having PBE values greater than 1 primarily clustered within five small genomic regions (Fig. 3a). Notably, irrespective of male–male, female–female, or all individuals’ comparisons, the same top locus was identified, indicating that these highly divergent loci were not linked to sex-specific inheritance. One of these loci, spanning 80 kb on chromosome 14, contained only two pigmentation genes: one beta-carotene oxygenase 2 (BCO2) gene and three copies of PTS.

The BCO2 gene, associated with carotenoid processing, has been linked to non-melanin-based coloration in birds (Eriksson et al. 2008; Toews et al. 2016; Enbody et al. 2021), mammals (Berry et al. 2009; Våge and Boman 2010), and fishes (Lehnert et al. 2019; Ahi et al. 2020). Notably, a recent study on the European common wall lizard (P. muralis) has reported that BCO2 contributes to the observed yellow coloration, while PTS, a crucial enzyme in pteridine metabolism, is potentially implicated in the variation of red color (Andrade et al. 2019). Given our detection of different levels of carotenoid and pterin pigments in the GN compared to the GD populations, BCO2 and PTS emerge as strong candidate genes for the red skin of GN mature males. Intriguingly, we identified a highly divergent gene associated with aggressive behavior, KIRREL3 (Prince et al. 2013), located nearby the BCO2-PTS loci, implying a potential coevolution pattern of aggression and coloration in GN lizards.
Another highly differentiated region spanning 500 kb of chromosome 2 was also noteworthy (Fig. 3a). A gene within this region, annotated as ensconsin or MAP7, plays a crucial role during reorganization of microtubules during polarization and differentiation of epithelial cells (Masson and Kreis 1993). MAP7 is instrumental in triggering intracellular redistribution during induction of keratinocyte terminal differentiation, suggesting a potential involvement in altering scale morphology to mitigate water loss in desert environments. Notably, MAP7 shares a similar function with the gene CCDC170, responsible for female-limited color pattern in brown anole lizard (Anolis sagrei; Feiner et al. 2022). It suggests the potential regulation of the blue-colored skin in adult females and immature individuals by controlling iridophore migration in GN. This proposition aligns with the observed sex-differential distribution of iridophores in the GN lateral skin, as evidenced by TEM micrographs at a 20 μm resolution (supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online).
It is important to consider that while the coloration of the flanks may be a prominent differing trait among populations, it might not be the sole distinguishing characteristic. Other traits, which could be co-evolving with coloration (such as visual processing), and possibly linked to adaptations to desert environments (such as stress responses), could also contribute to the genetic signatures observed in the genome scans. In addition to the KIRREL3 gene described above, several non-coloration-related genes exhibited significant genetic differentiation. For instance, ATP2B2, located near the PBE peak on chromosome 3, likely plays a role in vision evolution by controlling the transmission of visual signals (Duncan et al. 2006). SLC1A2 is related to visual cortex development (Sipe et al. 2021), and SLC6A11 on chromosome 3 is responsible to Gamma-aminobutyric acid neurotransmitter transport and brain development (Salcedo et al. 2021), suggesting a role in visual dependent communication and visual signal processing. DDX60 is involved in the innate immune response and exhibits antiviral activity (Miyashita et al. 2011), while RB1CC1 is involved in autophagy within the retinal pigment epithelium of the eye and repair of DNA damage caused by ionizing radiation (Yao et al. 2015). NPBWR1 (Motoike et al. 2016) and OPRK1 (Xu et al. 2013) have roles in stress responses. Five copies of TAS2R41-like gene clustered on the chromosome 3, and likely respond to avoiding bitter, toxic, and harmful compounds from the diet (Thalmann et al. 2013).
The five local PBE peaks were further validated using PBS, pairwise comparisons of FST, and composite likelihood ratio (XP-CLR) test, with the exception of the GD-GM pair (supplementary fig. S9, Supplementary Material online). Notably, the top 1% XP-CLR values revealed robust selective sweeps, particularly for PBE candidate loci such as BCO2, PTS, IVL, MAP7, ATP2B2, SLC6A11, TAS2R41, DDX60, KIRREL3, and SLC1A2. Incorporating color measurement data (hue, chroma, and achromatic) alongside population sequence data, we also conducted phenotype-SNPs association analyses (GWAS). However, no significant GWAS signals were detected around the BCO2-PTS loci. This could be attributed to the likelihood of non-genetic factors playing a role in determining color presentation. In addition to genetic factors, color quantification may also be influenced by development stage (age) and ingestion condition (as carotenoids must be obtained through the diet). These factors could introduce potential bias in phenotype measurement, resulting in an association failure.
We employed a multifaceted approach to identify and characterize genomic regions associated with differences in coloration and additional traits, shedding light on potential correlations with pigmentation, behavior, and visual processing in the toad-headed agamid lizards.

## Characteristics of Candidate Loci for Sexually Dimorphic Color Variation
To elucidate the underlying genetic dynamics, we further conducted a detailed examination of the differentiated region within the BCO2-PTS and MAP7 loci. Motivated by the need to understand the observed patterns, we focused on population pairs containing GN (GN/GM and GN/GD). Intriguingly, both pairs exhibited a highly divergent region within the BCO2-PTS locus, with FST values ranging from 0.6 to 0.8 (Fig. 3b). In contrast, the GD and GM pair showed a continuous low FST values (<0.2) in the same region. Noteworthy is the observation that the heightened divergence between GN and other groups predominantly occurred within genic regions, sharply decreasing in intergenic regions. This implies a reduced rate of recombination and a robust selective signal specifically in the genic regions of BCO2 and PTS in GN populations. The nucleotide diversity (π) at the BCO2-PTS locus was significantly lower than the average of chromosome 14 and the entire genome (Wilcoxon test P ≤ 7.5 × 10−6), aligning with the anticipated impact of a strong selective event. Particularly striking was the clear reduction in nucleotide diversity in GN from exon 8 of BCO2 to the end of BCO2-PTS locus, compared to the GD and GM (Fig. 3b). Despite GN displaying the lowest level of genome-wide linkage disequilibrium (LD), we found that both the BCO2-PTS and MAP7 loci in GN had higher LD than in GD and GM. This finding further bolsters the hypothesis that these target candidates underwent positive selection (Fig. 3c).
Within the BCO2-PTS genic region, the major reference allele frequencies in the GN populations approached 1, while the major allele frequency in GM and GD decreased to 0 (Fig. 3d). A ML tree constructed using SNPs from BCO2-PTS region revealed a distinct topology, diverging from the inferred species tree. Surprisingly, GM and GD exhibited a closer genetic relationship in this tree, contrary to the genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis where GM was found to be closely related to GN (Fig. 3d). Moreover, the majority of GN individuals were observed to be homozygous for the red allele (OO), while the oo genotype was predominated in the dull pools, including GM, GD, and P. vlangalii (supplementary fig. S10, Supplementary Material online). This raises an intriguing possibility that the mutant red allele may potentially confer a selective advantage to GN individuals inhabiting the Mugetan sandy land, potentially contributing to its fixation in the population under intense selective pressure. The credibility of this hypothesis finds support through the utilization of modified D-statistics, specifically fdM (Malinsky et al. 2015), serving as a direct test for gene flow. Our analysis revealed an exceptionally low rate of gene flow in both the BCO2-PTS and MAP7 region between GN and each of GM and GD. This held true when considering regions up/downstream of the BCO2-PTS and MAP7, the entire chromosome, or the whole genome (Wilcoxon test P ≤ 1.8 × 10−5; Fig. 3e and supplementary fig. S11, Supplementary Material online). While the genomic sequences of GM were primarily derived from GN, as previously mentioned, it appears that the genotypes responsible for GN coloration have shown limited transmission to GM and infrequent exchange with GD. This observation tentatively suggests that these genotypes may potentially serve as local adaptive loci for GN populations. Furthermore, it is worth considering the possibility that the fitness of GN coloration could be less pronounced outside of the Mugetan sandy land.
In the BCO2-PTS genic region, the majority of differences (>90%; 766 of 820 SNPs) were identified within intronic regions, whereas the coding region exhibited only 14 synonymous and 5 nonsynonymous (missense) substitutions (Fig. 3f). Notably, an observed G-to-A transition in exon 8 of BCO2 (chr14:26,069,289; p26069289 hereafter) resulted in a substitution at residue 420, changing it from a negative charged aspartate to a polar, neutral asparagine (D420N; Fig. 3g). This substitution is unique to the GN lizard, with the D residue at this site being highly conserved across other squamates (Fig. 3g). The genomes of other squamates predominantly feature the G allele at the first codon position, and no other A mutations have been characterized except in GN, implying that G was the ancestral allele, and A is a derived mutation. Upon examining of the p26069289 variation in 100 individuals, it was observed that the mutant homozygote (AA) was predominantly (∼96%) found in the GN populations, while the wild-type homozygous genotype (GG) was largely (94%) confined to the GD and GM populations (Fig. 3h).
To probe the functional implications of the D420N substitution in the GN BCO2 protein, we conducted a 3D receptor–ligand docking analysis of the BCO2-carotenoid system. Polar amino acids, such as asparagine (N), are commonly found on the surface of proteins and play crucial roles in substrate recognition and catalysis in enzymes (Alberts 2017). Our constructed 3D docking system revealed that residue 420, along with the four surrounding residues, forms an alpha-helix secondary structure buried within the binding domain at the receptor–ligand interface. Furthermore, the β-carotene ligand interacted with residue 420 of BCO2 via a hydrogen bond (H bonds with a distance of <4 Å; Fig. 4). Both GN BCO2 and GD BCO2 exhibited similar binding affinities to the β-carotene ligand, with binding energy of approximately −12 kcal/mol. From a surface perspective, the D420N mutation contributed to the electrostatic potential on the surrounding surface patch to change from negative (GD) to positive (GN), likely impacting enzyme–substrate interactions. A previous kinetic study on creatine kinase mutagenesis has demonstrated that asparagine (N) at the active site exhibits substantially higher catalytic efficiency compared to aspartate (D) at the same position (Borders et al. 2003). This finding provides strong evidence that polar N plays a crucial role in enzymatic transition state stabilization in catalyzed reactions. However, it remains uncertain whether this holds true for the D420N mutation in BCO2. Nevertheless, GN lateral skin has been shown to have lower levels of carotenoids (Fig. 1f). Therefore, we postulate that the D420N mutation may enhance or maintain catalytic activity. However, to fully elucidate the role of the D420N substitution in BCO2, further investigations are warranted to determine whether it directly enhances cleavage activity, whether its effect is solely mediated by the increased expression of BCO2 or whether the interplay of these factors contributes to the observed decrease in carotenoid levels.

## Differential Expression Patterns and Sex-Related Color Variation
To elucidate the potential regulatory role of genes, especially those identified as color candidates above, in shaping the diverse skin colors observed in different populations, we conducted a comparative analysis of skin transcriptomes among GN, GM, and GD, considering sex as well. Our result unveiled differential expression of BCO2 among the distinct color groups, exhibiting significant up-regulated expression in GN (AA) compared to GM (AG), with the lowest expression observed in GD (GG; Wilcoxon test P = 0.026; Fig. 3i). Notably, the red mutant allele (A) generally exhibited higher expression relative to the ancestral allele (G). Given that BCO2 mediates the oxidative cleavage of carotenoids, the up-regulated expression pattern in GN may contribute to the reduction of carotenoids, aligning with our biochemical experiments that detected generally low levels of yellow carotenoid pigments in GN. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating upregulation of β-carotene dioxygenase expression in the red skin of lizards (McLean et al. 2017; McLean et al. 2019) and linking downregulation of BCO2 activity to increased deposition of yellow carotenoids, resulting in yellow skin (Andrade et al. 2019). Moreover, the PTS gene, located proximally to BCO2, stands out as another gene of interest. PTS genes exhibited elevated expression levels in GN relative to the two other groups, especially in red males (supplementary fig. S12, Supplementary Material online), aligning with findings in frill-neck lizards (McLean et al. 2019). This molecular signature suggests a potential association between PTS gene expression and the vivid red coloration observed in GN males. This inference is supported by the enrichment of pteridines in red skin compared to other colors, in accordance with the recognized role of PTS in pteridine synthesis (Woo et al. 2002). Specifically, PTS catalyzes the conversion of 7,8-dihydroneopterin triphosphate to 6-pyruvoyltetrahydropterin, a precursor to red pigments, potentially indicating its role as a rate-limiting step in the pteridine synthesis pathway. Consistent with this, a previous study on frill-neck lizards suggests that red skin may produce higher levels of 6-pyruvoyltetrahydropterin. Collectively, our results imply the involvement of PTS, a key enzyme in pterin metabolism, in red color variation through gene regulation changes. Additionally, MAP7, identified as a potential candidate for blue-colored skin in GN subadult males and females, harbored a synonymous variant (G525C; chr2:25, 318, 233) in exon 4. This gene showed higher expression in blue skin of females in GN relative to other color groups (Wilcoxon test P = 0.015; Fig. 3i). Notably, this variant is highly prevalent in GN populations, with 92% individuals being homozygotes (CC), whereas it is almost absent from GM and GD populations.
In the context of sexual dimorphism, the GN red coloration is exclusive to sexually mature males, whereas subadult males and females exhibit blue in lateral skin, we hypothesized that such sex-related color variation is likely associated with steroid hormones (e.g. testosterone), as documented in several species (Cox et al. 2005, 2008; Jessop et al. 2009; Rankin and Stuart-Fox 2015). Our examination of the adult skin transcriptome between sexes provides insight into this hypothesis. Notably, we observed distinct expression patterns of steroid hormone-associated genes in the skin across different coloration. Specifically, we found that the expression of the SRD5A2 gene appeared to be higher in GN red males compared to blue females, while the expression of the SULT2B1 gene seemed to be elevated in GN blue females relative to red males. Intriguingly, we observed a reversed sex-contrast expression pattern in the dull GD and GM populations (Fig. 3j). Despite these discernible differences, statistical significance was not achieved, likely attributable to the limited sample size per sex (three samples). This constraint heightens the risk of failing to reject a false null hypothesis, with Type II error rates (β) consistently exceeding 0.6, as indicated by power analysis. SRD5A2 plays a pivotal role in sexual differentiation and androgen physiology, particularly in catalyzing conversion of testosterone into dihydrostestosterone and progesterone into oxosteroids (Thigpen et al. 1992; Makridakis et al. 2000); SULT2B1 exhibits substantial activity with pregnenolone and contributes to epidermal cholesterol metabolism, as well as the regulation of epidermal proliferation and differentiation (Fuda et al. 2002; Heinz et al. 2017). Importantly, enhanced expression of SRD5A2 and SULT2B1 was associated with the pigmentation of red/yellow and blue colors in lizards, respectively, as evidenced in the frill-neck lizard (Chlamydosaurus kingii) and the tawny dragon (Ctenophorus decresii; McLean et al. 2017; McLean et al. 2019). Volcano plots comparing females to males revealed a higher incidence of significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in GN compared to GM and GD lizards. Specifically, 39 sex-DEGs were identified in GN, whereas GM exhibited 10 and GD only one (supplementary fig. S13, Supplementary Material online). Notably, the gene QRICH2, crucial for the positive regulation of sperm flagella formation and flagellar structure maintenance, displayed increased expression in GN males but was down-regulated in GM males. Another gene, MOB3A, potentially involved in kinase activity regulation exhibited a reversed sex-differential expression pattern between GN and GM.
Our results, in conjunction with previous findings, underscore the intricate interplay among gene expression, hormonal regulation, and skin coloration in lizards.

## Driving Forces of Color Variation in GN
In light of limited gene flow observed in both the BCO2-PTS and MAP7 region between GN and neighboring populations, we sought to understand the driving forces behind color variation in GN individuals. We hypothesized that the observed coloration in GN may confer specific advantages in their desert habitat compared to neighboring areas (GM and GD).
To explore the role of natural selection, we employed visual modeling to assess whether the coloration of GN males and females color could create a camouflage effect in sand dunes when compared to dark soils, thereby potentially increase the survival rate (Fig. 5a). Frequent sightings of potential predators including Falco tinnunculus, Milvus migrans, Lanius sphenocercus, and Buteo buteo were noted in GN and GM areas (supplementary fig. S14, Supplementary Material online). If male and female colors have high camouflage effect in sand dunes, our hypothesis posits that their colors may be less conspicuous in the sand dunes compared to dark soils, which are the typical habitat of GM lizards. We utilized the receptor noise limited (RNL) model implemented in the R package pavo (Maia et al. 2019) to evaluate the conspicuousness of body color against sand dunes and dark soils in a sample of 209 lizards (90 males and 119 females). This assessment was conducted in relation to the bird's visual system, with measurements based on just noticeable differences (JNDs) of chromatic and achromatic contrast. Our findings indicate a significant reduction in the conspicuousness of GN lizards within sand dunes in comparison to dark soils. Specifically, chromatic contrast exhibited a decrease (estimate = 0.84, t = 4.57, P < 0.001; Fig. 5b), as did achromatic contrast (estimate = 4.17, t = 15.33, P < 0.001; Fig. 5c). This suggests that both males and females GN lizards might blend well into sand dunes, potentially experiencing a lower predation rate than those residing in dark soils. This may hinder the movement of GN lizards out of Mugetan sandy land and may contribute to the promotion of the fixation of color-related genes.
Natural and sexual selection for the evolution of color variation in GN lizards. a) Schematic representation of GN lizard conspicuousness on sand dunes and dark soils. b) Chromatic contrast differences in GN lizards (red males and blue females) relative to a predator's visual system on sand dunes and dark soils. Females exhibit increased conspicuousness in dark soils. “*” indicates the significant difference. c) Achromatic contrast differences in GN lizards on sand dunes and dark soils. Both males and females show heightened conspicuousness in dark soils. d) Experiment design for male mate preference, where size-matched females are randomly assigned to glass chambers with different color scenario treatments. e) Female body color manipulation scenarios: MW (blue-blocking and brightness-reduction), ND (brightness-reduction without blue-blocking), and FULL (no change in color). f) Chromatic contrast-SVL relationship in females. g) Achromatic contrast-SVL relationship in females. h) Achromatic contrast-male–male competition relationship. i) Achromatic contrast-SVL relationship in males.
Given the evident sexual color dimorphism, we further investigated the influence of coloration on male mate assessment and male–male competition to elucidate the role of sexual selection. Our hypothesis posited that conspicuousness of female blue color likely serves as an indicator of individual quality, with males potentially showing a preference for brighter blue females. To test this hypothesis, we conducted three sets of dichotomous male mate choice trials: (1) blue-blocking and brightness-reduction versus control (examining the importance of blue color and brightness in mate choice), (2) brightness-reduction versus control (investigating whether brightness, rather than color, is crucial in male choice), and (3) blue-blocking and brightness-reduction versus brightness-reduction (evaluating the significance of blue color, not brightness, in male choice). All trials were conducted within a choice arena comprising two female chambers, positioned in front of a resident male in the field. These chambers were constructed from glass with nearly 100% light transmission (supplementary fig. S15, Supplementary Material online; Fig. 5d).
Initially, we investigated the influence of blue color and brightness on male mate choice. Males were given the choice between a MW (blue-blocked and brightness-reduced) female and a FULL (unfiltered) female. Males significantly preferred FULL females over MW (P < 0.001; Fig. 5e), suggesting that both blue color and brightness may be important factors in mate choice decisions.
To assess whether male preference for blue females was solely based on brightness, we introduced a neutral density (ND) filter, altering achromatic brightness independently of hue, around the glass of a female chamber (i.e. ND female), paired with a female chamber without a filter (i.e. FULL female). Males demonstrated a preference for FULL females over ND females (P < 0.001; Fig. 5e), indicating that males specifically favor brighter blue females, underscoring the role of blue brightness in male mate choice decisions.
Further analysis aimed to determine the relative importance of blue color in male mate choice decisions. In this case, males were given the choice between an MW female and an ND female. Males exhibited a significant preference for ND females over MW females (P < 0.001; Fig. 5e), highlighting the decisive role of blue color compared to brightness in male mate choice.
We rule out the possibility that males disliked the filters, as they consistently remained in proximity to the burrow during the acclimation phase before the introduction of females. Additionally, the transparency of the filters (supplementary fig. S15, Supplementary Material online) and observed natural social communication between lizards in and out of the glass chamber indicate that the chamber glass did not obstruct the view of alternative females.
We observed a significant and positive relationship between female chromatic contrast and individual body size (n = 119), encompassing snout-vent length (SVL; estimate = 0.42, t = 5.45, P < 0.001; Fig. 5f), relative head length (estimate = 0.39, t = 3.65, P < 0.001; supplementary fig. S16a, Supplementary Material online), relative head width (estimate = 0.42, t = 3.29, P = 0.001; supplementary fig. S16b, Supplementary Material online), and relative tail length (estimate = 0.42, t = 2.13, P = 0.04; supplementary fig. S16c, Supplementary Material online). Additionally, female achromatic contrast exhibited a significant and positive association with individual SVL (estimate = 0.32, t = 3.68, P < 0.001; Fig. 5g), relative head width (estimate = 0.34, t = 2.33, P = 0.02; supplementary fig. S16d, Supplementary Material online), and relative tail length (estimate = 0.49, t = 3.14, P = 0.004; supplementary fig. S16e, Supplementary Material online). These findings imply that there is a possibility for female blue coloration and brightness to function as potential indicators of individual quality. Considering the observed male preference for females with blue coloration, we propose that it might play a role in the establishment of the fixation of blue coloration, as reported in cichlid fishes (Wagner et al. 2012). However, caution is warranted in interpreting the results, as the observed significance may be influenced by a limited number of data points. After excluding biased points, the discernible trend showed a slight reduction but retained its overall stability.
For males, the conspicuousness of red coloration may serve as an indicator of individual quality, with more intensely red males potentially demonstrating higher competence in social interactions (Hamilton et al. 2013). To assess this hypothesis, we conducted 20 male–male competitions, during which the competence of nine males was scored in the field, and the colors of participating males were measured. Additionally, we examined the relationship between male coloration and individual quality in the GN populations. Morphological traits indicative of quality (e.g. SVL, head size, and tail length) and the coloration of individual males (n = 90) were measured in the field. Our findings revealed that males with higher achromatic contrast were more likely to emerge victorious in competitions (t = 3.23, P = 0.004; Fig. 5h). Furthermore, male achromatic contrast exhibited a significant and positive correlation with individual SVL (estimate = 0.21, t = 1.99, P = 0.05; Fig. 5i) and head length (estimate = 0.66, t = 5.30, P < 0.001; supplementary fig. S16f, Supplementary Material online). The findings imply that males exhibiting more vibrant coloration could potentially signify higher quality and competence in male–male competitions. However, further field observations and targeted research on size matching are necessary to disentangle the impact of body size on competitive outcomes.
Our experiments were designed to understand the ecological and evolutionary drivers of color variation in GN populations. The outcomes lend support the hypothesis that GN individuals’ coloration is likely influenced by a combination of natural and sexual selection, with potential adaptive advantages in desert habitats and meaningful implications for mate choice and male–male competition dynamics.

## Discussion
Our study revealed that male color variation in the toad-headed agamid lizard results from a combination of carotenoid and pteridine pigments with varying relative concentrations in xanthophores. This variation is primarily governed by major effects at the BCO2 and PTS loci on chromosome 14. In contrast to previous studies reporting one or multiple independent genes for pigmentation determination (Toews et al. 2016; Jones et al. 2018; Andrade et al. 2019; Jin et al. 2020; Enbody et al. 2021), our research highlights the intriguing role of four tightly linked pigmentation genes (BCO2 and downstream PTS) responsible for red coloration in GN males. Notably, with 90% of variants falling in the non-coding region of the BCO2-PTS locus, it raised the possibility that some of these variants may function as cis-acting regulators, controlling the activity of BCO2 and PTS and resulting in skin pigment differences, as detected by our biochemical analyses, providing a promising avenue for further assessment (Steiner et al. 2007). One divergent gene, MAP7, responsible for microtubule dynamics exhibits a sex-differential expression pattern in GN lizards. This suggests its potential role in contributing to blue coloration observed in subadults and adult females. Correspondingly, a skin ultrastructural study indicates that the blue color is primarily a structural color (Bagnara et al. 2007), influenced by the interplay between the intensity of melanin and light-scattering GCs (Morrison et al. 1995; Macedonia et al. 2000). Nevertheless, as of now, no studies have conclusively established a direct link between MAP7 and structural color formation. Notably, a recent investigation identified CCDC170, a gene with a comparable function to MAP7, as the determinant for the female-limited color pattern in the brown anole lizard (Feiner et al. 2022). The involvement of MAP7 in this process may be more intricate or indirect than our current understanding. For instance, MAP7 could potentially contribute to the assembly of the iridophore cytoskeleton, or microtubules may play a role in regulating iridophore distribution. This hypothesis stems from the observed elongated arrangement pattern evident in TEM micrographs of female iridophores, characterized by densely packed light-reflecting GCs (supplementary fig. S2, Supplementary Material online; Fig. 1e). Further research is necessary to explore the intricate relationship between MAP7 and GN female phenotypes in greater detail. Additionally, we demonstrated that the color difference between males and females in GN is likely associated with a steroid hormone. Candidate color SNPs were not sex-related but differently expressed, consistent with the hormonal control hypothesis (Rand 1992; Cox et al. 2005; Rankin and Stuart-Fox 2015). Furthermore, we identified two steroid hormone-associated genes, SULT2B1 and SRD5A2, displaying sex-specific expression pattern may serve as upstream switches influencing blue and red colors, respectively, as reported in other lizards (McLean et al. 2017; McLean et al. 2019).
Repeated evolution likely plays a pivotal role in driving color variation across diverse species. Numerous pigmentation genes have been identified (Hubbard et al. 2010; McLean et al. 2017; Funk et al. 2021), with several emerging as recurrent targets of evolutionary change in various taxonomic groups. For instance, the BCO2 gene, implicated in chromatophore-based coloration and discussed in this study, has been associated with color variation in wall lizards (Andrade et al. 2019) and several non-squamate species (Eriksson et al. 2008; Våge and Boman 2010; Toews et al. 2016; Lehnert et al. 2019; Ahi et al. 2020; Enbody et al. 2021), showcasing its recurrent role. Additionally, the melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1R) gene has been implicated in repeated evolution of melanin-based color in mice (Nachman et al. 2003; Hoekstra et al. 2006), sand lizards (Rosenblum et al. 2010; Jin et al. 2020), and multiple squamate species (Rosenblum et al. 2004). Similarly, the agouti-signaling protein (ASIP) gene has been associated with melanin-based color in warblers (Toews et al. 2016; Baiz et al. 2021), finch (Campagna et al. 2017), snowshoe hares (Jones et al. 2018), and deer mice (Linnen et al. 2013). These instances highlight the recurrent involvement of specific pigmentation genes in driving color diversification across various lineages, suggesting their essential role in pigments generation subjected to certain genetic or developmental constraints that interconnect phenotypic and genetic convergence.
Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that even when targeting on the same gene, natural selection has acted on different regions in different lineages. Examples include the upstream area of BCO2 in wall lizards (Andrade et al. 2019), the genic region of BCO2 with a missense variation in GN lizard in our study, and a synonymous change of BCO2 in Darwin's ﬁnch (Enbody et al. 2021). These findings suggest multiple independent evolutionary paths and genetic solutions that likely lead to similar outcomes. It raises the intriguing question of the source of variants of the repeatedly selected pigment genes—are they a result of introgression, ancestral polymorphism, and/or de novo mutation? Given that GN is likely the sole toad-headed agamid lizard in that area with sexually dimorphic coloration, this trait is unlikely to have resulted from introgression through hybridization, the usual mechanism for acquiring color traits observed in other species (e.g. wall lizards, Darwin's finches, warblers, and snowshoe hares). In our study, the red variation was detected in all three distinct color groups, including a few homozygotes found in dull GD populations (Fig. 3h), although the frequency of this variation was extremely asymmetric (dominant in bright GN but rare in dull GD and GM populations). This, combined with the simulated demographic of long period of population isolation without migration (Fig. 2f), suggested that the red allele was ancestrally polymorphic, although de novo mutation cannot be completely ruled out. Evolution of sexually dimorphic coloration appears to have been governed by robust selection, playing a critical role in restricting gene flow (Maan and Sefc 2013; Brawand et al. 2014). Our simulations suggest that the origin of the genetically mixed population (GM) likely occurred through hybridization between GN and GD around 0.3 mya. Intriguingly, while the majority of the genomic sequences in GM were derived from GN, the underlying genotypes responsible for GN coloration were rarely transferred to GM or exchanged with GD. This implies that the red and blue alleles likely confer distinct advantages to GN inhabiting in sand dunes, favoring them through natural and sexual selection. Consequently, color differentiation may act as pre-zygotic isolation barrier, accelerating divergence. Our field work provided supportive evidence for this hypothesis. First, regardless of red males or blue females, both faced an elevated risk of predation when venturing outside the sand dune habitat, potentially acting as a barrier to GN dispersal to other areas. Second, the preference of males for females with blue coloration, suggesting a potential indication of individual quality, likely contributes to the fixation of blue coloration. Lastly, males displaying a more pronounced red coloration demonstrated greater competitive success compared to individuals with less red coloration, potentially contributing to the prevalence of red coloration within the GN populations.

## Conclusion
By integrating color phenotypic, skin ultrastructural, pigment biochemical, genomic and transcriptomic data, along with field behavioral experiments, we conducted a comprehensive investigation into the genetic architecture and evolutionary forces underlying sexually dimorphic color variation in the toad-headed agamid lizards. Notably, we identified four tightly linked pigmentation genes (BCO2 and downstream PTS) on chromosome 14 that exert a major effect on male red coloration. Additionally, a structure related gene, MAP7 on chromosome 2, emerged as a potential candidate associated with female blue coloration. Our findings suggest that steroid hormone-associated genes (SULT2B1 and SRD5A2) may function as upstream regulators influencing sexually dimorphic coloration. Moreover, we have put forth evidence that implies a possible biological significance linked to these colors. Our findings suggest the presence of ongoing selection in GN, potentially contributing to the fixation of red and blue alleles, confining the GN phenotype to the Mugetan sandy land. While these observations are intriguing, we propose that future research could delve into elucidating the functional relevance and cooption mechanism of these loci. Additionally, an exploration into the potential role of steroid hormones in the development of sexually dimorphic coloration could offer valuable insights into the regulatory processes governing sex-differential coloration.

## Materials and Methods

## Field Sampling and Color Measurement
To investigate color variation in P. putjatai, we measured the body color of 268 adult individuals from six populations in Guide (GD) and Guinan (GN) County in northwestern China between June and July 2020. This comprised 106 individuals from GN (61 females and 45 males) at GN1 and GN2 sites, 70 individuals from GD (40 females and 30 males) at GD1 and GD2 sites, and 92 individuals from GM (52 females and 40 males) in potential contact zone (GM1 and GM2 sites; Fig. 1a). Body color measurements were taken at eight separate locations on the lateral side using a Jaz optic spectrophotometer (Ocean Optics Inc., Dunedin, FL, USA) with a PX2 light source.
All measurements were standardized relative to a white 99% WS-1 (Labsphere) standard, utilizing a fiber-optic reflectance probe positioned at a fixed distance of 5 mm and at a 90-degree angle to the skin surface. In addition, we recorded SVL, head length, head width, and tail length for each lizard using a digital caliper (Mitutoyo) to the nearest 0.01 mm. Spectral reflectance (nGN = 30, nGD = 50, and nGM = 60) and absolute irradiance from each site (nGN1 = 16, nGN2 = 17, nGD1 = 14, nGD2 = 19, nGM1 = 19, and nGM2 = 16) were measured using a separate irradiance channel held at ground level and parallel to the ground to capture side-welling light. All measurements were conducted during the peak period of the lizard's activity. To ensure color quantification consistency and minimize potential bias due to development and sex, we specifically sampled adult-like individuals and attempted to balance the number of males and females. Lizards were released at their original sampling locations post experimentation.
Following color measurement, muscle tissue from one toe was sampled from 100 individuals for subsequent genome re-sequencing, including 37 GN (18 females and 19 males), 32 GD (16 females and 16 males), and 31 GM (15 females and 16 males). Additionally, a small piece of skin tissue was collected and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen from 18 individuals of GN, GD, and GM, then stored at −80 °C for further transcriptome sequencing. To construct genomes, fresh muscle tissue was sampled from one female individual of GN, GD, and P. vlangalii, respectively.
To assess color variation with respect to population and sex, we performed cluster analysis based on spectral characters, specifically hue and chroma. Receptor quantum catches were calculated across a wavelength of 300–700 nm. Individual spectral reflectance curves underwent smoothing to eliminate electrical noise, and negative values were corrected. Color measurements were averaged from eight points on the lateral side for each individual. Three color variables—hue, chroma, and achromatic—were extracted for the subsequent analysis of color variation. The “k-means” function in the stats Package of R (R Core Team 2013) was employed to investigate whether color patches from lateral side formed distinctive color groups based on population and sex. Although this analysis does not consider the animal's visual system, it serves as an informative cluster analysis. Initially, we determined the optimal number of clusters (k) using the sum of squares error (SSE) and the elbow method (Syakur et al. 2018). The smallest SSE value and the elbow of the curve identified the optimal k value. Subsequently, each lizard's color was assigned to a group based on the determined k value, with individuals from GD1 and GD2 categorized as the GD color group, GM1 and GM2 as GM color group, and GN1 and GN2 as the GN color group.
To assess the individual contributions of variables in the cluster analysis, we conducted a PCA using the “prcomp” function from the stats R package. The outcomes indicated that hue and chroma collectively accounted for 89.13% of the total variance in females and 93.86% in males (Bradbury and Vehrencamp 1998). Consequently, we opted to utilize hue and chroma as key variables in the subsequent cluster analysis.
Further examination color variation between color groups and sexes utilized low mass (LMs) with the “lm” function of the stats R package (R Core Team 2013). Hue and chroma were Z score transformed and treated as dependent variables (Schielzeth 2010), with color groups, sex, and interactions as independent variables. Lizards’ SVL was included as a covariate to account for body size effects on coloration variation. Residuals were tested for normality assumption using the “qqnorm” function in the stats R package (R Core Team 2013).

## TEM Micrographs of the Lateral Skins
We conducted ultrastructure characterization and analyzed the distribution of chromatophores using TEM following established procedures (Haisten et al. 2015). Lateral skin patches were collected from GN (three males and three females) and GD (three males and three females) individuals. The selected patches for TEM analysis were red/blue in color for GN lizards and brown for GD lizards. Fresh skin patches (ca. 1 mm2) were swiftly excised and placed in the Eppendorf tube containing TEM fixative for further fixation, followed by storage at 4 °C. Subsequently, the tissues were avoided light post-fixed with 1% OsO4 (osmium tetroxide) in 0.1 M PB buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 h at room temperature. After OsO4 removal, the tissues underwent three rinses in 0.1 M PB buffer (pH 7.4; 15 min each), followed by dehydration through an ascending ethanol series (30–100%). The samples were embedded in pure EMBed 812 resins (SPI CHEM) and incubated in a 37 °C oven overnight. Polymerized resin blocks were then sectioned into 60–80 nm thin slices using an ultra-microtome. These thin sections were transferred onto 150-mesh cuprum grids with formvar film, stained with lead citrate to avoid CO2 for 8 min, and subsequently rinsed three times with ultra-pure water. Finally, ultra-thin skin sections were imaged using the HITACHI HT7700 equipped with a scanning transmission electron microscope system.

## LC–MS Measures of Pigments Level
We employed LC–MS to quantify the carotenoid and pterin contents in the skins of GD and GN populations. Lateral skins from 10 GN and GD individuals underwent a series of pre-treatments before LC–MS measurement. Skin samples were promptly dissected, weighed, and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at −80 °C. Each tube contained 800 µL methanol:ethylacetate (6:4 v/v) liquid with 0.01% butylated hydrotoluene (BHT) and three 4 mm stainless steel beads (Servicebio). Skins were homogenized (420 s at 50 Hz, 420 s at 30, and 600 s at 50 Hz) using a tissue grinder (Servicebio). The homogenized tissues were incubated for 30 min at 4 °C on a thermomixer at 1,000 rpm and then centrifuged for 5 min at 4 °C at 15,000 rpm. Supernatants were collected, and the sediments were re-extracted for an additional 30 min with 400 µL of methanol:ethylacetate (6:4 v/v; containing BHT) liquid on the thermomixer. After centrifuging for 5 min at 4 °C at 16,000 rpm, supernatants were collected and stored at −80 °C until analysis (within 4 days). Prior to LC–MS analysis, the combined carotenoid fractions were dried in a termovap sample concentrator (MD200 to 1, ALLSHENG), re-suspended in 300 µL acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid):acetone (6.7:3.3 v/v), with an additional 100 µL methanol, and centrifuged for 5 min at 15,000 rpm. The resulting 200 µL supernatants were transferred into a sample vial for LC–MS analysis.
The sediments were used for following extracting of pteridines. Tissue pellets were treated with 800 µL 2% ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH), incubated for 30 min at 4 °C on a thermomixer at 1,000 rpm, and then centrifuged for 5 min at 4 °C at 15,000 rpm. The resulting supernatants were collected. Subsequently, an additional 400 µL of 2% ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH) was added to the remaining tissue pellets. After 30 min at 4 °C on a thermomixer at 1,000 rpm, the samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 4 °C at 15,000 rpm, and the supernatant were collected. The combined pteridine fractions were stored at −80 °C until analysis (within 4 days). Prior to LC–MS analysis, the samples were allowed to melt at room temperature and then centrifuged for 5 min at 15,000 rpm. Subsequently, 200 µL of the supernatants were used for LC–MS analysis.
LC–MS analysis was conducted using the external standard method with a Xevo TQ-MS system (WATERS). Carotenoid standards, including lutein (98%, ACMEC), β-carotene (98%, D&B), β-cryptoxanthin (98%, NATUREWILL), astaxanthin (96%, MERYER), as well as pteridine standards of xanthopterin (98%, ACMEC), pterin (95%, YUANYE), 6-Biopterin (97%, ACMEC), and isoxanthopterin (95%, YUANYE), were initially obtained. Subsequently, a standard curve was established using these reference standards. The carotenoid and pteridine reference standards were separately mixed at consistent concentrations and assigned into distinct concentration gradients: carotenoids (12,500, 1,250, 125, 12.5, 1.25, 0.125, and 0.0125 ng/ml) and pteridines (14,500, 1,450, 145, 14.5, 1.45, 0.145, and 0.0145 ng/ml). The autosampler was maintained at 4 °C, and the MS operated using the following conditions: capillary voltage 3.25 kV, cone voltage 30 V, desolvation temperature 500 °C, desolvation gas flow 550 L/h, cone gas flow 5 L/h, LM resolution 1 was 3.00, high mass (HM) resolution 1 was 15.00, ion energy 1 was 0.3, LM resolution 2 was 3.00, HM resolution 2 was 15.00, and ion energy 2 was 1.0.
Chromatographic separation of carotenoids was carried out using an Agilent Zorbax Bonus RP column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.8 µm) maintained at 30 °C, with a flow rate of 600 µL/min and a total run time of 12 min. A 20 µL injection volume was utilized. The mobile phase consisted of mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in methanol). The LC gradient was: 5 min linear gradient from 78% to 85% mobile phase B, followed by 1.5 min linear increase to 100% B, then a 3.5 min isocratic hold at 100% B, and 2 min re-equilibration to 78% B. For the chromatographic separation of pteridines, a Phenomenex Luna Phenyl-Hexyl column (2 × 100 mm, 3 µm) was employed, maintained at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 300 µL/min and a 10-min run time. The mobile phase composition comprised mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in methanol). The LC gradient was 3 min isocratic elution at 1% mobile phase B, followed by 2 min linear gradient to 60% B, then 0.5 min linear increase to 90% B, and a 2.5 min isocratic hold at 90% B and 2 min re-equilibration to 1% B.

## Reference Genome Long-Read Sequencing
To obtain a chromosome-level reference genome for the toad-headed agamid lizard, we selected one female GN for de novo genomic sequencing using PacBio long reads sequencing, aiming for a target coverage of approximately 105-fold based on an estimated genome size of 1.83 Gb. For comparative genomic purpose, we also sequenced one female GD at approximately 100× coverage using PacBio technology (Pacific Biosciences). Additionally, long-read sequencing at a coverage of about 80-fold was conducted for one adult female from the sister species P. vlangalii, sampled from Ulan County of Qinghai Province, for evolutionary comparison. PacBio Single Molecular Real Time (SMRTbell) libraries were constructed following the PacBio 40 kb template preparation protocol and sequenced on a PacBio Sequel II platform (Pacific Biosciences). To enhance the accuracy of the genome sequence, short-read sequencing was performed for the same individual on an Illumina NovaSeq platform, ensuring an average coverage of at least 46×. For scaffolding assembled contigs, Hi-C data were generated for the same individual. The process involved fixing chromatin in the nucleus with formaldehyde, followed by DNA digestion with MboI. Sticky ends were filled in with biotinylated nucleotides, and free blunt ends were ligated. Purified DNA was randomly sheared to a size of 300–500 bp. Biotin labeled fragments were captured by streptavidin coated magnetic beads and processed into library preparation for paired-end sequencing. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform, producing 104 Gb of clean data (57× coverage) after filtering out adapters and low-quality reads as described previously.

## Reference Genome Construction and Quality Assessment
The PacBio long reads were initially de novo assembled using the falcon v0.2.3 assembler (Chin et al. 2016), employing an overlapping layout consensus assembly algorithm. The following parameters were utilized: pa_HPCdaligner_option = -v -B128 -e.75 -k18 -h480 -l1800 -w8 -s1000 -M32, ovlp_HPCdaligner_option = -v -B128 -e.96 -k24 -h750 -l1500 -w8 -s1000 -M32.
To enhance assembly accuracy, three correction rounds were executed. Initial polishing was performed using the long reads with Arrow algorithm in PacBio smartlink 5.1.0 with the default parameters. Last two rounds of polishing were performed with Pilon v1.18 (Walker et al. 2014) after mapping Illumina short reads to PacBio contigs via BWA-MEM v0.7.17 (Li 2013) with default parameters.
Chromosome-scale scaffolding was performed using Hi-C-based proximity-guided assembly platform based on the LACHESIS method (Burton et al. 2013; Bickhart et al. 2017). Short reads generated from Hi-C library were mapped to the polished genome using BWA-MEM v0.7.17 (Li 2013). Paired reads with mate mapped to different contigs were employed for Hi-C associated scaffolding. The Hi-C linked signals were utilized to correct assembly errors, cluster scaffolds into chromosomal groups, and order and orient scaffolds on chromosomes (CLUSTER_MIN_RE_SITES = 1,800, CLUSTER_MAX_LINK_DENSITY = 4). Scaffolds were named as chromosomes from larger to smaller.
To assess the integrity of the final assembly, the PacBio CLR (continuous long reads) subreads were aligned against the final assembly using minimap2 with default parameters. A total of at least 91% of long reads can be mapped properly. The assembled genomes underwent BUSCO v4.0.6 (Simão et al. 2015) analysis against the vertebrata OrthoDB 10 to evaluate the completeness by examining the status of well-conserved vertebrate genes. Overall, 91.4–92.2% complete and 2.6–2.9% partial BUSCOs were identified in the three assembled genomes. To evaluate genome accuracy at the single base level, Illumina short reads were aligned to the reference genome from the same individual by BWA-MEM v0.7.17 (Li 2013). At least 98.08% of clean short reads could be successfully mapped.

## Annotation of the Reference Genome
We employed two distinct approaches to identify repeat contents within the reference genome, homology-based and de novo prediction strategies. The homology-based analysis utilized RepeatMasker v4.07 and RepeatProteinMask v4.07, employing the Repbase transposable element library (Bao et al. 2015; Smit et al. 2015). Ab initio prediction involved the use of LTR_Finder v1.0.6 (Xu and Wang 2007), RepeatScout v1.0.5 (Price et al. 2005), and RepeatModeler v1.0.3 (Smit and Hubley 2010) to construct a de novo transposable element library, subsequently utilized for repeat prediction with RepeatMasker v4.0.7. Tandem repeats were identified using Tandem Repeats Finder v4.0.9 (Benson 1999).
To annotate the structure of protein-coding genes, a comprehensive approach incorporating de novo, homology, and RNAseq-based methods were implemented. Augustus v3.2.3 (Stanke et al. 2006), GlimmerHMM v3.0.4 (Majoros et al. 2004), and Genscan v1.0 (Burge and Karlin 1997) generated ab initio predictions with internal gene models. Homology evidence was derived from protein sequences of Anolis carolinensis (GCF_000090745.1), Gallus gallus (GCF_000002315.6), and Homo sapiens (GCF_000001405.39), downloaded from National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) and aligned against the genome using TBLASTN (Camacho et al. 2009). Short RNA-Seq reads derived from muscle, heart, and liver tissues of the individual from which the reference genome was constructed were mapped to the final assembly using Hisat2 v2.2.0 (Kim et al. 2019) with default parameters, and gene structure was determined using Cufflinks v2.2.0 (Trapnell et al. 2012). The final set of protein-coding genes were predicted by integrating gene models from all three approaches through MAKER (Cantarel et al. 2008), refined by PASA v2.4.1 (Haas et al. 2008). GN lizard genes were aligned to those of Anolis carolinensis, and chromosome synteny relationships were established using MCScanX (Wang et al. 2012) with default parameters.
Functional annotation of predicted genes was conducted by searching known functional motifs, domains, and potential biological processes in databases including InterPro, Swiss-Prot, and NR, using BLASTP (Camacho et al. 2009) with an e-value cutoff of <1 × 10−5. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes annotations were performed using Blast2GO (Conesa et al. 2005), retaining only the single best hit for each gene.

## Population Genomic Sequencing
Genome-wide polymorphism data were obtained through a population-wide whole genome sequencing approach. Genomic DNA was extracted from skeletal muscles using DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. To ensure high quality, DNA degradation and contamination were evaluated on 1% agarose gels, while DNA purity was assessed using the NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (Implen, CA, USA). The DNA concentration was quantified using the Qubit DNA Assay Kit on the Qubit 2.0 Flurometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). For library preparation, 1.5 µg DNA from each sample was utilized, resulting in an insert size of approximately 350 bp. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the Illumina Truseq Nano DNA kit following the manufacturer's instructions, and unique index codes were assigned to attribute sequences to individual sample. Subsequently, all library pools underwent sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), generating 150 bp paired-end short reads with a minimum effective coverage of 11-fold per individual. Before subsequent analyses, the raw reads underwent preprocessing steps, including the removal of the adapter sequences, low-quality bases, and reads, accomplished using trimmomatic v0.38 (Bolger et al. 2014). Specific parameters applied were: LEADING = 15, TRAILING = 15, SLIDINGWINDOW = 4:20, and MINLEN = 30.

## RNA Sequencing
We conducted skin transcriptomes sequencing on 18 individuals representing distinct color groups to identify DEGs. Skin samples were obtained from three females and males each from the GD, GM, and GN color groups. RNA extraction was performed using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA), with RNA degradation monitored on 1.5% agarose gels. Assessment of RNA purity, integrity, and concentration was conducted using the Spectrophotometer, Agilent 2,100 Bioanalyzer, and Qubit, respectively. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the VAHTS mRNA-seq v2 Library Prep Kit for Illumina, following the manufacturer's protocols, and subsequently sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Approximately 6 Gb of RNA-seq data was generated for each sample. Raw reads underwent processing, involving the removal of the adapter sequences, low-quality bases, and reads, and were then mapped to the reference genome to estimate gene expression levels using the Hisat2 v2.2.0 (Kim et al. 2019) and StringTie v2.2.0 (Pertea et al. 2016). The reads aligned to expressed gene was standardized as fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads (FPKM) values.

## Comparative Genomic Analyses
To cluster genes into orthologous groups (OGs), we extracted proteins from the longest transcripts of each gene from toad-headed agamid lizards and 11 other representative squamata species (supplementary table S3, Supplementary Material online). OGs were identified using OrthoFinder v2.3 (Emms and Kelly 2015) with an all-versus-all alignment strategy and Markov Cluster Algorithm (Enright et al. 2002) using the default parameters.
To elucidate phylogenetic relationships within and among the three toad-headed agamid lizards and other squamata species, putative one-to-one orthologues (single copy) were extracted from all 15 species for tree reconstruction. Protein sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.4 (Katoh and Standley 2013) with the FFT-NS-2 progressive alignment strategy. Corresponding coding sequence codon alignments were generated guided by the protein alignment. Four-fold degenerate (4D) sites, where all mutations produce synonymous changes, were extracted from the concatenated dataset for phylogenomic reconstruction. A ML tree was inferred using RAxML v8.2.12 (Stamatakis 2014) with the GTR + G model and evaluated with 1,000 bootstrap replicates.
Divergence dates were estimated using a relaxed molecular clock Bayesian method implemented in the MCMCTREE program (Yang and Rannala 2006) in PAML v4.9 (Yang 2007). Multiple calibration points are proved to provide more credible divergence time estimates (Donoghue and Benton 2007). The specific priors on multiple calibration nodes were collected from the TimeTree database (Kumar et al. 2017) and can be found in supplementary table S4, Supplementary Material online. Substitution rates (rgene gamma) and rate-drift parameter (sigma2 gamma) were set as G (1, 6.9) and G (1, 6.9), respectively. Trees were generated using the birth-death process with species sampling (birth rate λ, death rate μ, and sampling fraction ρ were set to 1, 1, and 0.1, respectively). The absolute rate of molecular evolution for each species based on the 4D sites was calculated using r8s (Sanderson 2003) with the penalized likelihood method and truncated Newton algorithm to accommodate rate heterogeneity.
Using the phylogenetic tree, we estimated the rate ratio (ω) of nonsynonymous to synonymous nucleotide substitutions to assess selective constraints on candidate GN single-copy ortholog genes. After obtaining high-quality alignments of related sequences, we contrasted evolutionary models using the species trees in a likelihood framework. The adaptive branch-site random effects likelihood method (Smith et al. 2015), an improved version of the “branch-site” model implemented in HYPHY v2.5.1 (Pond and Muse 2005; Kosakovsky Pond et al. 2020), was employed to detect ω heterogeneity, indicating a signature of positive selection on a proportion of the specified interested branches.

## CR Gene Family Identification
CR genes play crucial roles in feeding, mate identification, and predator avoidance (Nei et al. 2008). However, CR repertoires vary extensively among species (Nei et al. 2008) due to frequent gains and losses, posing challenges in identifying the entire set, especially without high-quality genome assembly. To shed light on the dynamic evolution of chemosensory systems in toad-headed agamid lizards, we characterize and compare the repertoire of ORs and vomeronasal receptors gene families across 15 representative squamata species.
For the identification and comparison of OR gene families, we utilized reference OR sequences from vertebrates and a pipeline based on a previous study (Johnson et al. 2018). Candidate OR region in each genome assembly were identified, and conceptual translations were obtained using fasty tool from FASTA packages v36 (Zhang et al. 1997). Alignment to the Hidden Markov model (HMM) database containing OR and other rhodopsin gene family profiles was performed, and sequences not best aligned to the OR HMM were discarded as false positives. Identified OR genes were classified into intact and disrupted categories based on the presence of full-length open reading frames (ORFs) with seven complete transmembrane domains or incomplete ORFs due to pseudogenization or incomplete genome sequencing. For V2R genes involved in controlling pheromone-induced male–male aggression, we employed SPALN (Iwata and Gotoh 2012) to screen each genome assembly using previously identified V2R protein sequences in vertebrate. Putative V2R genes were then searched against Swiss-Prot and NR database to ensure that the best hit was a previously known V2Rs. Similar to the OR genes, identified V2R genes were classified into intact and disrupted categories. To assign CRs from toad-headed agamid lizards into potential groups, translated amino acid sequences were aligned by MAFFT v7.4 (Katoh and Standley 2013). An approximately ML phylogenetic tree was constructed using the JTT + CAT model with FastTree v2.1 (Price et al. 2009).

## Read Mapping and Variant Calling
Cleaned, high-quality short reads from each sample were mapped to the GN reference genome assembly using the BWA-MEM v0.7.17 (Li 2013) algorithm with default settings. Subsequently, duplicate reads were identified and removed from coordinate-sorted Binary Alignment Map files using the Picard MarkDuplicates function in Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) v4.2 (Van der Auwera and O’Connor 2020). Simultaneously, QC-failed reads, unmapped reads, nonprimary aligned reads, and supplementary alignment reads were filtered out using SAMtools v1.9 (Li et al. 2009), ensuring that only uniquely mapped reads were retained for subsequent variant calling.
Variant calling was conducted employing the HaplotypeCaller method following the GATK best practices procedure with the default parameters. To minimize the influence of sequencing and mapping bias, variants from joint calling were filtered with a hard filtration strategy based on the density distributions of the parameters of passed and failed variations, as recommended in the GATK procedure. The variant filtration algorithm applied the following filter expression parameters: for SNVs, QD < 2.0||FS > 60.0||SOR > 3.0||MQ < 40.0||MQRankSum < −12.5||ReadPosRankSum < −8.0; and for indels, QD < 2.0||FS > 200.0||SOR > 10.0||ReadPosRankSum < −20.0. Before filtering, 77,814,289 SNPs were identified, and 69,938,175 were retained after filtering. For population analysis, only biallelic SNPs meeting the criteria of (1) coverage should be higher than two and lower than 30, (2) genotype quality should be more than 20, and (3) minor allele frequency should be higher than 0.05, missing data should be less than 10% were retained. After applying these ﬁlters using VCFtools v0.1.16 (Danecek et al. 2011), a total of 26,087,744 biallelic SNPs were retained for subsequent analysis.

## LD, Phylogenetic Tree, and Population Structure
Before conducting phylogenetic and structure analysis, SNP data underwent LD pruning due to the computational demands of analyzing large datasets. LD was measured using correlation coefficients (r2) for pairwise SNPs within each chromosome via PLINK v1.9 (Purcell et al. 2007), and whole genome LD was averaged across all chromosomes. LD decay was visualized as pairwise LD against pairwise distance between SNPs. SNPs with an r2 value greater than 0.1 with any other SNP within a 50-SNP sliding window (shifted by 10 SNPs each time) were excluded. Population structure was assessed using PCA of the pruned SNPs for all 100 individuals with PLINK v1.9 (Purcell et al. 2007).
To assess clustering of individuals by species designation, ADMIXTURE v1.3 (Alexander et al. 2009) was employed with default settings. Assumed genetic clusters (K) ranged from two to nine, and the optimal structure was determined based on the lowest cross-validation error. An individual-based ML tree was constructed using RAxML-NG v1.1 (Kozlov et al. 2019) with the pruned SNPs for all accessions, evaluated with 100 bootstrap replicates. To conduct phylogenetic analysis, sites with missing data were discarded, and invariant sites were also removed as RAxML-NG cannot interpret them. GTR + ASC_LEWIS (Lewis ascertainment bias correction) model was employed taking into account the fact that only variable sites were used. P. vlangalii short-read data mapped to the GN genome served as the outgroup.

## Demography Reconstruction
To examined historical changes in effective population size (Ne) of GN and GD lizards, we utilized the same individuals employed for de novo genomic sequencing, ensuring an average sequencing depth greater than 46×. We employed the PSMC method (Li and Durbin 2011) to infer population size over time. Consensus sequences were generated and ﬁltered by excluding sites with low-inferred consensus quality (<20), as well as those with low (<one-third of the average coverage) and high-read depth (>twice the average read depth). The PSMC parameters were configured as follows: “-N25 -t15 -r5 -p 4 + 25*2 + 4 + 6”. PSMC results were scaled using the approximated neutral mutation rate (μ; 5 × 10−9 substitutions per site per generation), calculated using four-fold degenerate (4D) sites as described above, with a generation time (g) of 3 year. Sea surface temperature estimates were sourced from the literature (Hansen et al. 2013).
For additional estimation of evolutionary scenario parameters, coalescent simulations were conducted using fastsimcoal v2.7 (Excoffier et al. 2021) based on the composite likelihood method. Folded two-dimensional site-frequency spectrums (2D-SFS) were generated to minimize potential biases in determining ancestral allelic states. These spectra were then fitted with 11 alternative models, representing four possible scenarios with three gene flow patterns (no gene flow, recent gene flow, and continuous gene flow). The scenarios included: (1) a hybrid origin of the GM lineage from the other two lineages (models 1–3; supplementary fig. S8, Supplementary Material online); (2) a sister relationship of GM and GD (models 4–6; supplementary fig. S8, Supplementary Material online); (3) a sister relationship of GM and GN (models 7–9; supplementary fig. S8, Supplementary Material online); and (4) a radiative origin of the GD, GM, and GN (models 10–11; supplementary fig. S8, Supplementary Material online). The mutation rate per generation was set as same as in the PSMC analysis described above. A total of 100,000 coalescent simulations (−n) were performed to approximate the expected SFS per likelihood estimation, and 40 optimization cycles (−L) were run to estimate the parameters. For each model, 50 independent runs were conducted to ensure convergence, the best model was determined according to the Akaike information criterion.

## Genome-Wide Scanning of Selective Signatures and Candidates
To identify loci exhibiting strong genetic differentiation between distinct color groups, we conducted a window-based genome-wide scan using the complete set of previous filtered biallelic SNPs. We determined an appropriate window size based on the levels of LD to mitigate diversity estimation bias. LD showed a rapid decrease within 10 kb, following by a slower decline until 15 kb, suggesting that a window size of greater than 15 kb would avoid autocorrelation between adjacent windows. Nucleotide diversity (π) within populations and pairwise differentiation values (FST) between populations were calculated in non-overlapping 20-kb windows across the genome using VCFtools v0.1.16 (Danecek et al. 2011). Additionally, values for Tajima's D were calculated as a measure of the probability of sequence departure of a neutrally evolved model, also using VCFols.
While FST measures differentiation between two populations, PBS (Yi et al. 2010) estimates the amount of divergence in terms of allele frequency change specific to a particular species by incorporating pairwise FST values involving a third outgroup population. Loci with large PBS may represent targets of focal population-specific positive selection. However, high PBS values may arise when all populations have long branches. To address this limitation and avoid the identification of universally selected loci, PBE, a derivative of PBS (Yassin et al. 2016), was used to quantify the difference between observed and expected PBS value:
The subscript “a” refer to the focal population, and “b” and “c” are the two no-focal populations, with “med” denoting the median value. PBE is expected to be strongly positive when natural selection acts specifically on the focal colorful population, but not when selection has increased all branch lengths similarly. Windows with PBE values exceeding the 99th percentile of the distribution were considered highly divergent outliers.
To detect genomic regions under selective sweeps, xp-clr values were computed in non-overlapping 20-kb sliding windows across the genome using the package XP-CLRv1.1.2 (Chen et al. 2010). We estimated the degree of introgression and detected candidate introgression regions using modified D-statistics, fdM (Malinsky et al. 2015), with 20-kb sliding windows. Genomic region with positive fdM value suggested gene flow between GN and GM, while negative fdM value implied introgression between GN and GD.

## Protein 3D Structure and Complex Modeling
The 3D structure of the β-carotene ligand (PubChem CID: 5280489) was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The 3D structure of the BCO2 protein was predicted using AlphaFold2 (Akdel et al. 2022). Electrostatic potentials of BCO2 were calculated with the Adaptive Poisson-Boltzmann Solver in PyMOL v2.5.5 (Schrodinger 2015). The BCO2 was pre-processed using AutoDock tools (Morris et al. 2009) to eliminate water and add polar hydrogens, and assign Kollman charges. Following these steps, a protein blind docking procedure were conducted using QuickVina-W (Hassan et al. 2017) to optimize local searches with default docking parameters.

## Nature Selection Testing
We performed visual modeling in the R package pavo (Maia et al. 2019) to determine whether male and female color contribute to the mitigation of predation risk in sand dunes compared to dark soils in GN, with a focus on enhancing survival rate. Employing the RNL model (Vorobyev and Osorio 1998), we calculated both chromatic and achromatic contrasts for the color patches of GN lizards against sand dunes and dark soils, which represent the typical habitat of GD. The visual modeling is an objective method for color conspicuousness analysis because it simultaneously considers the senders’ spectral reflectance curve, the spectral sensitivities of cone pigments for the UV (UVS), short (SWS), medium (MWS), and long wavelengths (LWS) for the receivers’ visual system, and environmental irradiance measures (Endler and Mielke 2005). This approach has been widely used in camouflage research (Price et al. 2019; Mark et al. 2022; Whiting et al. 2022). In our visual modeling, we used the spectral reflectance curve of 209 GN individuals (90 males and 119 females). Receptor quantum catches between 300 and 700 nm wavelengths were derived, and each spectral reflectance curve was smoothed to eliminate electrical noise while correcting negative values. Additionally, we incorporated the spectral reflectance (nGN = 30, nGD = 50) and absolute irradiance (nGN = 33, nGD = 33) of habitats from GD and GN separately. For the predator visual system, we employed the spectral sensitivities of potential predator Buteo buteo for visual modeling, assuming λmax for the UVS, SWS, MWS, and LWS as 405 nm, 449 nm, 504 nm, and 567 nm, respectively, along with photoreceptor class densities of 1:2:2:4 (UVS:SWS:MWS:LWS; Lind et al. 2013). We calculated the discrimination thresholds as JNDs for chromatic and achromatic contrasts. To examine sexual differences in conspicuousness, we calculated the JNDs for males and females separately. Traditionally, a JND value greater than one is assumed as the threshold of discrimination between two colors, while JNDs greater than three indicate easy discrimination in optimal lighting conditions (Siddiqi et al. 2004). A signal-to-noise ratio (Weber fraction) of 0.10 was assumed in all cases.
The distinction in color conspicuousness (chromatic and achromatic contrast) of GN lizards between sand dunes and dark soil habitats was assessed using a linear mixed effect model (LMM; Bates et al. 2014) with a Gaussian error distribution in R v4.2.3 (R Core Team 2013). Conspicuousness was log transformed and treated as a dependent variable, with sex, habitat, and the interaction of sex and habitat as independent variable. To account for the impact of sampling site on color conspicuousness, we included sampling site as a random effect. SVL was added as a covariate to consider the effect of body size on color conspicuousness. Model residuals’ normality was assessed using the “qqnorm” function and served as an indicator of model goodness of fit.

## Sexual Selection Testing
Given the evident sexual color dimorphism in color variation, we conducted further analyses to explore the implications of color variation in males and females separately. For females, we hypothesized that conspicuousness of blue color likely signals individual quality, leading males to prefer blue-colored females to ensure the fitness of their offspring. To examine this hypothesis, we conducted three sets of dichotomous male mate choice trials: (1) blue-blocking and brightness-reduction versus control (examining the importance of blue color and brightness in mate choice), (2) brightness-reduction versus control (investigating whether brightness, rather than color, is crucial in male choice), and (3) blue-blocking and brightness-reduction versus brightness-reduction (evaluating the significance of blue color, not brightness, in male choice).
All trials were conducted within a choice arena comprising two female chambers (L: 30 cm × W: 30 cm × H: 20 cm), positioned in front of a resident male in the field. Glass chambers with nearly 100% light transmission were used for construction (supplementary fig. S15, Supplementary Material online; Fig. 5d). To manipulate female color and brightness, we employed three filter configurations: (1) a blue-blocking filter (R73, Rosco Laboratories Inc., Stamford, CT, USA) for minimal brightness and no blue color (MW), (2) a ND filter (Rosco E298.15 and E209.3) for reduced brightness while preserving blue color (ND), and (3) no filter for full brightness and natural blue color (FULL; Painting et al. 2016). To validate the blocking effect, we measured the transmission of MW filter, ND filter, and FULL glass using a Jaz optic spectrophotometer (Ocean Optics Inc., Dunedin, FL, USA) with a PX2 light source.
Twenty-four hours before each trial, we captured and marked the resident males and intruding females using noosing. SVL was measured to facilitate subsequent size matching. After the trials, males were returned to their original burrows and marked with numbered chopsticks. Paired females were size-matched to mitigate the influence of body size on male preference. Twenty-four hours later, we returned to the field and confirmed the presence of marked males using binoculars. A camera (100 frames per second, Sony RX10 markⅡ) was set up at least 4 m away from the resident male, and two empty chambers with different color treatments or controls were presented to the focal males simultaneously 30 cm away from the entrance of the male's burrow (Fig. 5d).
Each trial comprised three phases: (1) male acclimation phase, (2) female acclimation, and (3) mate assessment phase. The male acclimation phase of 5 min began when the two empty chambers were placed. This phase controlled for the possibility that males preferred a particular light environment or side instead of choosing between females with different reflectance or brightness. After the acclimation, the experimenter slowly approached the chambers, placed two size-matched females randomly into them (Fig. 5d), and simultaneously set up an opaque PVC panel horizontally between the focal male and two chambers. Five minutes were given to facilitate female acclimation. After female acclimation, another experimenter initiated video recording, and the male's response was recorded using video cameras (Fig. 5d). Each resident male underwent two trials in a random sequence, with each trial lasting 20 min and at least 24 h break between trials. The position of females in each trial was randomized to standardize the effect of directional bias. All experiments were conducted during the active time of the lizard between 10:00 and 17:00 h to avoid biases introduced by individual physiological conditions.
Male preference was assessed based on the time associated with particular females. A female was considered selected if the male either approached her exclusively or spent 50% more time near her chamber than the other chamber. A total of 71 trials were carried out in 29 males, and a binomial test was used to assess the male preference in each paired trial (MW vs. ND, MW vs. FULL, and ND vs. FULL) with the “binom.test” function in stats package in R v4.2.3, including 95% confidence intervals (R Core Team 2013).
For males, we hypothesized that conspicuousness of red color likely signals individual quality, with redder males being more competent in social interactions (Hamilton et al. 2013). To test this, we observed the male–male contests in the field and scored their competence. We conducted field surveys after the mating season in August 2021. Twenty-four hours before the observations, candidate lizards were captured within the study area using nooses. Each lizard was marked using a combination of two or three colored beads, allowing for individual observation and distinction from a distance without negative impacts on lizard behavior (Galdino et al. 2014; Galoyan et al. 2019). We measured the individual SVL using digital calipers (Mitutoyo) to nearest 0.01 mm. We measured male reflectance using the same method as described above. Twenty-four hours later, we carried out observations using focal sampling (Galoyan et al. 2023). To minimize the effect of animal physical condition, field observations were conducted between 10:00 and 17:00 h when lizards were active. The male who gave up the competition was regarded as the loser, and vice versa. We observed 20 male–male contests and scored the competence of nine males. To examine the association between male competence and individual coloration, we used the R package pavo (Maia et al. 2019) to calculate the chromatic (ΔS) and achromatic (ΔL) contrast for individual color using the same procedures as described above. Paired t-tests were used to compare the difference of color conspicuousness between winners and losers in stats package in R v4.2.3 (R Core Team 2013).
To explore the relationship between color conspicuousness and individual quality, we measured quality related morphological characters (SVL, tail length, head length, and head width) from 90 males and 119 females. We examined the relationship between color conspicuousness and individual quality using LMMs with a Gaussian error distribution using “lmer” function in the lme4 R package (Bates et al. 2014). Conspicuousness was log transformed and treated as a dependent variable, with SVL, tail length, head length, and head width as the independent variables. As for test relationship between conspicuousness and head size and tail length, we added SVL as covariate to account for the effect of body size. Residuals were tested using the “qqnorm” function in the stats package in R to ensure the assumption of normality.
