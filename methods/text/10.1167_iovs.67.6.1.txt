# Revisiting the Molecular Architecture of Photoreceptor Ribbon Synapses Using Ultrastructure Expansion Microscopy

## Abstract
AbstractPurposeImmunofluorescence-based mapping of photoreceptor ribbon synapse nanostructure in extended formaldehyde-fixed specimens is challenging due to diffraction-limited resolution and fixation-associated loss of antigen accessibility that restrict reliable molecular localization and structural interpretation. To circumvent these physicochemical limitations, we tested whether ultrastructure expansion microscopy (U-ExM) could overcome these barriers and enable compartment-resolved structural and molecular analysis of photoreceptor ribbon synapses in archival canine retinal tissues.MethodsCryosections from non-fixed and formaldehyde-fixed canine retinas were immunolabeled by conventional immunohistochemistry to screen 44 antibodies targeting 29 ribbon synapse–associated proteins. The same antibody set was then evaluated in U-ExM–processed formaldehyde-fixed cryosections. U-ExM datasets were analyzed using multi-orientation visualization, line-profile measurements, and three-dimensional segmentation for ribbon morphometry in mature and developing retinas.ResultsAntibody performance differed markedly between non-fixed and formaldehyde-fixed sections, indicating fixation-dependent loss or gain of detectability across targets. U-ExM increased the fraction of compatible antibodies in formaldehyde-fixed tissue and improved effective spatial resolution within synaptic compartments. Multi-orientation visualization refined interpretation of rod spherule architecture and revealed multiple postsynaptic density 95 (PSD-95) subcellular sites of expression, including a postsynaptic pool in horizontal cell axon terminals within the invaginated compartment. U-ExM further enabled subcompartment-level visualization of active zone proteins, extended compartment-resolved mapping to the more complex cone pedicle, and supported quantitative ribbon measurements in mature retina and across postnatal development.ConclusionsU-ExM provides a practical framework for compartment-resolved molecular mapping and quantitative phenotyping of photoreceptor ribbon synapses in formaldehyde-fixed/frozen archival retinal tissue, enabling systematic comparisons across conditions and developmental stages in translational and comparative vision research.Keywords: canine retina, immunohistochemistry, photoreceptor, ribbon synapses, super-resolution microscopy, ultrastructure expansion microscopy (U-ExM)
Photoreceptors transmit visual information to downstream retinal circuits through highly specialized ribbon synapses optimized for sustained glutamate release and precise signal transmission across varying lighting conditions. Central to this specialization is the synaptic ribbon, a presynaptic electron-dense structure that organizes large pools of synaptic vesicles (SVs) adjacent to the active zone (AZ) to support continuous neurotransmitter release.1 Beyond this presynaptic specialization, photoreceptor ribbon synapses form an especially complex synaptic interface in which bipolar cell (BC) dendrites and horizontal cell (HC) axon terminals are deeply invaginated and tightly interdigitated within the presynaptic terminal. This distinctive synaptic organization, characterized by extensive membrane invagination and the close apposition of multiple neuronal processes within a confined space, distinguishes photoreceptor ribbon synapses from other ribbon synapses.2 Superimposed on this complexity, fundamental functional and structural differences between rod and cone photoreceptors further complicate efforts to accurately define synaptic architecture and the underlying molecular organization (Fig. 1A).3

Much of our current understanding of photoreceptor ribbon synapse organization comes from electron microscopy (EM), which has provided indispensable insights into ribbon ultrastructure and the spatial arrangement of postsynaptic elements.3,4 However, EM-based approaches are low throughput, making systematic comparisons across synaptic orientations, developmental stages, or experimental conditions difficult.5 Molecular localization in EM datasets typically relies on immunogold labeling, which is technically demanding, inefficient, and poorly suited for multiplexed or large-scale analyses. Interpretation is further complicated by the limited availability of molecular identifiers, requiring synaptic components to be annotated primarily by morphology and spatial relationships.6,7 These constraints are especially problematic for photoreceptor ribbon synapses, where densely interwoven pre- and postsynaptic elements from multiple cell types must be interpreted simultaneously within a tightly confined synaptic volume.
Recent advances in super-resolution fluorescence microscopy, including SIM, STORM, and MINFLUX, enable nanoscale protein localization and are beginning to bridge the resolution gap between optical and electron microscopy.8–10 Yet, these approaches depend critically on efficient antibody labeling, which is often compromised in chemically fixed tissues by epitope masking and elevated background.11 This limitation is particularly severe for formaldehyde-fixed/frozen archival samples, which constitute a major fraction of clinically relevant human tissue and large-animal disease models such as dogs and non-human primates.12 As a result, despite their central importance for translational and comparative vision research, photoreceptor ribbon synapses in archival retinal specimens have remained largely inaccessible to high-resolution molecular analysis.
Ultrastructure expansion microscopy (U-ExM),13 an optimized form of expansion microscopy,14 offers an alternate strategy by combining physical tissue expansion with denaturation-mediated epitope unmasking, thereby enhancing both effective spatial resolution and molecular detectability in fixed and frozen tissues.15,16 Although U-ExM has recently been applied to selected photoreceptor subcompartments,15,17 photoreceptor ribbon synapses represent a particularly stringent and unresolved test case due to their dense molecular packing, deep membrane invagination, and complex cellular composition. Here, we applied U-ExM to extended formaldehyde-fixed/frozen canine retinal tissue and demonstrated that this approach enables systematic, multi-orientation visualization of ribbon synapse architecture while substantially expanding the range of ribbon-associated proteins that can be reliably analyzed. Using this framework, we revisited the organization of rod and cone ribbon synapses, integrated quantitative morphological analyses with molecular mapping, and uncovered structural features that were previously difficult to access in archival tissue. Together, these findings establish U-ExM as a powerful method for probing the molecular and structural organization of photoreceptor ribbon synapses in fixed retinal samples.

## Methods

## Animals
All archival retinal tissues originated from dogs that had been housed at the Retinal Disease Studies Facility, University of Pennsylvania, under standardized conditions, including a consistent diet and a 12-hour light/12-hour dark ambient illumination cycle. All procedures adhered to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and had been approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Pennsylvania. Retinal tissues used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1, including specimens that were analyzed in previous studies.18–20

## Retinal Tissue Processing
Procedures for both non-fixed and formaldehyde-fixed cryopreservation of canine ocular tissues were performed as described previously.15,19 For fixed samples, the fixation regimen was standardized across all specimens (4% formaldehyde for 3 hours followed by 2% formaldehyde for 24 hours). Retinal tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (O.C.T.) compound (Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan), snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C. Frozen O.C.T. blocks were cryosectioned at 10 or 20 µm, and the sections were stored at −20°C until further use. Sections stored at −20°C for several years after cryosectioning were not used because tissue architecture can be affected by desiccation during long-term storage.

## Immunohistochemistry on Non-Expanded Retinal Sections
Cryosections, 10 µm thick, were immunolabeled largely as described previously21 with minor modifications. No antigen retrieval was performed for either non-fixed or formaldehyde-fixed sections. Counterstaining with fluorescein-conjugated wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) and/or Hoechst 33342 was performed after secondary antibody incubation, as described previously.15 Antibodies and counterstains used for conventional immunostaining are listed in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3. All non-expanded samples were processed using the same staining workflow, with fixation condition as the only variable.

## U-ExM on Canine Retinal Cryosections
A previously validated U-ExM protocol for use on long-term cryopreserved retinal tissue was applied.15 Briefly, 20-µm-thick cryosections prepared from fixed/frozen canine retina were thawed, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove the O.C.T., and incubated overnight in an anchoring solution containing 2% acrylamide and 1.4% formaldehyde. Sections were then infiltrated with monomer solution containing 23% w/v sodium acrylate, 10% w/v acrylamide, 0.1% w/v N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide, and 1× PBS. The sections were subsequently gelled under a coverslip using monomer solution supplemented with 0.5% ammonium persulfate and 0.5% tetramethylethylenediamine, then denatured in 200-mM sodium dodecyl sulfate–containing buffer at 95°C, followed by the de-crowding step (first round of expansion) in distilled water. After the de-crowding step, immunohistochemistry was performed as described previously.15 For counterstaining, fluorescein-conjugated N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) ester (2 mg/mL stock in dimethyl sulfoxide) was diluted 1:200 in PBS and applied for 30 minutes after secondary antibody incubation. Primary and secondary antibodies and counterstains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3.

## Antibody Validation
Antibody validation was conducted using three biological replicates per antibody under each condition: non-expanded/non-fixed, non-expanded/fixed, and U-ExM/fixed (Supplementary Tables S1, S4). For each condition, staining was scored as compatible when the observed signal distribution agreed with the previously reported localization of the target protein (summarized in the Table). Antibodies were classified as “compatible” for a given condition if compatibility was observed in at least two of three biological replicates. Detailed imaging settings and scoring criteria are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

## Confocal Imaging
Image acquisition was performed using a STELLARIS 8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with a 63× water-immersion objective (NA 1.20), as described previously.15 Representative and quantitative images were processed in Leica Application Suite X (LAS X) for deconvolution using the LIGHTNING function with default parameters and for generating merged images and maximum-intensity projections (MIPs). Three-dimensional (3D) rendering was performed using Imaris 10.2.0 (Oxford Instruments, Bristol, UK). Unless otherwise indicated, representative images are shown as single optical sections.

## Quantification
Quantitative analyses, including expansion factor calculation, fluorescence intensity measurements, line-profile analysis, and Imaris-based 3D quantification, are described in detail in the Supplementary Methods. All quantifications were performed using three biological replicates (Supplementary Table S1).

## Statistical Analysis
For pairwise comparisons, we used the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test. For analyses involving more than three groups, we applied the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's post hoc multiple-comparison test. Quantitative results are presented as scatterdot plots; the central line indicates the median, and the error bars represent the interquartile range (IQR). Statistical significance is annotated as follows: ns, not significant (P > 0.05); ****P < 0.0001. All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA). Source data for all graphs are provided in the Supplementary Data.

## Results

## U-ExM Improves Antibody Labeling and Effective Resolution of Ribbon Synapse–Associated Proteins in Formaldehyde-Fixed/Frozen Retinal Tissue
To benchmark antibody performance for ribbon synapse–associated proteins under conditions relevant to archival retinal material, we compared canine retinal cryosections prepared from non-fixed/frozen tissue and extended formaldehyde-fixed (4% formaldehyde for 3 hours followed by 2% formaldehyde for 24 hours)/frozen tissue. Using standard immunohistochemistry and confocal imaging, we tested 44 antibodies targeting 29 proteins spanning multiple ribbon synapse subcompartments (Fig. 1B; Table; Supplementary Table S4). As a representative example, triple immunolabeling for protein kinase C alpha (PKCα), parvalbumin (PVALB), and C-terminal binding protein 2 (CtBP2) illustrated that formaldehyde fixation provided better preservation of tissue morphology, whereas antibody performance varied substantially across targets (Fig. 1C). Some antibodies, including anti-PKCα and anti-CtBP2, produced stronger labeling in non-fixed sections than in formaldehyde-fixed sections under matched imaging conditions, whereas others, exemplified by anti-PVALB, yielded little or no detectable signal in non-fixed tissue despite robust labeling in formaldehyde-fixed samples (Figs. 1C–E).
To systematically summarize these trends, we scored each antibody as compatible when its staining pattern matched previously reported localization for the corresponding target protein, based on prior studies in vertebrate retina. Using this criterion, 59% (26/44) and 52% (23/44) of antibodies were compatible in non-expanded/non-fixed and non-expanded/fixed samples, respectively (Fig. 1F; Table; Supplementary Table S4). Among antibodies compatible in non-fixed tissue, many showed higher signal intensity in non-fixed sections than in fixed sections, consistent with reduced antibody performance after fixation. In contrast, a subset of antibodies exhibited notably stronger staining in formaldehyde-fixed samples (Fig. 1G), including those against translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (TOMM20), cytochrome c, HC-enriched calcium-binding proteins (calbindin 1, calbindin 2, and PVALB), and the alpha subunit of Go (Goα). Together, these results indicate that formaldehyde fixation can compromise immunoreactivity for many targets yet remains essential for preserving tissue morphology and for retaining specific antigens that are not reliably detected in non-fixed cryosections.
Building on the non-expanded screening results, we next evaluated the same panel of antibodies (PKCα, PVALBP, and CtBP2) in U-ExM–processed formaldehyde-fixed/frozen retinal tissue. U-ExM enabled detection of all three targets in the representative triple-label experiment and improved visualization of synaptic components within a representative rod spherule in fixed samples (Figs. 1C, 1H). In line profiles across individual synaptic components, signals were distinguishable in non-expanded tissue but were typically represented as single broad peaks, whereas U-ExM revealed finer spatial features and sharper intensity gradients (Figs. 1D, 1E, 1I). Importantly, in U-ExM–processed tissue, approximately 80% of antibodies (35/44) of antibodies were scored as compatible (Fig. 1J; Table), indicating broad improvement in antibody performance in extended formaldehyde-fixed, long-term archived retinal tissue. Together, these results show that applying U-ExM to extended formaldehyde-fixed/frozen retina improves both effective resolution and antibody performance for ribbon synapse–associated proteins.

## Multi-Orientation U-ExM Visualization Refines Structural Interpretation and Protein Localization of the Rod Spherule
To enable quantitative comparison of spatial measurements, we first determined the expansion factor (EF) of our U-ExM workflow. Using rod nuclear diameter as an internal metric, we obtained an EF of 4.03 (Supplementary Fig. S1). To further assess whether the expansion factor derived from rod photoreceptor nuclear size was applicable to synaptic layer measurements, we independently estimated the EF within the outer plexiform layer (OPL) by comparing the lengths of corresponding regions before and after expansion along the horizontal and vertical axes at matched retinal locations (Supplementary Fig. S1F). The OPL-based EF values obtained from both axes were comparable to the nuclear-based EF value (Supplementary Fig. S1G). Unless stated otherwise, all distances were corrected by the nuclear-based EF.
We next analyzed the rod spherules ribbon synapses in normal adult canine retinas processed by U-ExM. Orientation-dependent differences in rod spherule appearance have been recognized from EM-based ultrastructural studies.43 However, incorporating orientation into routine immunohistochemistry has been challenging because diffraction-limited resolution precludes clear separation of closely apposed pre- and postsynaptic elements. As a result, prevailing schematics frequently portray the invaginated interface in a simplified manner (Figs. 2A, 2C).1,2,44

U-ExM enabled multi-orientational visualization of rod spherules with improved delineation of individual postsynaptic processes (Fig. 2B). In front-view images, the two rod-BC dendrites were readily resolved, whereas HC axon terminal labeling was evident but the individual terminals were often difficult to distinguish within the same plane. Notably, recoverin labeling revealed a photoreceptor-derived signal between the two rod-BC dendrites (green arrowhead), reminiscent of diverticuli-like structures previously suggested by EM studies (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. S2A).7,43 In specimens labeled for CtBP2, ribbon morphology facilitated orientation assignment of individual rod spherules (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Fig. S2B).
We then examined the localization of plasma membrane calcium ATPase 1 (PMCA1) and postsynaptic density 95 (PSD-95), two markers previously reported to localize along the photoreceptor plasma membrane.27,28,45 Consistent with prior reports, both PMCA1 and PSD-95 exhibited prominent labeling along the photoreceptor plasma membrane. In addition, PSD-95 displayed distinct signal clusters within the invaginated region in both front and side views (Fig. 2D). Because PSD-95 is classically discussed as a presynaptic marker in photoreceptor ribbon synapses,28,45 these intrainvagination signals prompted us to test whether PSD-95 might also be associated with postsynaptic processes at the rod spherule, as described at conventional synapses.46
To address this, we mapped the PSD-95 site of expression relative to defined postsynaptic elements. In front-view images, rod-BC dendrites were delineated by metabotropic glutamate receptor 6 (mGluR6) labeling (Fig. 2E). Line-profile analysis showed leucine-rich repeat, immunoglobulin-like and transmembrane domain 3 (LRIT3) peaks located outside the mGluR6-demarcated dendritic region, consistent with localization at the photoreceptor–BC interface (Fig. 2F).24,26 Importantly, PSD-95 peaks aligned with the mGluR6 signals were likewise positioned outside the dendritic region (Fig. 2F), supporting a presynaptic membrane assignment near the photoreceptor–BC interface (red arrowheads). In contrast, PSD-95 signals near the ribbon region (white arrowheads) consistently colocalized with PVALB-positive HC axon terminals in both front and side views (Fig. 2G) and spatially separated from recoverin-labeled photoreceptor cytoplasm (Supplementary Fig. S2B), supporting an HC-associated component.
Together, these multi-orientation analyses indicate that PSD-95 labeling in rod spherules is comprised of at least three spatially distinct patterns: a prominent pool along the non-invaginated photoreceptor plasma membrane that overlaps with PMCA1; a second presynaptic-associated pool near the rod-BC contact zone (including the diverticuli-like region), clearly separated from the mGluR6-demarcated dendritic region; and a third pool within the invagination that colocalizes with PVALB-positive HC axon terminals (Fig. 2H).

## U-ExM Resolves Rod Spherule Subcompartments With Quantitative Mapping of the Active Zone Proteins
Having established an orientation-aware interpretation of rod spherules, we next asked whether U-ExM could resolve the molecular organization of the AZ as a representative synaptic subcompartment. In our non-expanded screening (Fig. 1; Table), AZ-associated targets were among the most challenging to label reproducibly in formaldehyde-fixed/frozen sections. We therefore tested whether U-ExM enables nanoscale AZ mapping in archived tissue.
U-ExM enabled robust labeling and clear visualization of representative AZ proteins, including the voltage-gated calcium channel Cav1.4, regulating synaptic membrane exocytosis 2 (RIM2), cytomatrix at the active zone–associated structural protein (CAST), and Bassoon, in both side and front views of rod ribbon synapses (Figs. 3A–D). Each AZ marker formed a narrow distribution along the inner edge of the ribbon that was readily separable from co-labeled reference signals for the ribbon (CtBP2) and PSD-95 (Figs. 3E–H). Thus, U-ExM provides sufficient effective resolution to compare AZ protein distributions relative to shared reference landmarks, even when individual AZ proteins were not co-imaged. Consistent with established AZ organization,47,48 Cav1.4, RIM2, and CAST showed closely aligned peak positions in line profiles, whereas Bassoon localized deeper toward the ribbon-proximal side of AZ (Fig. 3I). Notably, Cav1.4 localized between the ribbon and the PSD-95 signals, supporting the assignment of the ribbon-adjacent PSD-95 population to HC axon terminals (Figs. 3A, 3E).

We next examined the subcellular localization of calcium/calmodulin-dependent serine protein kinase (CASK), implicated in AZ-associated scaffolding but with unclear distribution at photoreceptor ribbon synapses.23,49 Triple labeling for PSD-95, RIM2, and CASK revealed two reproducible CASK populations. At the AZ, CASK signal closely overlapped with RIM2 in side-view images but remained distinct from the juxtaposed PSD-95 signals in front-view images (Fig. 3J). Consistently, line profiles across the AZ region showed the CASK peak positioned between the PSD-95 and RIM2 peaks and shifted toward RIM2 (Fig. 3K), supporting localization of CASK within an AZ-proximal scaffold. A second CASK population was observed near the PSD-95 signal associated with the rod-BC invaginated interface (Fig. 3J). These observations define CASK localization at rod ribbon synapses with subcompartment-level resolution and support its association with both the AZ and the photoreceptor–BC contact site. We have summarized the resulting rod spherule and AZ architecture, integrating our observations with previously reported interactions,1 in updated schematics (Figs. 3L, 3M).
Finally, to illustrate the broader applicability of U-ExM beyond the AZ, we present representative examples of additional rod ribbon synapse compartments visualized in formaldehyde-fixed/frozen retinal tissue, including presynaptic mitochondria, synaptic vesicle markers, and photoreceptor–BC contact sites (Supplementary Fig. S3). Although descriptive, these panels illustrate that U-ExM reveals finer compartment-level structural detail and marker organization than conventional immunohistochemistry in fixed tissue.

## Applying U-ExM to Cone Pedicles Enables Component-Resolved Visualization in a Highly Complex Synapse
Cone pedicles contain multiple ribbons and a larger set of interwoven pre- and postsynaptic elements, making systematic protein mapping substantially more challenging than in rod spherules (Fig. 1A).3 Building on our rod analyses, we next focused on establishing whether U-ExM can be extended to cone pedicles to achieve component-resolved visualization of key synaptic compartments. U-ExM enabled clear visualization of canonical triad organization in cone pedicles, with a single ON-BC dendrite and two HC axon terminals forming an invaginating arrangement that is difficult to resolve by diffraction-limited imaging (Fig. 4A). Consistent with our rod data, PMCA1 labeling was restricted to the non-invaginated photoreceptor plasma membrane, whereas PSD-95 showed prominent signals both along the non-invaginated membrane and within the synaptic contact region (Fig. 4B). Within synaptic contact sites of cone pedicles, PSD-95 formed three spatially distinct cluster patterns (Figs. 4C–E): clusters positioned along the lateral wall of the BC invagination (red arrowheads), clusters at the deepest portion of the invagination aligned with the AZ (white arrowheads), and clusters arranged in a flat plane outside the invaginated region (green arrowheads). The lateral clusters were clearly segregated from G protein-coupled receptor 179 (GPR179)-labeled ON-BC dendrites (Fig. 4D), whereas the deep clusters fully overlapped with PVALB-labeled HC terminals (Fig. 4E). Notably, the two invaginated PSD-95 patterns mirrored those observed in rod spherules. In addition, synapse-associated protein 102 (SAP102) was detectable on invaginating HC terminals, but SAP102 and PSD-95 formed distinct clusters (Fig. 4E), consistent with the idea that membrane-associated guanylate kinase (MAGUK) scaffolds can segregate into separable nanodomains within a shared synaptic interface.50

We next examined ionotropic glutamate receptors at cone pedicle contacts (Figs. 4F–H). Glutamate ionotropic receptor α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) type subunit 4 (GluA4) labeling was enriched on HC terminals, consistent with prior work in mammals.37,38 In contrast, glutamate ionotropic receptor kainate type subunit 1 (GluK1) and subunit 2/3 (GluK2/3) signals were segregated from HC terminals and concentrated near non-invaginating basal contact sites. In GluK2/3 and PSD-95 co-labeled images, GluK2/3 puncta were positioned adjacent to the flat PSD-95 clusters on the cone pedicle side, suggesting that PSD-95 may also be associated with presynaptic domains facing OFF-BC contacts in canine cone pedicles.
Finally, analogous to the rod examples (Supplementary Fig. S3), we provide representative U-ExM visualizations of presynaptic mitochondria and synaptic vesicle markers in cone pedicles (Supplementary Fig. S4). Although descriptive, these panels further illustrate that U-ExM can resolve multiple cone pedicle compartments in formaldehyde-fixed/frozen tissue.

## Three-Dimensional U-ExM Analysis Enables Quantitative Ribbon Morphometry in Mature and Developing Retina
To test whether U-ExM supports quantitative analyses beyond qualitative protein localization, we performed 3D segmentation and volumetric measurements of photoreceptor ribbons in mature canine retina. Ribbons were identified by CtBP2 and referenced to RIM2-labeled AZs, and all measurements were corrected for the EF. The 3D renderings revealed two recurrent rod ribbon morphologies: the conventional C-shaped ribbon and a second form in which one or two small CtBP2-positive spherical bodies (SBs) were positioned adjacent to the main ribbon structure (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Video S1). These SBs consistently lacked a corresponding RIM2-defined AZ signal, suggesting a ribbon-associated specialization not directly aligned with an AZ. In light-adapted samples, SB-associated ribbons accounted for approximately half of rod ribbons across retinal regions (Fig. 5B). Using 3D segmentation, we quantified individual rod ribbon volumes and found no regional difference between the visual streak region (VSR) and peripheral region (PR) (Fig. 5C), consistent with independent manual measurements of ribbon length and height (Supplementary Fig. S5).

We next extended the quantitative framework to cones. Cone pedicles were identified by arrestin 3 (ARR3), and ribbons and AZs were visualized by CtBP2 and RIM2, respectively. Three-dimensional reconstructions revealed two cone pedicle organizational patterns: one with ribbons broadly dispersed across the pedicle and another with ribbons clustered near the center (Fig. 5D; Supplementary Video S2). Because subtype-specific opsins are normally restricted to outer segments, cone subtype assignment is difficult in intact mature tissue. To link these patterns to cone subtype, we leveraged early-stage X-linked progressive retinal atrophy (XLPRA2) retina, in which cone opsins mislocalizes throughout the cell including to the pedicles, enabling identification of S-cone versus L-/M-cone pedicles.51 Consistent with classic observations in non-human primates,39,52 L-/M-opsin–labeled pedicles exhibited more widely distributed ribbons than S-cone pedicles (Supplementary Fig. S6), supporting the interpretation that the two organizational patterns observed in normal canine retina likely reflect cone subtype-associated differences.
Unlike rod spherules, cone pedicles contain multiple closely spaced ribbons, limiting reliable separation of individual CtBP2-positive ribbons in 3D, particularly in S-cone pedicles where ribbons are densely clustered and frequently layered (Fig. 5D). We therefore quantified AZ number per pedicle as a robust proxy for presynaptic release site organization. Total ribbon volume per pedicle did not differ between VSR and PR (Fig. 5E) and did not segregate the two cone organizational patterns, suggesting that overall ribbon material is not strongly predictive of subtype. In contrast, plotting planar ribbon dispersion area against AZ number revealed two apparent clusters: broadly dispersed ribbons with higher AZ counts versus centrally clustered ribbons with fewer AZs (Fig. 5F). We also occasionally observed SB-like structures in cone pedicles that lacked an associated RIM2-defined AZ signal; however, these were not systematically analyzed in the present study. Together, these results show that U-ExM–based 3D analysis can quantify ribbon organizational features in cones and provide a tractable framework to test how ribbon geometry and AZ allocation relate to cone subtype and circuit specialization.
Finally, we applied the same U-ExM–based 3D workflow to normal developing canine retinas to characterize how photoreceptor ribbons mature across postnatal stages. Structural maturation in canine retina is accompanied by progressive thinning of the total retinal thickness and has been reported to approach an adult-like state by approximately 20 weeks of age.53,54 Consistent with this, widefield imaging of non-expanded sections revealed a gradual decrease in outer nuclear layer and inner nuclear layer thickness from 2 to 24 weeks (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Fig. S7), whereas the OPL exhibited a distinct trajectory, thickening between 2 and 6 weeks and subsequently thinning toward 24 weeks (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Fig. S7). In parallel, the density and overall distribution of CtBP2-labeled ribbons within the OPL also varied across stages (Fig. 6A). At synapse scale, U-ExM revealed progressive enlargement of rod ribbons associated with RIM2-defined AZs across development (Figs. 6B, 6C). At 2 weeks, rod ribbons were small and frequently appeared fragmented or accompanied by multiple CtBP2-positive puncta, consistent with incomplete assembly. By 6 weeks, ribbons had largely consolidated into the characteristic C-shaped morphology, followed by continued growth through 24 weeks (Figs. 6B, 6C). Cone pedicles showed a similar qualitative trajectory: Ribbon structures were still assembling at 2 weeks but appeared largely established by 6 weeks, with further maturation thereafter (Fig. 6D). Overall, these analyses demonstrate that U-ExM–enabled 3D mapping supports quantitative morphometry of photoreceptor ribbons across both mature and developing retina in extended formaldehyde-fixed/frozen tissue.

## Discussion
We demonstrated that U-ExM enables systematic molecular and structural analysis of photoreceptor ribbon synapses in extended formaldehyde-fixed/frozen retinal tissue, a type of sample that has been largely inaccessible to high-resolution fluorescence-based interrogation. A key advantage is that U-ExM improves more than effective spatial resolution: Protein denaturation coupled with physical expansion reduces molecular crowding and effectively acts as antigen retrieval, improving antibody accessibility and, in many cases, labeling specificity (Fig. 1). In conventional immunolabeling workflows for synaptic proteins, formaldehyde fixation is often intentionally kept brief (typically on the order of minutes) to preserve epitope reactivity while maintaining basic tissue integrity.10,22,25,26,30,33,35,37–39,49,50 By contrast, in clinical specimens and large-animal models where samples are scarce and fine-tuning of fixation duration is rarely feasible, tissues are frequently subjected to prolonged immersion fixation that imposes strong epitope masking and limits reliable nanoscale labeling. Under such conditions, precise immunofluorescence mapping of synaptic nanostructures has remained challenging. By restoring broad antibody usability in archival sections, U-ExM can add substantial value to specimens previously considered suboptimal for detailed molecular analysis. Moreover, related expansion-based workflows have been extended to additional specimen formats, including paraffin-embedded tissue,16 suggesting a practical route toward nanoscale molecular phenotyping across diverse retinal repositories.
An important practical limitation highlighted by this study is that antibody compatibility in canine retinal tissue cannot be assumed, even when U-ExM is applied. Most antibodies judged incompatible under U-ExM conditions were also incompatible in both non-expanded/non-fixed and non-expanded/fixed samples, suggesting that in many cases the limiting factor was not the U-ExM procedure itself but insufficient baseline reactivity to the canine target protein. Because many commercially available antibodies are generated against human immunogens, cross-reactivity with canine proteins is not necessarily guaranteed, which likely contributed to the poor performance of some antibodies. One illustrative example was the anti-Bassoon antibody (#3), which was only marginally compatible in non-fixed/non-expanded samples and lost the expected signal pattern after fixation. Application of U-ExM did not restore sufficiently robust labeling in this case, suggesting that, although U-ExM can reduce the impact of epitope masking, it cannot fully rescue weak or borderline antigen recognition. Thus, successful application of U-ExM still depends on the underlying affinity and specificity of the antibody, and pilot antibody validation remains important when designing experiments on archival fixed tissue.
In this study, compartment-level features of photoreceptor ribbon synapses were visualized primarily using endogenous molecular markers. By targeting proteins that are enriched in defined retinal cell classes and broadly distributed within the cytoplasm, including recoverin and ARR3 for photoreceptors, PKCα for rod-BCs, and PVALB or calbindin 1 for HCs, we delineated cellular profiles while simultaneously resolving substructures within the invaginated interface. For example, recoverin labeling helped outline photoreceptor-derived features at rod spherules and supported the identification of a recoverin-positive structure positioned between adjacent rod-BC dendrites that is reminiscent of diverticuli-like elements7,43 (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. S2). Moreover, photoreceptor-associated proteins such as pikachurin, dystrophin, and LRIT3 provided complementary landmarks around rod-BC dendrites, thereby constraining the geometry of the photoreceptor–BC interface that is often simplified in schematic models (Figs. 2–4; Supplementary Fig. S3). At the same time, endogenous labeling alone was insufficient to fully resolve and reconstruct the complete architecture of the invaginated interface. This limitation reflects heterogeneity in endogenous protein abundance and subcellular distribution, which can yield nonuniform labeling of relevant cellular profiles and fine protrusions. In addition, the limitation is especially evident for cell classes for which bright, broadly applicable cytoplasmic markers are not readily available, including OFF bipolar cell pathways. Thus, combining U-ExM with orthogonal labeling strategies, such as targeted expression of fluorescent reporters or delivery of neuronal tracers, should further improve segmentation and enable more comprehensive structural interpretation of photoreceptor ribbon synapses.
In terms of biological insight, one of the most informative findings of this study is that PSD-95 at photoreceptor ribbon synapses is not restricted to a single “photoreceptor terminal marker” pattern. Rather, our multi-orientation U-ExM visualization supports a model in which PSD-95 occupies multiple spatially separable domains within the photoreceptor terminal and, unexpectedly, is also prominent in HC axon terminals inside the invaginated compartment (Figs. 2H, 3L). This organization argues against the simplified depiction in many retinal schematics and motivates a revised working model in which MAGUK-based scaffold assemblies are partitioned across distinct contact zones within a single ribbon synapse, potentially coordinating receptor organization and signaling at different interfaces. In both rods and cones, PSD-95 colocalized with PMCA1 along the non-invaginated plasma membrane, whereas PMCA1 was not detected within the invaginated compartment (Figs. 2C, 4B), consistent with the idea that PSD-95 participates in molecularly distinct scaffold assemblies in non-invaginated versus invaginated domains. Extending U-ExM mapping to additional candidate components proposed to associate with photoreceptor terminals, including MPP4 and TMEM16B, should help refine the molecular role of these PSD-95-positive domains.
A limitation of the present analysis is that HC terminals were not always resolved as two fully separated axon terminals, in part because the HC markers used here, calbindin 1 and PVALB, are cytoplasmic rather than membrane-delimiting markers. Accordingly, the present data do not define HC terminal boundaries with complete precision. However, the PSD-95 signal in this region consistently overlapped with HC-associated labeling (Figs. 2G, 4E) and remained spatially distinct from Cav1.4-labeled photoreceptor presynaptic membrane domains (Figs. 3A, 3E), supporting the interpretation that this PSD-95 population is associated with HC terminals rather than the photoreceptor plasma membrane.
Notably, postsynaptic PSD-95 in HCs at photoreceptor ribbon synapses has received limited attention in prior retinal literature, making its robust detection here an important conceptual advance. Given the canonical role of PSD-95 as a postsynaptic scaffold at conventional synapses46 and transcriptomic evidence that discs large MAGUK scaffold protein 4 (DLG4) is not confined to photoreceptors,55 HC-associated PSD-95 is biologically plausible. Two key questions follow. First, what is the functional role of PSD-95 in HC axon terminals within the invagination? Our front-view localization is spatially consistent with electron-dense specializations described at HC-associated contacts in the invaginated region (Figs. 2, 3),3 raising the possibility that PSD-95 contributes to organizing AMPA receptor at these sites. Second, why was PSD-95 not detected in BC dendrites under our conditions? Although we identified another MAGUK scaffold, SAP102, its signal was likewise confined to HC terminals (Fig. 4E),42 suggesting that BC pathways may rely on distinct scaffolds, including SAP97 and Chapsyn110,55 that remain to be identified. Addressing these questions will require expanded antibody panels and higher-density co-labeling strategies compatible with U-ExM, enabling systematic assignment of scaffold–receptor architectures across each contact zone.
A further structural insight from our 3D analysis is that cone pedicles in mature canine retina exhibited two recurring organizational patterns: one with broadly dispersed ribbons and relatively higher AZ counts and another with more tightly clustered ribbons and fewer AZs (Fig. 5). In light of the XLPRA2-based subtype assignment (Supplementary Fig. S6) and prior observations in non-human primates,39,52 these patterns are most consistent with subtype-associated differences between L-/M-cone-like and S-cone-like pedicles rather than representing a new cone classification. The significance of this finding is therefore not that cone subtype differences in pedicle organization are newly identified here but that U-ExM–based measurements of ribbon dispersion and AZ allocation can capture presynaptic architectural features that parallel known subtype-dependent circuit organization. In this sense, U-ExM provides a practical framework for linking cone subtype identity to synaptic output architecture at the level of ribbon arrangement and release-site distribution. We also note that, within the broadly dispersed/high-AZ cluster, a possible regional shift between the PR and VSR retina can be seen in Figure 5F. In particular, pedicles within this cluster in the PR tended to occupy a somewhat higher AZ-count range than those in the VSR. Because cone subtype could not be assigned directly in normal mature tissue (in which cone opsins do not mislocalize to the synaptic pedicle) and no dedicated statistical analysis was performed for this comparison, this observation should be considered preliminary. Nevertheless, it raises the possibility that presynaptic cone architecture may vary not only with cone subtype but also with retinal region.
Another biological insight from this study is the frequent presence of CtBP2-positive ribbon-associated SBs in canine rod spherules. These SBs were consistently positioned adjacent to the main C-shaped ribbon but lacked an aligned RIM2-defined AZ (Fig. 5). The presence of SBs is reminiscent of spherical or club-shaped ribbon profiles described by EM in mouse retina, whose relative abundance has been linked to circadian and light–dark conditions.56–58 However, because our analyses were limited to light-adapted archival canine samples, we were not able to assess whether SB occurrence varies between light- and dark-adapted conditions or across circadian states in this species. Notably, we did not observe the prominent giant club-shaped ribbon structures reported in rodents, suggesting that ribbon morphology and remodeling in mature photoreceptors may differ across species and/or experimental contexts. SB-like ribbon-associated structures were occasionally noted in cone pedicles, as well, but these were not systematically characterized in the present study. Given that mechanistic insight into physiological ribbon remodeling in mature photoreceptors remains limited, broader application of quantitative U-ExM across species should help determine whether SBs represent a canine-enriched feature or a conserved ribbon substructure across vertebrates.
We conclude that U-ExM provides a practical and broadly applicable workflow for nanoscale, compartment-resolved analysis of photoreceptor ribbon synapses in extended formaldehyde-fixed/frozen retinal archives, overcoming key limitations of conventional immunofluorescence in strongly fixed specimens. Looking forward, applying this approach to clinically relevant and translational disease collections should provide a tractable route to test how retinal degeneration perturbs synaptic microarchitecture, how synaptic connectivity is remodeled after effective gene therapy interventions, and how donor photoreceptors establish new synaptic contacts with host BCs and HCs in retinal cell transplantation settings.

## Figure Figure 5.: Morphological characterization and quantitative analysis of rod and cone synaptic ribbons in the mature canine retina. (A) Representative 3D surface renderings of rod synaptic ribbons and AZs, with corresponding two-dimensional MIP images shown on the right. Conventional C-shaped ribbons are shown in magenta, whereas ribbons accompanied by small SBs are highlighted in cyan. Scale bars: 1 µm, corrected for the expansion factor. (B) Comparison of frequency of rod ribbons with SBs across retinal regions. Data were obtained from six retinal locations across three individual eyes per location. The central line indicates the median, and the error bars represent the interquartile range. No significant difference was observed between the VSR and PR (Mann–Whitney U test; n.s., not significant, P > 0.05). (C) Quantification of the volume of individual rod synaptic ribbons. Data were obtained from 104 to 141 ribbons across three individual eyes per location. The central line indicates the median, and the error bars represent the interquartile range. No significant difference was observed between the VSR and PR (Mann–Whitney U test; n.s., not significant, P > 0.05). Volume values were corrected by the cube of the expansion factor. (D) Representative 3D surface renderings of cone pedicles illustrating two organizational patterns: one with widely dispersed ribbons (left) and one with ribbons clustered near the center (right). Cone ribbons and AZs are shown in magenta and yellow, respectively. Individual cone pedicles were identified by ARR3 labeling (cyan). Scale bars: 1 µm, corrected for the expansion factor. (E) Quantification of the total ribbon volume per cone pedicle. Data were obtained from 20 to 26 cone pedicles across three individual eyes per location. The central line indicates the median, and the error bars represent the interquartile range. No significant difference was observed between VSR and PR (Mann–Whitney U test; n.s., not significant, P > 0.05). Volume values were corrected by the cube of the expansion factor. (F) Scatterplot illustrating the relationship between the number of AZs per cone pedicle and the planar dispersion area of synaptic ribbons within individual cone pedicles. Each data point represents one cone pedicle that was also used for the volumetric analysis in panel E. Area values were corrected by the square of the expansion factor.
