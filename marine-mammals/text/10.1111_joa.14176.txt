Variation in whale (Cetacea) inner ear anatomy reveals the early evolution of “specialized” high‐frequency hearing sensitivity
Rachel A. Racicot, Mickaël J. Mourlam, Eric G. Ekdale, Abigail Glass, Lori Marino, Mark D. Uhen (2024). Journal of Anatomy. https://doi.org/10.1111/joa.14176. doi:10.1111/joa.14176. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Variation in whale (Cetacea) inner ear anatomy reveals the early evolution of “specialized” high‐frequency hearing sensitivity

## Abstract
AbstractAs fully aquatic mammals, hearing is arguably the most important sensory component of cetaceans. Increasingly, researchers have been harnessing computed tomography (CT) to investigate the details of the inner ear as they can provide clues to the hearing abilities of whales. We use microCT scans of a broad sampling of the ear bones (periotics) of primarily toothed whales (Odontoceti) to investigate the inner ear bony labyrinth shape and reconstruct hearing sensitivities among these cetaceans, including several taxa about which little is currently known. We find support for sensitivity to the lower frequency spectrum in the archaeocete Zygorhiza kochii and an early toothed mysticete cf. Aetiocetus. Oligocene odontocetes (including one from our novel dataset), stem delphinidans, and two additional species of the long‐snouted eurhinodelphinids are found to have been able to hear within the narrow‐band high‐frequency spectrum (NBHF), which is thought to be a specialized form of hearing that evolved convergently multiple different times in extant groups to avoid predation by macroraptorial predators. Our results thus indicate that NBHF evolved as early as the Oligocene and certainly in stem delphinidans by the early Miocene, and thus may be an ancestral characteristic rather than a more recent innovation in select groups.

## INTRODUCTION
Hearing is a crucial sense to the fully aquatic lifestyles of all whales, dolphins, and porpoises (Cetacea). Cetaceans use sound to communicate, navigate, forage, and avoid predators. The aquatic environment restricts vision and the sense of smell, and sound propagates faster and farther in water than in air, making hearing an effective sensory modality for fully aquatic mammals. The evolution of hearing in whales has been a topic of great interest, both because of its central importance to a fully aquatic lifestyle (Nummela & Yamato, 2018) and for modern conservation efforts (e.g., Simmonds & Lopez‐Jurado, 1991; Wartzok et al., 2004). MicroCT scanning has enabled detailed investigations into hearing sensitivities of whales through deep time (reviewed in Racicot, 2021), particularly as it enables unprecedented visualization of the cochlear region of the inner ear. Comparative anatomy of the cochlea has been integral to our ability to interpret hearing‐related structures in this small area of the auditory region of whales, as multiple metrics of the cochlea correspond well with specific hearing sensitivities.
Low‐frequency hearing is apparent in the earliest fully aquatic whales such as the basilosaurid archaeocete Zygorhiza kochii (Ekdale & Racicot, 2015), and in early‐diverging baleen whales (Mysticeti), including those that possessed teeth prior to the evolution of baleen (Ekdale, 2016; Park, Evans, et al., 2017). All extant toothed whales (Odontoceti) echolocate, which involves locating objects and surroundings by actively producing and receiving reflected sound. Odontocetes use exceptionally high‐frequency (ultrasonic, >100 kHz) signals in their echolocation (e.g., Galatius et al., 2019). Based on skull and cochlear shape in the Xenorophidae, an early‐diverging sister group to the clade including crown odontocetes, underwater echolocation likely evolved over the course of approximately 10 million years during the Oligocene (~30 Mya; Churchill et al., 2016; Geisler et al., 2014; Park et al., 2016). The early origins of echolocation in the lineage leading to crown odontocetes are still unclear, as a member of the stem‐group Simocetidae (cf. Olympicetus) was found to be likely not sensitive to echolocation frequencies (Racicot et al., 2019), suggesting that echolocation evolved in parallel in xenorophids and the clade including crown odontocetes. Regardless, the evolution of echolocation involved extensive modification of craniofacial anatomy and formation of specialized, novel sound production structures (e.g., Coombs et al., 2022; Cranford et al., 1996), as well as restructuring of the brain and auditory complex to accommodate the processing of sound (Berns et al., 2015).
Extant odontocetes use four different types of echolocation clicks: the broadband multi‐pulsed (MP) click of sperm whales (Physeteridae), frequency‐modulated (FM) clicks of the Ziphiidae, broadband (BB) clicks, and narrow‐band high‐frequency (NBHF) clicks (Au, 1993; Jensen et al., 2018; Johnson et al., 2004, 2006; Kyhn et al., 2009, 2010, 2013; Møhl et al., 2003). Of these, most delphinoids and river dolphins use simple broad band (BB) clicks, whereas several other groups rely on narrow‐band high‐frequency (NBHF) clicks. Narrow‐band high‐frequency signals are considered a specialization for using signals in the higher end of the ultrasonic spectrum (peak frequencies at 125–140 kHz, e.g., Jensen et al., 2018). Extant odontocetes that use NBHF signals (all members of the clades Kogiidae, Phocoenidae, Pontoporiidae, and some members of Delphinidae, namely Sagmatias spp. [sensu Vollmer et al., 2019] and Cephalorhynchus spp.) are disparately related, and so it is thought to have evolved convergently in these groups (e.g., Andersen & Amundin, 1976; Madsen et al., 2005; Morisaka & Connor, 2007). In part because of the small size of these animals, workers have suggested that NBHF evolved as an “acoustic crypsis” to avoid detection by macroraptorial predators such as Orcinus orca who cannot hear NBHF signals. Additional support for this hypothesis was recently provided by Galatius et al. (2019), by comparing the earliest known extinct (fossil) species of each extant NBHF clade with that of the macroraptorial sperm whale Acrophyseter deinodon and finding that this macropredator had similar hearing abilities to Orcinus orca and likely could not hear well in the NBHF spectrum.
Increasingly, members of extinct odontocete clades are found to have had the ability to hear within the NBHF spectrum, with further suggestion of evolutionary convergence of this feature in the disparate stem delphinidan groups. For example, some members of the Squalodontidae, Platanistoidea, Kentriodontidae, and Eurhinodelphinidae share cochlear morphologies and consequently nest within the morphospace of extant species known to use NBHF signals (e.g., Galatius et al., 2019; Lambert et al., 2022; Sanks & Racicot, 2024). Squalodontidae is an extinct, early‐diverging group that lived during the Miocene and retained ancestral characteristics such as heterodont dentition and nasal turbinates (Godfrey, 2013). Platanistoidea are a formerly diverse (particularly in the Miocene) group of long‐snouted homodont dolphins that today comprises two species (Platanista gangetica and Platanista minor). The Eurhinodelphinidae are an extinct group of hyperlongirostrine dolphins with a longer upper jaw than lower jaw that flourished in the Miocene. The Kentriodontidae is also a now‐extinct group that comprises one species, Kentriodon pernix (Godfrey & Lambert, 2023) of small‐ to medium‐sized “generalized” odontocete with a relatively symmetrical skull shape. Thus, extinct relatives of modern odontocetes may have expanded or specialized their echolocation abilities before their modern relatives, potentially convergently, and possibly driven by the same mechanisms such as “acoustic crypsis” from coeval macroraptorial predators in the Miocene.
Here, we combine a novel body of fossil cetacean bony labyrinth data representing extant (e.g., Kogiidae, Platanistidae) and extinct (e.g., Basilosauridae, Aetiocetidae, Squalodontidae, Xenorophidae, and Eurhinodelphinidae) clades with previously published datasets to give a deep time evolutionary context for our understanding of the evolution of hearing in whales, particularly odontocetes. Based on previous results, we hypothesize that the cochlear morphology of all odontocete taxa in our novel dataset correspond with the ability to hear echolocation signals in the ultrasonic frequency spectrum and that Zygorhiza and the extinct toothed mysticete (cf. Aetiocetus) have morphology that corresponds with the ability to hear in the lower, if not infrasonic, frequency spectra. We further hypothesize that members of the Squalodontidae, the early delphinidan previously ascribed to the Kentriodontidae, Brevirostrodelphis dividum, and Eurhinodelphinidae, will have had the ability to hear especially high‐frequency signals within the spectrum used by NBHF specialists. Finally, we seek to quantitatively distinguish NBHF bony labyrinths from other “hearing types” so that they may be readily identified by other researchers, assisting future investigations. Our dataset and results complement the growing body of knowledge surrounding cetacean inner ears and can be used to further enhance our understanding of the ecology and evolution of cetaceans.

## METHODS

## Specimens and scanning
Specimens representing primarily archaic odontocete taxa were selected, as well as a basilosaurid Zygorhiza kochii, and a toothed mysticete, cf. Aetiocetus. Specimens were scanned at The University of Texas High Resolution X‐ray CT Facility (specimen list and relevant scanning parameters in Table 1).
Specimen list and scanning parameters of novel dataset.

## Segmentation, measurements, and analyses
The endocasts of the bony labyrinths of all specimens were digitally isolated in VGStudioMax v. 2.2 and 2023.4 using standard methods, for example, primarily the “magic wand,” “paint,” and "paint and segment” tools. Curved (cochlear length, secondary bony lamina length) and fenestra cochleae area (FC) measurements were taken in VGStudioMax. Subsequently, the endocasts were extracted as STL files and imported into Avizo 3D 2021.2 along with the original TIFF image stacks (i.e., CT slices) to obtain additional measurements. Following previous work, we collected nine measurements of each cochlea (i.e., cochlear canal length (Cl), secondary bony lamina length (SBL), cochlear height (Ch), cochlear width (Cw), shorter diameter of the basal turn, perpendicular to Cw (W2), interturn distance (ITD), maximal radius of the spiral ganglion canal (GAN), fenestra cochleae area (FC), and number of turns of the cochlear canal (#T)) and added them to a collated dataset totaling 147 specimens comprising 114 taxa (Churchill et al., 2016, Mourlam & Orliac, 2017, Racicot et al., 2018, Racicot et al., 2019, Galatius et al., 2019, Viglino et al., 2021, Racicot & Preucil, 2022, Park et al., 2023, Sanks & Racicot, 2024; Sanchez‐Posada et al., 2024; Table S1) to perform a principal component analysis (PCA). The PCA can be used to distinguish general hearing abilities in artiodactyls. The analysis was performed in R statistical software, using the “FactoMineR” package (Lê et al., 2008; R Development Core Team, 2015). Any missing data (i.e., 5.74% of the dataset) were imputed using the “missMDA” package (Josse & Husson, 2016). To discuss the position of our new specimens on the first factorial plane, we computed the Euclidean distance separating them from the other taxa on the first two PCs. Some taxa in our dataset are the same individuals of specific taxa from previous analyses, so we excluded the previous measurements to investigate the results using our novel measurements. We also ran the analysis with all specimens included, that is, previous measurements of these taxa plus our new measurements, for informational purposes (Figure S1).
To quantify the differences between the NBHF cochlear morphology (list of known NBHF taxa in Table S2) and that of the three other groups highlighted by the PCA (i.e., modern non‐NBHF odontocetes [BB, FM, and MP echolocators], modern mysticetes, and land artiodactyls), we performed “Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney” tests on the nine cochlear parameters, and on the following six measurement ratios: axial pitch (i.e., height of the cochlea divided by the number of turns of the cochlear canal), basal ratio (i.e., height of the cochlea divided by the maximum width of the basal turn), and cochlear slope (i.e., height of the cochlea divided by the length of the cochlear canal, together divided by the number of turns of the cochlear canal) defined by Ketten and Wartzok (1990), and the interturn distance ratio (i.e., interturn distance sensu Ekdale and Racicot (2015) divided by the maximum width of the basal turn [Itdr]), secondary bony lamina extension (i.e., secondary bony lamina length expressed as a percentage of the cochlear canal length [SBLr]), and the relative size of the spiral ganglion canal diameter (i.e., diameter of the spiral ganglion expressed as a percentage of the area of the fenestra cochleae [GANr]) as defined by Mourlam and Orliac (2017).
To estimate the auditory abilities of extinct odontocetes as well as of two extant taxa for which the type of echolocation is still poorly known (e.g., Leucopleurus acutus [sensu Vollmer et al., 2019] and Phocoena dioptrica; see for instance Vollmer et al., 2019 and Giardino et al., 2019); five proxies were explored based on two complementary multivariate statistics methods commonly used in morphometrics (int. al. Martínez & Kak, 2001; Claude, 2008; Dickson et al., 2017); the principal component analysis (PCA) and linear discriminant analysis (LDA). First, based on the first factorial plane of the PCA, whether a taxon belonged to the morphospace of NBHF taxa or that of non‐NBHF odontocetes was assessed using the R packages “sp” and “sf” (Pebesma & Bivand, 2005, 2023). The affiliation to morphospace was also computed for the first factorial space of the PCA and LDA (see below) using the R package “alphashape3d” (Lafarge et al., 2014). Then, the Mahalanobis distances (i.e., distance between two points in a multivariate space; Mahalanobis, 1936) were calculated on the first five PCs to find the nearest group centroid of a taxon. In addition, an LDA was performed to calculate the posterior probability of belonging to a group (i.e., extant NBHF odontocetes, extant non‐NBHF odontocetes, extant mysticetes, and extant terrestrial taxa), following the protocol of Ginot et al. (2016) and Boivin et al. (2018), using the R package “MASS” (Venables & Ripley, 2002). To summarize these observations, we defined a confidence index ranging from 0 to 5, where 5 corresponds to the attribution of a taxon to the five proxies mentioned above and 0 corresponds to no attribution. If a taxon scores less than 2, the attribution is not considered acceptable. To put these observations in their phylogenetic context, we also generated a composite phylogenetic tree at the Odontoceti scale based on several studies (int. al., Tanaka & Ichishima, 2016; McGowen et al., 2020; Ben Chehida et al., 2020; Viglino et al., 2021; Vélez‐Juarbe, 2023). This tree includes all the odontocete specimens from our dataset except the Odontoceti incertae sedis (NCMS 16207) for which, as the name suggests, there is too much uncertainty. The first and last appearance dates of the taxa were mostly sourced from The Paleobiology Database (paleobiodb.org). We generated the time‐calibrated tree and mapped our observations using the R packages “ape” (Paradis et al., 2004), “phytools” (Revell, 2012), and “strap” (Bell & Lloyd, 2015; Table S3).
For additional comparative purposes, we measured the semicircular canal deviations from 90 degrees, or 90var (Malinzak et al., 2012), which is calculated by averaging the absolute values of the difference between each canal angle and 90 degrees. The angles for each canal were measured following previous work (Ekdale, 2010, 2016; Ekdale & Racicot, 2015), by determining planes using the “fit to points” tool in Avizo, where points were selected at either end of the slender portion of each canal and a third point near the midpoint of the canal. The 90var calculation is independent of body mass and therefore may be more appropriate than other semicircular canal measurements for comparisons across whales and fossils for which body mass estimates are difficult to determine. The 90var is inversely correlated with head movement speed such that a lower value will be associated with increased sensitivity to head movement. In whales, this can be associated with coordinating feeding (e.g., in bulk filter feeders) and/or agility required for different habitats or lifestyles. All measurements were taken three times and averaged for consistency.

## RESULTS

## Principal component analysis
Unlike the other Zygorhiza specimens from previous analyses, Zygorhiza kochii (ALMNH 2000 1.2.1) from our novel dataset plots just outside, on the edge of the mysticete morphospace (Figure 1). It is located extremely close to the mysticete (possible balaenid) fossil Peripolocetus vexillifer, with a Euclidean distance (Ed) between them of only 0.03 (Table S4). For comparison, the second closest taxon to Z. kochii (ALMNH 2000 1.2.1) is another mysticete, Piscobalaena nana (Ed = 0.26), and overall, it is closer to the hippopotamid Choeropsis liberiensis (Ed = 0.63) than it is to the nearest basilosaurid (cf. Cynthiacetus, Ed = 0.79). The other two Zygorhiza are 0.85 (USNM PAL 214433) and 1.16 (USNM 4748) apart from it. The cf. Aetiocetus specimen (toothed mysticete) is nested within mysticete morphospace, close to the left‐hand side, on the edge (but outside) of the basilosaurid morphospace (Figure 1). In this aspect, cf. Aetiocetus is actually closer to two basilosaurids (cf. Cynthiacetus, Ed = 0.26; Zygorhiza kochii (USNM PAL 214433), Ed = 0.34), than it is to the nearest mysticetes (Piscobalaena nana, Ed = 0.47; Peripolocetus vexillifer, Ed = 0.51).

## Comparison of bony labyrinth morphology
Zygorhiza kochii has the longest cochlear spiral and largest number of turns of the specimens in our novel dataset (Figure 2; Table S1). Second to this is the cf. Aetiocetus specimen, similar in overall structure to other baleen whales. In anterior views, all odontocetes have “flattened” cochlear spirals reflecting their shorter cochlear lengths and fewer number of turns in contrast with baleen whales and Zygorhiza (Figure 2). Although not clearly within the extant NBHF morphospace, Scaphokogia cochlearis has similar morphology to other kogiids as well as to NBHF‐type cochleae, with a dorsoventrally flattened coil and somewhat narrow perpendicular width (W2, Table S1).
Representative views of the digital bony labyrinths of taxa from this study at the tips of a phylogeny (modified from Viglino et al., 2021). Specimens are either from the left side of the body or horizontally “mirrored” (as indicated) to enable ready visual comparison across specimens.
A dorsal expansion/extension of the basal turn of the cochlea known as the tympanal recess is observed in Xiphiacetus bossi, Scaphokogia cochlearis, Squalodon calvertensis, and cf. Aetiocetus (Figure 2). Although the function of the tympanal recess is unclear, it may serve as a “vibroacoustic duct mechanism” for sound transmission within the cochlea (March et al., 2016; Park, Fitzgerald, & Evans, 2017).
Regarding the 90var values calculated for the taxa from our study, Echovenator sandersi has the lowest value (5.63) indicating a high level of head movement, followed closely by Squalodon calvertensis (7.1), Scaphokogia cochlearis (9.18), and the Odontoceti incertae sedis specimen (10.07). The highest value was calculated for one of the Schizodelphis longirostris specimens (left labyrinth 15.20, although the right labyrinth is 12.77), followed by the cf. Aetiocetus specimen (15.12). The range found among the remaining specimens, between 10.07 and 15.20, is within the range calculated for other cetaceans (4.6–16.2; Ekdale & Racicot, 2015; Ekdale, 2016; Table S5).

## On identifying the cochlear morphology of the NBHF taxa
To ensure the most accurate determination of hearing in extinct odontocetes, it is essential to first define the morphological characteristics of the cochlea of the known NBHF taxa. Based on the first factorial plane of the PCA (Figure 1), the morphospace of NBHF taxa is clearly distinct from that of the non‐NBHF odontocetes. Indeed, NBHF taxa mainly occupy the third quadrant (i.e., PC1 and PC2 negative), whereas the non‐NBHF taxa are mainly found in the fourth quadrant (i.e., PC1 positive and PC2 negative). The separation between these two groups mainly occurs along PC1 (58.33% of variance; Table S6); the principal positive drivers of variation are the cochlear widths (Cw, ctr = 17.24%; W2, ctr = 17.05%) and the cochlear canal length (Cl, ctr = 16.62%). In addition, on the first factorial space (Figure S2), the two odontocete morphospaces remain separated from each other.
Based on the first plane of the LDA (Figure 3; Table S7), the morphospace of the NBHF group is not fully separated from that of the non‐NBHF group. Indeed, 52.61% of the surface of the former overlaps with that of the latter. Nevertheless, the Jaccard index between these two groups is only 0.1, indicating a low degree of similarity between them. Furthermore, considering the space of the three first LD axes, the NBHF morphospace is then completely separated from that of the non‐NBHF group (Figure S3).

To quantify the morphological differences highlighted by the PCA, we performed several “Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney” tests on the nine cochlear parameters of this study, along with six different ratios (Figure 4; Table S8). Compared to the non‐NBHF taxa, the NBHF taxa differ mainly (and significantly) in terms of external morphology (Figure 5). Thus, NBHF taxa tend to have smaller Cl (W = 4; p < 0.001), Ch (W = 30; p < 0.001), Cw (W = 0; p < 0.001), W2 (W = 16.5; p < 0.001), FC (W = 41; p < 0.001), and number of turns (W = 166.5; p = 0.007). Concerning the more internal morphology, it is interesting to note that there is no significant difference in terms of the extension of the SBL (Table S8). However, it is noteworthy that NBHF taxa present the peculiarity of having a larger relative size of the spiral ganglion canal diameter (W = 397.5; p = 0.003) and a larger cochlear slope (W = 407; p = 0.008). Compared to the mysticetes, the NBHF taxa are, unsurprisingly, significantly smaller in many aspects (e.g., Cl [W = 0; p < 0.001], Ch [W = 0; p < 0.001]). Nevertheless, there are no significant differences between these two groups in terms of interturn distance ratio and secondary bony lamina length. Furthermore, the NBHF taxa are significantly bigger in three major aspects; the SBLr (W = 144; p < 0.001), GANr (W = 54; p = 0.004), and the cochlear slope (W = 123; p = 0.002). Finally, it is interesting to note that compared to land artiodactyls, there are no significant differences in terms of their outer cochlear envelope; indeed, Cl, Ch, Cw and W2 are quite similar between the two groups (Table S8). On the other hand, NBHF taxa are significantly larger than terrestrial taxa in terms of their SBL (W = 162; p < 0.001), ITD (W = 123; p = 0.002), GAN (W = 114; p = 0.001), axial pitch (W = 153; p < 0.001), and cochlear slope (W = 152; p < 0.001).

## Auditory capabilities assignment of extinct and extant odontocetes
To estimate the auditory capabilities of 43 odontocete taxa, we investigated 52 bony labyrinths in light of five proxies (Table S9). Seven taxa were assigned to the non‐NBHF group, 25 to the NBHF group, and one (Haborophocaena toyoshimai) to both groups depending on the individual studied. For the remaining 10 taxa, it was not possible to determine their hearing ability due to conflicting results among different proxies and/or too low confidence index values (ci <2).
Within the Delphinidae, one extinct globicephaline (UCMP 219487) was assigned as non‐NBHF (ci = 3), while the second (UCMP 219488) could not be determined. The latter presents a shorter Mahalanobis distance (Md) toward the centroid of the NBHF group (Md = 2.47; Table S10) while having a high posterior probability (pp) of belonging to the non‐NBHF categories (pp = 0.86; Table S7). Regarding the first delphinid offshoot (Figure S4), the extant Leucopleurus acutus (sensu Vollmer et al., 2019) could not be assigned with certainty to the NBHF (ci = 1.5), and this, despite a Mahalanobis distance and a posterior probability pointing in this direction. Within the Phocoenidae, two taxa could not be determined, namely Piscolithax boreios, and an undescribed phocoenid (UCMP 128285). Additionally, two specimens of Haborophocaena toyoshimai were investigated. While the first one (SMAC 1389) was found to be NBHF (right cochlea, ci = 2; left cochlea, ci = 3), it is interesting to note that the second one (HMNH 110–1) belongs to the non‐NBHF (ci = 5). Concerning the other phocoenids investigated, they are all found as NBHF (Table S9), including the extant Phocoena dioptrica (ci = 5). To conclude with the delphinidans, the two stem taxa, Kentriodon pernix and Brevirostrodelphis dividum, were also assigned as NBHF (both ci = 3). Within the Inioidea, as opposed to the present‐day Inia and Lipotes, the four extinct taxa are all found as part of the NBHF group, as is the case for the extant Pontoporia. Within the Physeteroidea, Scaphokogia cochlearis could not be assigned to a group. Despite the fact that it has a very high posterior probability of belonging to the NBHF (pp = 0.99), its confidence index is only 1. Furthermore, in terms of Mahalanobis distance, Scaphokogia cochlearis is closer to the archaeocete's centroid (Md = 7.21) than to the odontocete's centroid (Md = 10.22). Concerning the other physeteroids and pan‐physeteroids of this study, they are all found as non‐NBHF taxa, including the extinct kogiid (LACM 37258). Romaleodelphis, the sister taxon to the clade containing the physeteroids, ziphiids, inioids and the delphinidans has been assigned to the NBHF (left cochlea, ci = 3; right cochlea, ci = 4). Within the platanistoids, only their first offshoot, represented here by Zarhinocetus, belongs to the NBHF (ci = 2). Concerning the other platanistoids, Phocageneus (ci = 5), Notocetus vanbenedeni (AMNH‐FM 29026; ci = 4), and one species of Zarhachis (AMNH‐FM 145500; ci = 4) were assigned to the non‐NBHF category. The other two species of Zarhachis (NHMUK‐M 15789; USNM 13768), as well as one individual of Notocetus vanbenedeni (MPEF‐PV 1804), could not be successfully attributed. On the eurhinodelphinid side, Schizodelphis longirostris and Xiphiacetus bossi from our novel dataset, along with Xiphiacetus cristatus, are all found to be NBHF (ci = 3). Leaving the crown group, the first taxon of our sample is Squalodon calvertensis. Both specimens are found to be NBHF. Concerning Prosqualodon australis, no attribution could be made. Whereas it belongs to the non‐NBHF morphospace on the first factorial plane of the PCA and it is closer to the centroid of the non‐NBHF (Md = 10.20) than that of the NBHF (Md = 13.15) in terms of Mahalanobis distance, it was also assigned to the NBHF (pp = 0.82) in light of the LDA. Within the waipatiid dolphins, the three taxa of our study, along with Aondelphis talen, have been attributed to the NBHF group. Then, within the two first odontocete offshoots, Xenorophidae and Simocetidae, the three specimens could not be assigned to a specific group. Interestingly, the simocetid (CCNHM 1000) is closer to the centroid of terrestrial artiodactyls (Md = 2.27) than it is to that of odontocetes (Md = 15.61). To finish, the Odontoceti incertae sedis specimen (NCMS 16207) belongs to the NBHF (ci = 3).

## DISCUSSION
To more clearly see the origin and emergence of the extraordinary hearing abilities of cetaceans, we explored the bony labyrinth morphology from a novel dataset of eight specimens including the eurhinodelphinid Xiphiacetus bossi, the early mysticete cf. Aetiocetus, and a new specimen of the basilosaurid Zygorhiza kochii. We additionally take a first step in quantitatively defining NBHF‐type cochlear morphology.
The cochlear morphologies of Zygorhiza kochii and cf. Aetiocetus correspond in part with those expected for hearing ranges in the lower frequency or infrasonic spectrum. Indeed, although these two specimens are quite far from the morphospace of modern mysticetes (Figure 1), they present several characteristics pointing in that direction, including a large cochlear height (Ch) and a low relative size of their spiral ganglion canal diameter (GANr). Thus, the potential ability of Zygorhiza & cf. Aetiocetus to hear low‐frequency signals provides further evidence for the hypothesis that low‐frequency hearing may be the ancestral condition for hearing in Neoceti. Lower frequencies travel longer distances underwater, and thus, their use was likely beneficial during the early evolution of the group (Ekdale & Racicot, 2015).
The platanistid Zarhachis flagellator is found somewhere between the morphospaces of the NBHF and the non‐NBHF odontocetes (Figure 1). Based on our analyses, it displays a number of affinities with the NBHF taxa, but with a degree of confidence too low to be considered as such. Furthermore, other specimens from the genus Zarhachis (Viglino et al., 2021), along with the platanistoids Notocetus vanbenedeni and Phocageneus sp., are found within the morphospace of the non‐NBHF odontocetes. In fact, most of them belong to that group with a high confidence index. In the present day, Platanista gangetica uses unique short‐range echolocation signals with much lower source levels and centroid frequencies than marine delphinids of similar size (Jensen et al., 2013). It is possible that Zarhachis and the other platanistoids studied here faced similar challenges and had to hear similar signals to Platanista. At the base of this group (Figure S4), Zarhinocetus errabundus is probably the exception and is found to be NBHF with a rather low confidence index.
The stem odontocete and archaic delphinidans that we added to the existing dataset all grouped well within or just beyond the perimeter of the NBHF morphospace. It should be noted that the NBHF morphospace is defined by known NBHF hearers, and so some extinct NBHF taxa may fall outside of the extant morphospace. For example, the extinct specimens of Kentriodon pernix and Squalodon calvertensis plot to the left outside of the known extant NBHF species and can reasonably be considered to have had similar higher frequency hearing abilities to NBHF species based on our analyses. Of additional interest are the eurhinodelphinids, similar to Xiphiacetus cristatus (Lambert et al., 2022). They also plotted well within the extant NBHF morphospace and are considered as such with a confidence index of 3, which suggests that the group as a whole may have had the ability to hear these higher frequency signals (Figure S4). Similarly, Aondelphis talen and the waipatiids, along with an unnamed Oligocene odontocete, are all found to be NBHF.
The discovery of different members of the Eurhinodelphinidae and early delphinidans possessing the ability to hear NBHF‐type signals provides additional support for convergent evolution of this feature within different groups. However, several Oligocene odontocetes and the early‐diverging squalodontid Squalodon calvertensis also have this feature, which may indicate that it is an ancestral characteristic of at least delphinidans, if not earlier diverging odontocetes, and that expansion by other delphinidans into other hearing modes may be considered more recent innovations. In some phylogenetic estimations, the waipatiids Awamokoa tokarahi, Waipatia maerewhenua and Otekaikea huata are reconstructed along the stem leading to crown odontocetes (e.g., Coste et al., 2023; Velez‐Juarbe 2023), branching prior to the evolution of Squalodon calvertensis, which would indicate an incredibly early evolution of the NBHF hearing sensitivity. Although additional taxa should doubtless be examined, the present data are consistent with a model whereby hearing within the NBHF spectrum may be ancestral for odontocetes more closely related to the crown group, and potentially an ancient adaptation. Macroraptorial predators have been hypothesized to have driven the evolution of this feature in representatives of the modern taxa possessing NBHF (e.g., Kyhn et al., 2010). This is supported by the fossil record with the earliest recognizable fossil representatives of each of the modern groups that use NBHF signals (Galatius et al., 2019); however, this work was limited to known NBHF clades and their extinct relatives. Our investigation of earlier‐diverging odontocetes now provides evidence for an even earlier origin of this trait, as early as the Oligocene. To test the hypothesis that NBHF may be an ancestral condition, rather than convergently evolving multiple times early in the evolution of extant groups, ancestral character state reconstructions could be performed with existing data and a larger tree including all known extant NBHF taxa (i.e., all Cephalorhynchus spp. and Sagmatias spp. [sensu Vollmer et al., 2019]), all known kogiids from the fossil record, platanistoids, and eurhinodelphinids, and other early‐diverging taxa. Additional comparisons and inclusion of other macroraptorial cetacean taxa that coexisted with the archaic delphinidans of our study should also be performed.
In summary, analyses of the anatomy and morphospaces of our novel dataset of cetacean inner ears lends additional support for hearing sensitivities in the lower frequency or infrasonic spectrum in the archaeocete Zygorhiza kochii and an early toothed mysticete cf. Aetiocetus. We also highlight that, among modern odontocetes, NBHF taxa have a particular cochlear morphology with a smaller cochlear canal, a reduced number of turns, a greater cochlear slope, and a larger relative size of the spiral ganglion canal diameter. Thus, we provide new data in support of NBHF hearing sensitivity in a broader sampling of odontocetes; of particular interest, an unnamed Oligocene odontocete has morphology corresponding with that of NBHF‐type hearers. With regard to modern taxa whose hearing remains poorly understood or unknown, we were able to confirm that the spectacled porpoise Phocoena dioptrica belongs to the NBHF odontocetes. Furthermore, at the base of the Delphinidae (Figure S4), Leucopleurus acutus (sensu Vollmer et al., 2019) also shows affinities with the NBHF taxa, but with too low of a confidence index to be considered as such. We further find additional support for NBHF sensitivity in two eurhinodelphinids (Xiphiacetus bossi and Schizodelphis longirostris), supporting previous work (Lambert et al., 2022) and suggesting that eurhinodelphinids as a clade may have been sensitive to NBHF signals. Our results add to the growing body of knowledge surrounding the evolution of cetacean hearing based on μCT scans, and, intriguingly, suggest an early evolution of the NBHF type of hearing. The ability to hear NBHF‐type signals may turn out to be less a unique feature of certain extant species, but rather the ancestral condition from which other odontocetes were able to adapt to different ecologies.
