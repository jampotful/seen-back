Predicting ecology and hearing sensitivities in Parapontoporia —An extinct long‐snouted dolphin
Joyce Sanks, Rachel A. Racicot (2024). The Anatomical Record. https://doi.org/10.1002/ar.25538. doi:10.1002/ar.25538. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Predicting ecology and hearing sensitivities in Parapontoporia—An extinct long‐snouted dolphin

## Abstract
AbstractAnalyses of the cetacean (whale and dolphin) inner ear provide glimpses into the ecology and evolution of extinct and extant groups. The paleoecology of the long‐snouted odontocete (toothed whale) group, Parapontoporia, is primarily marine with its depositional context also suggesting freshwater tolerance. As an extinct relative of the exclusively riverine Lipotes vexillifer, Parapontoporia provides insight into a transition from marine to freshwater environments. High‐resolution X‐ray CT scans (~3 microns or less) of three individual specimens from two species, P. sternbergi and P. pacifica, were acquired. Digital endocasts of the inner ear labyrinths were extracted non‐destructively. Nine measurements of the inner ear were compared with an existing dataset covering 125 terrestrial and aquatic artiodactyls. These measurements were then subjected to a principal component analysis to interpret hearing sensitivities among other artiodactyls. Based on our analyses, Parapontoporia was likely to have been able to hear within narrow‐band high frequency (NBHF) ranges. This finding indicates another convergence of NBHF‐style hearing, or, more intriguingly, suggests that it may be an ancestral characteristic present among the longirostrine dolphins that dominated in the Miocene prior to the evolution of more modern lineages.

## INTRODUCTION
Cetaceans (whales, dolphins, and porpoises) embody one of the most dramatic transitions in mammalian history: the return to water from land. Consequently, this group acquired a series of aquatic adaptations, including movement of the nostrils to the top of the head, and streamlining of the body (Uhen, 2010; Berta et al., 2014). Echolocation evolved early in the evolutionary history of toothed whales (Odontoceti), in the Oligocene, and all modern odontocetes echolocate (Geisler et al., 2014; Park et al., 2016; Racicot et al., 2019). The biosonar clicks of most extant odontocetes typically encompass a broad frequency spectrum, ranging from tens of kilohertz up to 150–170 kHz. Conversely, certain odontocetes exhibit distinctive biosonar clicks that are narrow in bandwidth but have a high centroid frequency (NBHF). These unique clicks have peak frequencies ranging from 125 to 140 kHz and bandwidths of 11–20 kHz (Galatius et al., 2019).
Long after transitioning from land to water, odontocetes repeatedly secondarily entered riverine and estuarine systems from the ocean (Hamilton et al., 2001). Extant members of this polyphyletic group of cetaceans, known as “river dolphins,” include Inia geoffrensis (Amazon river dolphin), Pontoporia blainvillei (La Plata dolphin), Lipotes vexillifer (Chinese river dolphin or Baiji), and Platanista gangetica (South Asian river dolphin). Odontocete genera that are not included in the traditional classification of “river dolphins” include the Irrawaddy dolphin (Orcaella brevirostris), the Tucuxi dolphin (Sotalia fluviatilis), and the Yangtze finless porpoise (Neophocaena asiaeorientalis). We focus on the traditional classification of “river dolphins” that convergently evolved unique morphological characteristics such as an elongated rostrum (longirostrine morphology) (Hamilton et al., 2001; Page & Cooper, 2017). In riverine habitats, odontocetes have biosonar systems featuring low energy and high directionality (e.g., Ladegaard et al., 2015). These characteristics simplify the auditory environment, making detecting and discerning targets in complex, spatially heterogeneous settings easier. Conversely, similarly sized marine odontocetes operate their biosonar systems at higher amplitude and lower sampling rates (e.g., Ladegaard et al., 2015).
Extant “river dolphins” are noteworthy for having the longest snouts, or rostra, relative to body size, of all extant odontocetes. However, an even more pronounced elongation of the rostrum is found within some odontocete fossils, known as hyperlongirostry (McCurry & Pyenson, 2019). Previous literature recognized the repeated evolution of hyper‐longirostrine morphology in odontocete fossils and suggested ecological convergence for feeding to swipe and stun prey with the rostrum (McCurry & Pyenson, 2019).
Parapontoporia is one of several genera of hyperlongirostrine odontocetes that arose in the middle Miocene, including Pomatodelphis, Xiphiacetus, and Zarhachis (McCurry & Pyenson, 2019). Barnes (1984, 1985) provided the first comprehensive description of Parapontoporia based on cranial and mandibular remains, establishing its taxonomic status and highlighting its morphological similarities with the Pontoporiidae. However, later combined morphological and genetic analyses instead identified Parapontoporia as a member of the Lipotidae, a family of odontocetes including the recently extinct Baiji (Lipotes vexillifer), or Yangtze river dolphin (Boessenecker, 2013; Boessenecker & Poust, 2015; Geisler et al., 2011; Uhen, 2008; Figure 1). Barnes (1984) also suggested three species of Parapontoporia including P. sternbergi, P. pacifica, and P. wilsoni. However, the status of P. wilsoni as an independent species remains uncertain due to limited available data from Miocene‐aged fossil specimens (Borce, 2018).

Fossils of Parapontoporia have primarily been found in late Miocene and Pliocene North Pacific marine shelf sediments (Barnes, 1984; Boessenecker, 2013). Later identification of a specimen of Parapontoporia within the upper Pliocene nonmarine Tulare Formation of California suggests a notable environmental shift, indicating its ability to inhabit freshwater or brackish conditions, unlike its marine‐dwelling ancestors (Boessenecker & Poust, 2015; Figure 2).

Previous studies have established the utility of inner ear morphology in assessing hearing and echolocation capabilities, and these traits have been empirically linked to ecological factors and behavioral patterns (Ekdale, 2013; Ekdale, 2015; Ekdale & Racicot, 2015; Galatius et al., 2019; Park et al., 2019; Racicot, 2022). Whereas previous studies analyzed the cranial anatomy of Parapontoporia in correlation with ecology and behavior (Borce, 2018; McCurry & Pyenson, 2019), the bony labyrinth morphology of this genus has not yet been investigated. In the absence of audiograms, analysis of the inner ear structure offers a means of studying hearing in extinct species and for reconstructing the evolutionary history of hearing and potential habitat in odontocetes. Analysis of the hearing‐related anatomy of Parapontoporia may inform on the habitat‐mediated evolution of echolocation, feeding mode, and cranial morphology.
Given the pivotal role of habitat in the evolution of echolocation among “river dolphins,” Parapontoporia emerges as an ideal subject for investigation of the influence of ecology, morphology, and behavior, especially when considering its depositional context that infers its habitat as both marine and riverine (Barnes, 1985; Boessenecker & Poust, 2015). Our investigation thus provides insights into the evolutionary origins of the shared features observed in the inner ear morphology and auditory capabilities of Parapontoporia in comparison to both extant freshwater “river dolphins” and extinct oceanic hyperlongirostrine dolphins. The significance of Parapontoporia lies in its position at the threshold, representing a mostly marine taxon that briefly transitioned to freshwater habitats in parallel with Lipotes, and more distantly, Platanista and members of the Iniidae.
We use microCT to examine and quantify the bony labyrinth morphology of Parapontoporia, with the primary goal to investigate its hearing abilities. Members of the Parapontoporia genus have a unique habitat signal (living in both riverine and marine environments) congruent with both longirostrine “river dolphins” and marine long‐snouted dolphins. As Parapontoporia is a relative of the extant (functionally extinct) “river dolphin” genus Lipotes, analysis of the inner ear morphology of Parapontoporia contributes to a greater understanding of echolocating cetaceans, especially those considered ecologically vulnerable.

## METHODS

## Data acquisition
Three fossil specimens from the genus Parapontoporia, two right P. sternbergi (SDSNH 24734 and 24437) periotics and one left P. pacifica periotic (SDSNH 53169), were loaned from the San Diego Museum of Natural History (SDSNH) and microCT scanned. MicroCT scans were conducted at Vanderbilt University using the North Star Imaging microCT scanner in the Department of Earth and Environmental Sciences (for scanning parameters for each specimen see Table S1). ## Segmentation and endocast generation
Endocasts of the inner ear regions (bony labyrinth) were individually segmented using VGStudioMax v.3.3. After setting an initial tolerance value, we created a region of interest (ROI), and tolerance adjustments were made iteratively depending on internal structures and matrix infill to ensure accurate segmentation. We used a combination of the ROI selection tools in both 2D and 3D modes.

## Measurements
Nine measurement parameters of the cochlea have been associated with high frequency hearing abilities in artiodactyls (Churchill et al., 2016; Galatius et al., 2019; Mourlam & Orliac, 2017; Park et al., 2023; Racicot et al., 2018; Racicot et al., 2019; Racicot & Preucil, 2022; Table S2, Figure 3); we obtained the same measurements following protocols from this previous work. For measurements obtained in VGStudioMax software, the “poly‐line length” tool was used as these can directly make curved measurements as well as straight linear measurements. Linear measurements on cross sections of the cochlea were obtained in Avizo v. 2021.2 after importing TIFF stacks of the segmented volumes. The “slice” tool in Avizo was used to obtain accurate nonorthogonal slice planes for measurements to be made directly on the slices at the correct portion of the cochlea (e.g., first quarter‐turn) using the “fit to points” option.
Illustration of key measurements on endocast of P. sternbergi (SDSNH 53169). [Correction added after first online publication on 08 August 2024. The specimen number changed from “SDSNH 53619” to “SDSNH 53169”.]
For measurements of cochlear length (Cl), landmarks were added sequentially through the lumen of the cochlea, along the center of the laminar gap, where visible, and through the center of the lumen when it was not, or no longer, visible reaching the apex. The secondary bony lamina length (SBL) was measured along the outer wall of the bony labyrinth endocast from the corresponding part of the beginning of the laminar gap. The fenestra cochleae (FC) surface area was calculated using the equation for the surface area of an ellipse or circle, depending on the closest shape of the aperture, from the average radius for measured height and width of the aperture. Cochlear width (Cw) measurements were acquired by first marking three points (using “fit to points” in Avizo) on the 3D model of the cochlea to obtain the correct slice orientation, then obtaining the linear measurement at the widest points of the cochlea. Subsequently, for the cochlear width perpendicular to Cw (W2), a slice was determined by placing landmarks using “fit to points” and adjusting the orientation to be perpendicular to the slice for Cw. Then W2 was measured at the widest points of the cochlea in this slice. Cochlear height (Ch) was measured from the apex to the dorsal edge of the first basal turn. The spiral ganglion canal width at the first quarter turn (GAN) was not visible in any of the specimens, and therefore not measured. Inter‐turn distance (ITD), a measure of the thickness of the wall separating successive cochlear turns, was measured at the first quarter turn. The number of cochlear turns (T) was determined using established techniques (e.g., Ekdale & Rowe, 2011; Geisler & Luo, 1996; Racicot et al., 2018; Racicot & Preucil, 2022). First, a line was drawn in VGStudioMax from the start of the laminar gap through the cochlear axis, and crossings over this line were tallied. This count was multiplied by 180 and added to the angle between the remaining apex of the cochlea and the original line. The resulting sum was then divided by 360 to determine the number of cochlear turns. All measurements were conducted three times and averaged.

## Statistical analysis
A principal component analysis (PCA) was performed after adding our measurements of three specimens to an existing dataset covering 125 terrestrial and aquatic artiodactyls (Churchill et al., 2016; Mourlam & Orliac, 2017; Park et al., 2023; Racicot et al., 2018, 2019; Racicot & Preucil, 2022) to visualize multidimensional data and define the cochlear morphospace. The PCA was performed in RStudio v.4.2.2 using the FactoMineR and FactoExtra packages (Le et al., 2008; R Development Core Team, 2014), and analysis of contributions of data to the PCA was done in FactoExtra. Missing data were imputed using the missMDA package (Josse & Husson, 2016). Missing data were minimal; among our additions, only the spiral ganglion canal (GAN) measurements were not included due to bone damage or matrix infill.

## RESULTS

## Cochlear morphology
The cochlear morphology of Parapontoporia specimens generally resembles that of a typical odontocete (Figures 4, 5, 6). The overall cochlear structure of the three specimens is similar; all of them having thin canaliculi cochleae and relatively thin and short canals leading to the fenestrae cochleae. Beginning at the fenestra cochleae, the short, wide cochlear hook of each of the three specimens resembles that of a delphinid. The cochlear hook leads into the scala tympani, which is wide at the base and narrows as it reaches the apex of the cochlea. The cochlear lengths of all specimens are well within the ranges of extant and extinct porpoises such as Salumiphocaena stocktoni, Piscolithax tedfordi, and Phocoena dioptrica. All three cochleae have approximately two turns (ranging from 2.19 to 2.21), similar in values to Orcaella brevirostris and Peponocephala electra, two members of the extant Globicephalinae (Racicot & Preucil, 2022) and relatively wide inter‐turn distances (ITD) ranging from 0.752 to 1.26 mm, similar to some porpoises and Aglaocetus (Table S2). Parapontoporia sternbergi specimen SDSNH 24734 has greater measurements than the other P. sternbergi specimen in every category except cochlear length.

## Principal component analysis
Principal component 1 (PC1) accounted for 61.13% of the variation, and principal component 2 (PC2) accounted for 20.9% of the variation. The primary variables influencing the grouping of sampled artiodactyls in morphospace include cochlear width, length, and the number of turns. Cochlear width and height primarily influence PC1, with the surface area of the fenestrae cochleae also making a notable contribution. Meanwhile, the number of cochlear turns is the primary factor affecting PC2 (Figure 7). Combining PC1 and PC2, this PCA is representative of over 82% of the variation in the morphological dataset.

Parapontoporia specimens plotted within the odontocete morphospace cluster, with one P. sternbergi SDSNH 24437 (“Pa st1”) and one P. pacifica SDSNH 53169 (“Pa pa”) specimen falling within the NBHF specialist cluster (Figure 7). Notably, one P. sternbergi (SDSNH 24437) and P. pacifica (SDSNH 53169) lie closest to two P. blainvillei specimens, whereas the other P. sternbergi (SDSNH 24734; “Pa st”) plots near odontocete fossils Numataphocoena yamashitai (“Nu ya”) and Echovenator sandersi (“Ec sa,” Figure 7).

## DISCUSSION
Our results provide insights into the auditory capabilities of Parapontoporia and shed light on its potential adaptation to both marine and riverine environments. Specifically, our analysis indicates that Parapontoporia was potentially a narrow‐band high‐frequency (NBHF) echolocator, or at least could hear within the same frequency spectrum as these species, as evidenced by the cochlear morphology of two of the specimens scanned. This suggests independent acquisition of NBHF hearing in this group and thus convergent evolution with certain other, mostly marine, cetaceans.
Two of the three Parapontoporia cochleae measured in this study plot near two specimens of P. blainvillei, which is an NBHF specialist and is the only extant river dolphin species found to inhabit saltwater estuaries and oceans as well as freshwater river systems (Crespo et al., 1998). The positioning of Parapontoporia in this area of the PCA suggests similarities in behavior or lifestyle with Pontoporia, which might account for the morphological similarities in the teeth, rostrum, and mandible observed by Barnes (1984) that led to his inference of affinity with Pontoporia. Given adaptations of Pontoporia to both saltwater and freshwater environments, it is plausible that Parapontoporia would have shared similar ecological and behavioral traits. Further, the geological evidence supports inference that Parapontoporia inhabited estuarine and marine environments, reinforcing the link between its cochlear morphology and habitat. Like Pontoporia, Parapontoporia may have evolved to inhabit estuarine and marine environments.
Recent work lends added context to these results and suggests different drivers for the evolution of NHBF than have been proposed in the past. Specifically, a recently described member of the Eurhinodelphinidae, Xiphiacetus cristatus, was discovered to plot within the NBHF morphospace (Lambert et al., 2023). Together with the data presented here, this suggests that NBHF could represent an ancestral mechanism for effective echolocation in transitional habitats.
Previous work (Morisaka & Connor, 2007) suggested that NBHF signals may have convergently evolved as a form of “acoustic crypsis” that would have helped species evade detection by macropredators. In the present day, species possessing NBHF generally produce echolocation signals that cannot be heard by their predators (principally Orcinus orca). Extending this paradigm into the past, Galatius et al. (2019) showed that early‐diverging members of extant NBHF clades (e.g., phocoenids, kogiids, and pontoporiids) also possessed NBHF sensitivity, suggesting that this may have helped these taxa evade predation in similar fashion (in this context, from giant macroraptorial sperm whales such as Acrophyseter deinodon). Our hypothesis that NBHF may have instead evolved as a means for navigating transitional habitats does not necessarily clash with this previous model (i.e., they are not mutually exclusive), but suggests that selective pressures and/or ecological benefits may have driven an even earlier, widespread acquisition of NBHF echolocation in earlier‐diverging groups. In particular, NBHF may be especially beneficial in riverine systems where lower energy, highly directional biosonar signals enable better perception within spatially complex environments. For example, narrow‐band signals are advantageous in distinguishing small, rapidly moving prey in this acoustically “cluttered” environment (Jensen et al., 2013; Ladegaard et al., 2015). A prediction of this hypothesis is that within a robust phylogenetic framework, the ancestral nodes for hearing abilities in the earliest, usually longirostrine dolphins, will be reconstructed as within the NBHF spectrum.
Riverine species do not distinctly separate from non‐riverine species in the PCA, indicating that there is no consistent or morphologically convergent solution to hearing and echolocation in riverine environments in “river dolphins”. This is interesting in the context of the more obvious external convergent adaptations of classically defined “river dolphins,” suggesting that the inner ear could be a useful source of phylogenetic characters to help improve resolution, particularly in analyses that include fossils. However, we concede that differences in cochlear length and inter‐turn distance suggest potential variations in hearing capabilities or expected levels of individual variation. Additionally, this study only includes data for right P. sternbergi and a left P. pacifica ear, and right and left ears may exhibit differences due to cranial asymmetry and directional hearing (e.g., Coombs et al., 2020; Ekdale, 2010). Additional study should examine as much individual variation data as possible within the riverine dolphin groups.
The geologically youngest Parapontoporia specimen from our study, P. sternbergi SDSNH 24734, plots near other extinct odontocetes, Numataphocoena yamashitai (a porpoise fossil) and Echovenator sandersi (an early‐diverging Oligocene xenorophid odontocete) in the PCA. The closest extant species to plot near this specimen is P. electra (melon‐headed whale), which is a coastal, near‐shore species (Brownell Jr. et al., 2009). Additionally, SDSNH 24734 plots notably farther down and to the right than SDSNH 24437, the other P. sternbergi specimen. As the specimens were all identified by the same person (T. Deméré), it seems unlikely that they were misidentified or different species. We suggest possible explanations for the differences in placement among P. sternbergi specimens: specimen damage, intraspecific variation, and paleogeographic context. First, considering fossil damage, while we minimized the effect of the damage, measurements along the dorsal edges were potentially influenced by the morphological disparity. However, measurements unaffected by the damage, such as Ch, Cw, W2, are consistently larger than the other P. sternbergi specimen, suggesting that SDSNH 24734 has a wider and taller cochlear canal than SDSNH 24437, and is pulled farther to the right side of the morphospace as a result. Second, a previous study observed intraspecific variation in our artiodactyl dataset among monodontids (Racicot et al., 2018), which supports it as a plausible cause for the placement of the P. sternbergi specimens. However, in Martins et al. (2020), Phocoena exhibited lower levels of intraspecific variation compared to interspecific variation and is environmentally flexible, like Parapontoporia, so is perhaps a more reasonable comparison. The plotting of P. pacifica and P. sternbergi specimens closer together than the two P. sternbergi specimens suggests the influence of additional factors on the observed variation, and thus that other variables may be contributing to the observed differences in placement between the specimens. Further investigation through a comprehensive study of a larger number of individuals from different geological localities could provide valuable insights into the evolutionary dynamics and ecological adaptations of Parapontoporia. According to the collections database, the SDSNH collections contain 18 specimens that could be used towards such a project.
Lastly, it is noteworthy that SDSNH 24734 represents the geologically youngest of the three specimens, originating from the San Diego Formation during the late Pliocene to early Pleistocene. The other two specimens—SDSNH 24437 and SDSNH 53169—are from late Miocene to early Pliocene deposits. This geological context coincides with the disappearance of large coastal embayments (Pyenson, 2009; Boessenecker & Poust, 2015), a phenomenon previously suggested by Pyenson (2009) as a potential factor in the extinction of Parapontoporia before their freshwater invasion was suggested. While it remains uncertain if there exists a significant enough temporal gap between the specimens to allow for evolution of the cochlea, it prompts consideration of whether the distinct differences observed in SDSNH 24734 could be linked to the disappearance of embayments and a resulting potential transition to freshwater habitats. Although speculative, this proposition underscores the potential interactions between environmental change and inner ear morphology and acknowledges the need for further investigation. A comprehensive study of all available periotics and bony labyrinths of Parapontoporia would enable us to test for the presence of crucial connections between morphology, evolution, and environmental change.
In conclusion, our study contributes to the growing body of knowledge on cetacean bony labyrinth morphology and evolution and provides valuable insights into the auditory capabilities of Parapontoporia. Our finding that Parapontoporia hearing fell within NBHF ranges suggests a novel direction for future investigations into the sensory systems of fossil odontocetes, which may deepen our understanding of the origin(s) of NBHF hearing. Moreover, further exploration into the sensory systems of odontocetes, in conjunction with expanded analysis of the paleogeographical context of fossil groups, will be instrumental in informing the influence of habitat on cetacean hearing and elucidating the broader evolutionary dynamics among fossil and modern marine mammals.
