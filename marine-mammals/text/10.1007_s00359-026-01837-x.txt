Abstract
Single target acuity (STA) is a key measure for understanding visual object perception in harbor seals. While STA was
already determined with stationary and moving compact single targets of different contrasts in air, we herein assessed
underwater STA with the same stimuli as used for aerial testing for comparison. A two-alternative-forced-choice task was
conducted with two trained harbor seals under controlled light and turbidity conditions. Underwater STA was determined
as 0.30 deg for stationary and 0.27 deg for moving single targets, and thus matched the previously documented aerial
STA for one harbor seal. This result confirms that our test individual can resolve compact objects equally well in both
media under our experimental conditions. Underwater STA decreased when contrast was reduced, reaching 0.63 deg at
the lowest contrast condition tested. Performance for low-contrast stimuli was likely impacted by caustic effects, resulting
from the movements of the harbor seal’s head in and out of the testing basin. Additionally, as it was already found for
aerial STA, no difference between underwater STA for stationary and moving single targets was documented. Our findings contribute to the understanding of amphibious vision in harbor seals and are informative regarding the perception of
objects, such as prey fish.
Keywords Single target detection · Underwater vision · Marine mammals · Vision · Motion vision

Introduction
As for all organisms, marine mammals such as the harbor
seal (Phoca vitulina) will need to perceive biotic objects
such as predators, conspecifics, or prey under certain circumstances. In addition, harbor seals might want to perceive single abiotic objects such as landmarks e.g. for goal
localization (Maaß and Hanke 2022; Maaß et al. 2022).
Theoretically, harbor seals could rely on information from
numerous sensory modalities to perceive single objects. To
give an example, prey such as herring might be perceived
hydrodynamically or acoustically. Regarding the first, harbor seals are able to perceive and follow hydrodynamic

items, might also be perceived visually. To assess the conditions under which single objects will be visually perceived
by harbor seals, the parameter to be determined is the single
target acuity (STA). The STA determines how small single
objects can be while still perceivable for a harbor seal. Thus,
the determination of STA is informative in the mentioned
behavioral contexts.
Aerial STA for single targets with a high contrast to the
background has already been determined for harbor seals
presented with vertical lines (Jameson and Fisher 1970) and
dots (Sandow and Hanke 2024) as single objects. Jamieson and Fisher (1970) determined that harbor seals can
resolve a vertical gap of 0.03 deg between two black bars
on a white background, while Sandow and Hanke (2024)
found harbor seals can detect a black dot with a minimum
diameter of 0.27 deg on a white background. These given
visual angles refer to the angle subtended by the vertical

Journal of Comparative Physiology A

gap/dot with the smallest diameter perceivable by the harbor seals from their point of view. The difference in these
results for vertical lines and dots might be attributed to the
fact that linear versus compact objects were used in these
separate experiments. It is known from studies including
human participants that linear single objects lead to better STA measurements, meaning smaller STA values, than
compact single objects (Hecht and Mintz 1939; Hecht et al.
1947). Furthermore, differences in stimulus luminance, i.e.
the luminous light emitted by the stimuli per area, in the
two experiments might have influenced STA results. Studies
in mammals including cats and humans (see e.g. Pasternak
and Merigan 1981) have shown that stimulus luminance
affects contrast sensitivity, which is the physiological basis
of STA. Contrast sensitivity is defined as the inverse of the
minimum contrast needed to perceive a grating or a single
target. Thus, the contrast sensitivity of the eye determines
whether a single target of a certain size and contrast to the
background can be perceived or not (Amesbury and Schallhorn 2003; O’Caroll and Wiederman 2014). With regard to
previous STA studies, this means that a change in stimulus
luminance and accordingly contrast sensitivity could also
explain the differing results in harbor seals (Jamieson and
Fisher 1970; Sandow and Hanke 2024).
Jameson and Fisher (1970) additionally found aerial STA
to compare well with underwater STA in harbor seals. However, although the researchers tested STA in the two media
with the same experimental setting, it seems that the luminance of the linear stimuli was not equated in both media;
they only report a general change in stimulus luminance of
85 cd/m². In the present study, we thus decided to revisit
underwater STA in harbor seals to assess whether aerial and
underwater STA would also coincide when compact objects
would be used for STA testing, and under conditions in
which the stimuli in both media have the same luminance.
We were particularly interested in compact single objects,
in contrast to the vertical linear object presented by Jameson and Fisher (1970), as these seem to resemble many abiotic and biotic objects occurring in the harbor seal’s natural
habitat. We tested underwater STA considering changes in
contrast and stimulus condition, stationary versus moving,
to experimentally assess our hypothesis.
We hypothesized underwater STA for moving and stationary compact single targets of different contrasts would
correspond to aerial STA under the experimental conditions chosen. This hypothesis was based on (1) the results
of Sandow and Hanke (2024), which showed that motion
did not seem to affect STA in air, (2) present understanding of harbor seal vision, and (3) theoretical considerations
regarding contrast sensitivity. Prior studies of harbor seal
visual acuity indicate that their grating acuity, the ability to
resolve high contrast gratings, was very similar in air versus

13

underwater under bright light conditions (Schusterman and
Balliet 1970; Hanke and Dehnhardt 2009; Weiffen et al.
2006). In contrast, theoretical consideration of optics would
suggest an eye to be either adapted to aerial or underwater
vision, but not both. Previous studies indicated that harbor
seals are adapted to underwater vision (Johnson 1893; Walls
1942; Jamieson 1970, 1971; Hanke et al. 2006a, b; Weiffen
et al. 2006; Gläser et al. 2014). This underwater emmetropia, i.e. normal-sightedness, would in turn lead to aerial
myopia, i.e. near-sightedness, due to the refractive power
of the cornea contributing to aerial but most likely not to
underwater vision (Hanke et al. 2009). The good correspondence of aerial and underwater grating acuity in bright light
(Weiffen et al. 2006; Hanke and Dehnhardt 2009), however,
indicates that the harbor seal eye overcomes aerial myopia
most likely by the static interaction of the centrally flattened
cornea and the pupil closing as a vertical slit and finally
forming a pinhole (Walls 1942; Jamieson and Fisher 1970;
Jamieson 1971; Hanke et al. 2006a, b, 2009; Hanke and
Dehnhardt 2009).
In this study, we directly evaluated underwater STA for
comparison with prior aerial STA measurements (Sandow
and Hanke 2024) with stimuli that had a luminance comparable to the grating acuity stimuli used by Hanke and
Dehnhardt (2009) and Weiffen et al. (2006), which resulted
in a good correspondence of aerial and underwater grating
acuity. Accordingly, we conducted the STA experiments
under conditions under which we would not expect to find
a (strong) refractive difference in the two media. Consequently, contrast sensitivity, that, in general, can be affected
by refraction as shown in humans (Kebede et al. 2025;
Liou and Chiu 2001; Collins and Carney 1990; for review
see Pelli and Bex 2013), should not differ under aerial and
underwater STA testing conditions. In conclusion, we anticipated similar STA results in both media, even when reducing contrast.

Material and method
Experimental animals
The experiment was carried out at the Marine Science Center (University of Rostock, Germany) with two male adult
harbor seals identified as “Nick” (born in 1999) and “Luca”
(born in 2002). The harbor seals were kept in a mixed group
including harbor seals, California sea lions (Zalophus californianus) and a South African fur seal (Arctocephalus
pusillus). Both individuals were experienced with respect
to scientific experiments, including studies on vision and
orientation (e.g. Mauck et al. 2008; Maaß et al. 2022; Sandow et al. 2025), and had successfully participated in the

Journal of Comparative Physiology A

aerial STA experiment (Sandow and Hanke 2024). The harbor seals obtained most of their daily diet during the experimental sessions. The rest of the food was fed during general
training sessions including medical and playful training. In
total, the harbor seals received 1–4 kg fish a day (herring,
Clupea harengus or sprats, Sprattus sprattus), depending on
the motivation of the individual on a particular day or period
of time as well as on season.
The experiments carried out in this study were in accordance with the European Communities Council Directive
of September 22nd, 2010, (2010/63/EU) and the German
Animal Welfare Act of 2006 and the individuals included in
the study were not subjected to pain, suffering or injury as
approved by local authorities (State Office for Agriculture,
Food Safety and Fisheries).

Experimental setup
To assure a constant ambient illumination of 79 lx, similar to that during the aerial STA study (Sandow and Hanke
2024), we installed the experimental setup in an experimental chamber (3 m deep, 2 m wide, 2 m high) on a floating
platform. The ambient illumination was measured in air
using a Multifunctional Environment Measuring Instrument
4 in 1 (VOLTCRAFT, Hirschau, Germany) at the position
where the harbor seals’ eyes were during the experiment.
To assess STA under water, a Perspex basin was installed
in the experimental chamber (Fig. 1) and filled with clear
tap-water resulting in a turbidity never exceeding 0.8 FNU
(formazin nephelometric unit) during all sessions. Turbidity
was measured before every session using the TSS Portable
handheld measurement instrument for turbidity/solids (Hach
Company/Hach Lange GmbH, Düsseldorf, Germany).
During sessions, the harbor seal had to rest comfortably
on a raised platform and had to lower its head into the waterfilled Perspex basin (Fig. 1). In the Perspex basin, the harbor seal placed its muzzle on a stationing target installed
on the bottom of the basin. Due to its large dorsal visual
field (Hanke et al. 2006a, b), the harbor seal was able to
look at the stimuli (Fig. 1c) being presented on a monitor
(LG 23MB35PY, 23’’ LED-Display, resolution: 1920 × 1080
pixel, contrast: 5.000.000:1, response time: 5 ms, refresh
rate: 60 Hz, LG Electronics Deutschland GmbH, Eschborn,
Germany) that was placed directly to the front side of the
Perspex basin from outside and had a distance of 60 cm to
its eyes (Fig. 1a). A target was installed on the right as well
as on the left wall of the Perspex basin serving as response
targets (Fig. 1).
To prevent secondary cues of the experimenter from
influencing the response behavior of the harbor seal during experiments, the experimenter stayed in a separate
observation room adjacent to the experimental chamber.

From this room, stimulus presentation could be controlled,
and the behavior of the harbor seal could be observed via
a USB camera (Logitech C270 HD Webcam, max. resolution: 720p, diagonal field of view: 55°, Logitech GmbH,
München-Aubing, Germany). To reward the harbor seal
after correct choices, observation room and experimental
chamber were linked to each other by a window that could
be opened and closed with an opaque slider (Fig. 1a).

Luminance measurement of caustics
To account for water movements caused by the harbor seal’s
head moving in and out of the Perspex basin and resulting
small fluctuations in luminance due to reflection and refraction of light at the water surface, called “caustics”, we measured the luminance fluctuations occurring in the visual
field of the harbor seal during experimental sessions. We
manually created waves mimicking the immersion of a harbor seal head into the tap water-filled Perspex basin. These
luminance fluctuations were measured through the tap
water-filled Perspex basin with a custom-made light meter
which was based on the ambient light sensor EVL ALSPDIC144 (Everlight Electronics Europe GmbH, Karlsruhe,
Germany); please note that the luminance meter (LS-110,
Minolta, Langenhagen, Germany) we used for measuring
the luminance of the stimuli (see Stimuli) could not be used
as it only measures luminance under static conditions. During measurements, a white screen with a luminance of 80
cd/m², which corresponds to the luminance of the background of the underwater STA stimuli, was presented and
served as standard to allow the baseline fluctuations to be
measured. A hand-held oscilloscope (ZT-703 S ZOTEK
INSTRUMENTS CO., LTD) was used to plot the dynamic
luminance over time. The sensor output was then calibrated
against the static luminance meter (LS-110, Minolta, Langenhagen, Germany). Altogether maximal caustic luminance fluctuations of ± 24 cd/m² occurred for a background
luminance of 80 cd/m² (see. Supplement Part 1).

the associated Psychophysics Toolbox 3.0 (Brainard 1997;
Pelli 1997; Kleiner et al. 2007). In a two-alternative-forcedchoice behavioral experiment, in every single trial, we presented a circular single target with a square wave luminance
profile on a white background on one side of the monitor.
The other side of the monitor remained homogenously
white (Fig. 1b).

General experimental procedure
At the start of each experimental session, the harbor seal
was guided into the experimental chamber and onto the
platform (Fig. 1). Then, the experimenter closed the door of
the chamber and went into the adjacent observation room.
The experimenter cued the harbor seal to lower its head to
the hoop station in the water-filled Perspex basin, and then
closed the black, opaque slider between chamber and observation room. After confirming via the camera image that the
harbor seal was stationed correctly and was paying attention to the monitor, the experimenter started the first trial by
presenting the single target on one side of the monitor (see
example in Fig. 1b). In the two-alternative-forced-choice
task, the harbor seal indicated, on which of the two sides of
the monitor the single target appeared by moving its head

13

Journal of Comparative Physiology A

from the station and touching the response target on the corresponding side with its snout. Correct responses were signaled by a whistle, the secondary reinforcer, and rewarded
with fish, the primary reinforcer, by passing it through the
observation window (Fig. 1), while the monitor turned grey
again. Afterwards the harbor seal was directed back into the
station to wait for the next trial to start. If the harbor seal
answered incorrectly, the experimenter said “no”, the slider
remained closed, and no reward was given; the harbor seal
went back to its station by itself, while the monitor turned
grey.

Training procedure for basic task acquisition
During basic task acquisition, the harbor seal was presented
with a high contrast C1 single target with a diameter of 1.94
deg of visual angle. One session consisted of 30 trials presenting the single target 15 times on the left and 15 times on
the right side of the monitor in a pseudo-randomized order
after Gellermann (1933). Basic task acquisition was finished
when the harbor seal reached the learning criterion, defined
as a performance of ≥ 80% correct choices in two consecutive sessions.

Testing procedure for STA threshold determination
STA threshold determination was started after basic task
acquisition. STA thresholds for the three contrast conditions,
high C1, intermediate C3 and low C4, and the two stimulus
conditions, stationary versus moving, were determined in
this sequence: stationary STA for the highest contrast C1
single targets, moving STA for the highest contrast C1 single targets, stationary STA for the intermediate contrast C3
single targets, moving STA for the intermediate contrast C3
single targets, moving STA for the lowest contrast C4 single
targets and stationary STA for the lowest contrast C4 single
targets.
For the determination of each of the six STA thresholds,
we used the method of constant stimuli and presented six
single target sizes at the preset contrast to the harbor seal
in one 36-trials session: 0.11 deg, 0.45 deg, 0.80 deg, 1.20
deg, 1.55 deg, and 1.94 deg of visual angle. Each single

target size appeared six times within a session, counterbalanced for left and right presentations after Gellermann
(1933). Moreover, we divided the session in three 12-trial
blocks, and each block contained one left and one right presentation of each single target size to avoid that a putative
change in motivation during a session only influenced the
performance for specific single target sizes if these were
all accumulating in the particular interval during the session. Overall, we randomized not only the side on which
the single target appeared (Gellermann 1933), but also the
sequence of the six stimuli within the session by using the
RAND() function in Excel (Microsoft Office Professional
Plus 2016 v. 16.0.4266.1001, 2016 Microsoft Corporation,
Redmond, USA). For STA threshold determination, each
stimulus was presented 30 times. Thus, after five sessions
of 36 trials, we could plot the psychometric function, which
was then analyzed to determine the STA threshold.
The stationary and moving STA, respectively was defined
as the size of the single target needed to allow the experimental harbor seals to determine the side of presentation on
the monitor with a performance of 75% correct choices. The
STA threshold was calculated over the five sessions by linear interpolation of the performance of the harbor seal for
the last supra-threshold and first subthreshold single target.
We collected STA thresholds at least four times (see Table 1
and Supplementary Fig. S2-S4), until the harbor seal did not
further improve in performance over two consecutive STA
threshold determinations (see Supplementary Fig. S5). This
procedure was adopted as previous investigations had shown
thresholds to improve drastically over time (Chaib et al. 2019;
Spratte et al. 2021; Sandow and Hanke 2024). We defined the
STAs, reported under “Results”, to be the best STA threshold obtained over the course of STA threshold determination
for each specific contrast and stimulus condition (for all STA
thresholds see Table 1 and Supplemental Material Part 3).

Discussion

STA for seal Nick for stationary high contrast C1 single targets was determined as 0.30 deg (Table 1; Fig. 2). When
lowering the contrast between the single target and background, STA was 0.36 deg for the intermediate contrast
C3 condition and to 0.63 deg for the lowest contrast C4
condition.
The STA seal Luca had reached during the threshold
determinations for high contrast C1 stationary single targets
was 0.69 deg (Table 1; Fig. 2). Unfortunately, seal Luca
ceased to participate in experimental sessions, and we had
to stop data collection after we had determined the STA for
a stationary single target in the highest contrast condition
(C1).

We determined the STA of two harbor seals for stationary
and moving circular compact single targets at three contrast
conditions under water. The underwater STA for stationary
single targets in the highest contrast C1 condition differed
strongly between individuals, although the STA results
from the same two harbor seals tested in air compared well
(Sandow and Hanke 2024). We assume that the poor motivation of seal Luca during the first condition tested, most
likely caused by molting season, which ultimately required
ceasing data acquisition with this harbor seal, was already
affecting its performance during the STA threshold determination for this one contrast negatively (see Supplementary
Figure S4, S5). Altogether we thus need to question whether
we indeed recorded this seal’s best STA performance and
whether its performance is informative in first place. We
will thus solely discuss the underwater STA results of seal
Nick in the following sections.
Please note that we thus primarily present and discuss
the results of a single harbor seal individual. This harbor
seal individual, however, is behaving normally and does not
have visual deficits to our knowledge. Therefore, we conclude that the results are informative for this particular individual harbor seal. Comparisons between underwater and
aerial STA can be made as all results were obtained from
this one harbor seal individual. However, generalization

The underwater STA for stationary single targets in the
highest contrast C1 condition and the intermediate contrast
C3 condition determined for seal Nick amounted to 0.30

and 0.36 deg. These thresholds are broadly comparable to
the determined underwater STA for moving single targets of
the same contrast conditions (C1: 0.27 deg; C3: 0.37 deg),
as well as the STAs for stationary (C1: 0.27 deg; C3: 0.31
deg,) and moving single targets (C1: 0.21 deg; C3: 0.27 deg;

individual under comparable experimental conditions in air.
These findings support the hypothesis (see “Introduction”)
that STA in air and water will be comparable when compact
single targets are evaluated at the tested stimulus luminance.
A change in stimulus luminance could affect STA if contrast sensitivity values varied with stimulus luminance in
harbor seals as it does e.g. in cats and humans (see e.g. Pasternak and Merigan 1981) and with refraction as shown e.g.
in humans (Kebede et al. 2025; Liou and Chiu 2001; Collins and Carney 1990; for review see Pelli and Bex 2013).
Regarding the latter, a different refractive state of the harbor
seal eye depending on stimulus luminance can be assumed
based on aerial grating acuity, as a form of visual acuity, and
refractive measurements (Hanke et al. 2006a, b; Hanke and
Dehnhardt 2009). Even in water, harbor seal grating acuity might decrease with decreasing stimulus luminance as
shown for the California sea lion (Schusterman and Balliet
1971). Future experiments addressing the effect of stimulus
luminance or refraction on the harbor seal’s contrast sensitivity are needed to answer this question.
The STA results from the highest contrast C1 condition
are informative for underwater vision. While objects may
often have low contrast to the background underwater due
to e.g. light attenuation, they have a high contrast when
viewed from below against the bright water surface (Cronin
et al. 2017; Fasick et al. 2017). When thus chasing their prey
from below towards the water surface, for which there is
indication for harbor seals (Kilian et al. 2015), harbor seals
can benefit from their best STA performance provided that
the water surface is brightly lit.
While seal Nick’s underwater STA for single targets
of the highest contrast C1 condition and the intermediate
contrast C3 condition showed good correspondence to his
aerial STA for these contrast conditions (see Fig. 2 and Sandow and Hanke 2024), underwater STA for single targets
of the lowest contrast C4 condition (stationary STA: 0.63
deg, moving STA: 0.53 deg) was inferior to his aerial STA
(stationary and moving STA: 0.31 deg; Sandow and Hanke

13

2024). Thus, the harbor seal’s performance declined more in
water than in air. This difference might have resulted from
caustic effects or so-called wave-induced flicker observed
in our setup. These effects arise due to reflection of light
at the water surface induced by the movement of the water
and lead to luminance fluctuations (see e.g. Peregrine and
Smith 1978; Upstill 1979; Lock and Andrews 1992). During our experiment, movements of the water in the Perspex
basin resulted from external events such as waves from
ambient open water environment that set the floating platform, on which the experimental chamber was placed, in
motion, but particularly from the harbor seal itself lowering its head into the water-filled Perspex basin. As our
measurements indicate, these movements indeed caused
luminance fluctuations of 24 cd/m². McFarland and Loew
(1983) described that the interaction of flickering light with
the background could result in the boundaries of stationary
low-contrast objects blending in. Behavioral experiments
including humans (Matchette et al. 2018) and Picasso triggerfish (Rhinecanthus aculeatus, Matchette et al. 2020)
indeed revealed that individuals are worse at detecting a
moving target with a luminance similar to the background
luminance, thus in low contrast condition, in the presence of
water caustics. In line, caustics resulting from water movements in the Perspex basin could have masked the single
targets, stationary as well as moving, during (some) stimulus presentations. The masking effect might have interfered
with stimulus detection and thus with the side discrimination
task. Caustics might have particularly affected the performance for the lowest contrast C4 condition because it might
have partly reduced the stimulus luminance of the low contrast C4 single targets to the level of background luminance.
Consequently, caustics might explain the determined inferior performance particularly for single targets of the lowest
contrast C4 condition underwater in comparison to the other
contrast conditions (C1 and C3) underwater, which are less
affected, as well as to aerial STA in general. It needs to be
mentioned though that caustics do not generally interfere
with vision. Caustics can momentarily enhance visibility of
flickering objects when they move through the alternating
bright and dark regions (McFarland and Loew 1983).
The underwater STA for our experimental harbor seal did
not differ strongly when motion was added to the single target (see Fig. 2), which we had hypothesized based on the
results from aerial STA testing (Sandow and Hanke 2024)
anticipating that a change in medium would not affect the
pathways for processing stationary versus moving objects.
A comparable good correspondence between stationary and
moving STA are documented for budgerigars (Melopsittacus
undulates, Chaib et al. 2019; Chaib et al. 2021), and common sunfish (Lepomis gibbosus, Spratte et al. 2021; Hoppe
et al. 2024). Particularly for budgerigars, Chaib et al. (2021)

Journal of Comparative Physiology A

had speculated, based on findings from experiments on contrast sensitivity, which improved when motion was added to
the stimuli (Lind and Kelber 2011; Haller et al. 2014), that
a better moving in comparison to stationary STA would be
recorded, which was indeed not the case. For harbor seals in
air (Hanke et al. 2011) and for the bluegill sunfish (Northmore et al. 2007), a species closely related to the common
sunfish, data on contrast sensitivity is only available for
stationary stimuli. Therefore, we currently cannot assess
whether the contrast sensitivity of these species is affected
by movement. The relationship between contrast sensitivity
and STA, the effect of motion on contrast sensitivity as well
as specifics of the processing pathways for stationary and
moving objects need to be determined in (more) detail to
understand why motion seems to have only minor influence
on STA in general and in harbor seals, the latter otherwise
performing well with complex moving stimuli (Hanke et al.
2008; Gläser et al. 2014; Weiffen et al. 2014; Sandow et al.
2025). Future research could additionally focus on determining whether moving STA remains unaffected even if
stimulus velocity was changed (also see discussion in Chaib
et al. 2021; Sandow and Hanke 2024).
The two STA studies (Sandow and Hanke 2024; this
study) enabled us to systematically compare object perception in space in the same harbor seal across media, thereby
providing additional insights into the amphibious vision of
this harbor seal. The ambient illumination of 79 lx in our
experimental setting mimics an intermediate illumination
wild harbor seals encounter in their natural coastal habitat
where light intensities range from broad daylight to very
dim light e.g. during dives or at night. Thus, the determined
