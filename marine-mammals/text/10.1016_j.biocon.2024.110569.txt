Acoustic deterrent devices (ADDs) are used globally by the aquaculture industry to attempt to reduce pinniped
depredation. However, noise from ADDs sited in areas used by cetaceans may lead to changes in cetacean
behaviour and displacement from habitats impacting conservation status. Here, we investigate whether ADD
noise from aquaculture within a habitat reduces acoustic detections of harbour porpoises, indicating either
displacement or changes in echolocation activity. Nine years (2011–2019) of boat-based acoustic line-transect
data for harbour porpoises were collected across the west coast of Scotland together with ADD presence and
perceived loudness recorded by volunteers at regular listening stations along each transect. The most intense
ADD noise coincided with significantly fewer harbour porpoise detections than in areas with no ADD noise. This
corresponded with an estimated displacement of 34 % of the total undisturbed population, assuming that there
was no change in echolocation behaviour. Furthermore, significant responses occurred at estimated received
sound levels from ADDs >108 dB re 1 μPa RMS (frequency weighted for harbour porpoises). These findings
suggest porpoises are either displaced from habitats degraded by intense ADD noise and/or echolocate less. In
Scotland, ADD use in aquaculture has required a license since 2021. Limiting licensing, and monitoring ADD use
by aquaculture should be considered by conservation managers globally, particularly in areas used by acousti­
cally sensitive protected species. Non-acoustic (and non-lethal) measures to mitigate pinniped depredation on
aquaculture sites are available and should be encouraged.

1. Introduction
Marine soundscapes are complex and dynamic ‘acoustic habitats’
within which animals must detect and decipher acoustic signals from
conspecifics, predators and prey (Hatch et al., 2016). Human-made
sound has become an increasingly prevalent component of the ocean
environment (Hildebrand, 2009), resulting in detrimental effects for
marine wildlife (Nowacek et al., 2007; Slabbekoorn et al., 2010). Within
these acoustically degraded habitats, animals are forced to make tradeoffs between the benefits of remaining and taking advantage of impor­
tant resources, and the physiological and energetic costs of noise

disturbance and relocation (Hastie et al., 2021). The degree to which
animals are expected to respond to human-made noise is therefore
context dependent (Ellison et al., 2012; Gomez et al., 2016). This is of
particular relevance for repeated or chronic exposures, where animals
may become sensitised, tolerant or habituated to human-made noises
over time (Bejder et al., 2009).
In coastal areas with high levels of aquaculture production,
numerous Acoustic Deterrent Devices (ADDs; or Acoustic Harassment
Devices/Seal Scarers), which have variable duty cycles (Findlay et al.,
2021) but are often used continuously (The Scottish Government, 2021),
are deployed to attempt to reduce pinniped depredation (Coram et al.,

2014; Würsig and Gailey, 2002). ADDs used by the aquaculture industry
produce loud (>185 dB re 1 μPa RMS), mid- to high-frequency (0.5 to
40 kHz) sounds (Lepper et al., 2014; Todd et al., 2021) and are audible to
all marine mammals (Southall et al., 2019). Although the efficacy of
ADDs at deterring pinnipeds from aquaculture and static fisheries sites
has yet to be proven (Jacobs and Terhune, 2002; Yurk and Trites, 2000),
the extensive and continuous use of these devices by the aquaculture
sector has been shown to result in large-scale ensonification of coastal
habitats, leading to concerns for marine mammal species, in particular
killer whales (Orcinus orca; Morton and Symonds, 2002) and harbour
porpoises (Phocoena phocoena; Johnston and Woodley, 1998; Olesiuk
et al., 2002).
Aquaculture is an expanding industry on the west coast of Scotland
(Kenyon and Davies, 2018). This sector experiences depredation events
by harbour seals (Phoca vitulina) and grey seals (Halichoerus grypus)
which has resulted in considerable economic losses (Northridge et al.,
2013). Within the Scottish aquaculture sector, the use of ADDs had
previously been recommended as a non-lethal alternative to the shoot­
ing of seals at aquaculture sites (The Scottish Government, 2021; United
Kingdom Parliament, 2010). From 2006 and increasingly after 2011,
ADD use was widespread across aquaculture sites on the west coast of
Scotland (Findlay et al., 2018; The Scottish Government, 2021). How­
ever, concerns about the devices’ potential impacts to noise sensitive
cetaceans were frequently raised. All cetacean species inhabiting Scot­
tish waters are considered European Protected Species under The Con­
servation (Natural Habitats, &c.) Regulations 1994, prohibiting their
deliberate or reckless disturbance, capture, injury or killing (United
Kingdom Parliament, 1994). Given the potential concern for cetaceans
as a result of exposure to ADD noise, since 2021, the Scottish Govern­
ment has required the licensing of ADDs (Marine Scotland, 2021), which
has resulted in a rapid decline in the use of the devices by the aqua­
culture industry (Hampson et al., 2023).
In 2017, the widespread use of ADDs in Scottish aquaculture resulted
in over 23 % of the designated Special Area of Conservation (SAC) for
harbour porpoises (Inner Hebrides and Minches) on the Scottish west
coast being ensonified by this noise source (Findlay et al., 2021).
Harbour porpoises in this habitat occur in high-densities all year round
(Booth et al., 2013), and are believed to be among the most sensitive
species of marine mammals when it comes to acoustic disturbance
(Tougaard et al., 2015). Harbour porpoises have previously shown re­
sponses to ADD noise at distances up to 12 km, suggesting individuals
may alter their echolocation behaviour (implying a reduction in
foraging activity) and/or become displaced from biologically important
habitats where these devices are deployed (Brandt et al., 2013; Dähne
et al., 2017; Johnston, 2002; Olesiuk et al., 2002). The considerable
spatial overlap between this species, and the widespread use of ADDs by
aquaculture in this region since the 1980s (Ross, 1988) has therefore
raised concerns as to whether ADD noise may have acoustically
degraded habitats on the west coast of Scotland prior to the 2021 policy
change, leading to effects on behaviour or displacement of harbour
porpoises within this habitat and impacting conservation status for this
species.
Using nine years of acoustic line-transect survey data for harbour
porpoises and ADDs collected across the west coast of Scotland, we
investigate whether ADD noise from aquaculture has reduced the
acoustic detections of harbour porpoises when compared to areas
without ADD noise. Furthermore, we estimate received noise levels at
which individuals may be expected to show responses to ADD noise; and
what proportion of the undisturbed population may be predicted to
become displaced from habitats when exposed to ADD noise of varying
levels, if it is assumed that no change in echolocation behaviour has
occurred.

2. Materials & methods
2.1. Survey area and data collection
Acoustic line-transect surveys for harbour porpoises were conducted
by Hebridean Whale and Dolphin Trust trained volunteers onboard the
18 m motor-sailing vessel RV Silurian between 2011 and 2019. Surveys
were carried out off the west coast of Scotland (Fig. 1; extent: 55.15◦ N to
58.7◦ N and 4.7◦ W to 8.7◦ W) between March and October. This region
represents a temperate, shallow (<300 m), coastal shelf environment,
and is topographically complex with numerous islands, sounds and sea
lochs (Mcintyre and Howe, 2010). Although it was attempted to obtain
wide-scale coverage across as much of the west coast of Scotland as
possible each year, acoustic survey effort and tracks varied between
years. Global Positioning System (GPS) data were automatically recor­
ded every 1 or 10 s using Logger 2010 software developed by the In­
ternational Fund for Animal Welfare (Marine Conservation Research,
2016).
Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) of harbour porpoises was con­
ducted using a hydrophone array towed 100 m behind the survey vessel.
The array consisted of two omni-directional high-frequency elements
(HS150 elements – Sonar Research & Development Ltd.), with highest
sensitivity at 150 kHz (− 204 dB re 1 V/μPa). Continuous recordings
were made at a sampling rate of 500 kHz, resulting in an effective
analysis bandwidth of 250 kHz. This setup was designed to detect the
distinct narrow bandwidth, very high-frequency echolocation clicks
(120–140 kHz) produced by harbour porpoises (Møhl and Andersen,
1973), but also to record mid- to high-frequency ADD signals. PAM was
carried out in all sea states, during daylight hours and in waters deeper
than 20 m. To ensure consistent survey effort, periods of reduced survey
speeds (i.e., <4 knots) were removed from subsequent analysis.
Digitised signals from the hydrophone array were processed in real
time to detect harbour porpoises using PAMGuard (Gillespie et al.,
2008). All automatically classified porpoise clicks were manually veri­
fied based on frequency, amplitude, and waveform shape (Gillespie and
Chappell, 2002) in post-hoc analyses. False positives were removed, and
true porpoise detections assigned into different event classes (see Table
A1 for ‘event’ assignment details).
Every 15 min during surveys, the vessel was slowed to reduce un­
derwater radiated noise and to carry out a dedicated one-minute
‘listening station’ (Fig. 1a). During these listening periods, volunteers
listened to the hydrophone output to identify audible sounds, including
ADDs and scored the presence of ADDs by the intensity of the sound on a
scale of 0 (inaudible) to 5 (most intense) (Fig. 1c). As most ADDs
transmit sound in a frequency range of 500 Hz to 40 kHz (Lepper et al.,
2014; Todd et al., 2021), these devices are audible to humans (typical
adult human hearing range: 31 Hz to 17.6 kHz; Jackson et al., 1999). A
previous study using the same data from between 2011 and 2015 noted
high levels of accuracy by volunteers in identifying ADDs in recordings,
as false positive and false negative detections of ADDs both occurred at
<5 % of all listening stations conducted during this period (Findlay
et al., 2018).
2.2. Data processing, statistical analysis and estimates of displacement
Boat track lines were segmented around listening stations, whereby
GPS locations and acoustic data within 1 km either side of listening
stations were retained, while all other acoustic data obtained when the
vessel was beyond 1 km from a listening station were excluded. A 2 km
segment length was selected to reduce the possibility of overestimating
the significance of an ADD detection at the previous listening station on
harbour porpoise occurrence at distances noticeably further than this
point (Fig. 1b). ADD loudness for each segment was assigned based on
the loudness detection score reported by volunteers at the associated
listening station in the middle of each segment, whereby values of
0 indicated no ADD noise, and 1 to 5 indicated the increasing intensity of

Harbour porpoise positive track segments were defined as segments
where at least one porpoise event was detected (Table A1). To assess
differences in the proportion of harbour porpoise positive track seg­
ments, each year was treated as an independent sample giving nine es­
timates of the average proportion of positive track segments without any
ADD noise present, and for each of the loudness categories 1–5. This
allowed a comparison between the proportion of positive track segments
for each ADD loudness category and the baseline for that year with no
ADD noise present. To test for any significant differences in the pro­
portion of porpoise positive track segments for each ADD loudness
category and the baseline with no ADD noise present, a Generalized
Additive Mixed Model (gamm in the mgcv package in R; Wood, 2004)
with a random intercept for survey year to account for variations in
porpoise presence/absence between years, was fitted using data from all
2 km track segments (n = 9500). A binomial family with a logit link
function was used to model the response variable (harbour porpoise
presence or absence within track segments) in relation to explanatory
variables including the ADD loudness as a categorical variable,
smoothed variables day of the year, and an interaction between latitude
and longitude. The smoothed interaction between latitude and longitude
was included in the model to account for any potential spatial

autocorrelation between nearby track segment to ensure independence.
Model residuals were checked for over-dispersion. The proportion of
porpoise positive track segments for each ADD loudness category was
considered significantly different to the baseline (no ADD noise) if p <
0.05 and 95 % confidence intervals were calculated using the stats
package in R.

measure of relative porpoise density. As such, the initial analysis just
compared estimates of relative density but did not specifically include
displacement of animals from areas around the ADD source. If it is the

displacement of animals at short ranges might be expected to occur,
elevating densities slightly further away. To investigate this, individual
porpoises were assumed to be either ‘displaced’ or ‘tolerant’ within the

the total track segments, harbour porpoises were acoustically detected

stations) and ADDs were detected at 1199 listening stations (12.6 % of

3.2. Change in harbour porpoise detections with increasing ADD loudness
Harbour porpoises were acoustically detected within 2 km segments
around listening stations at all ADD loudness levels (Table 2). The pro­
portion of porpoise positive track segments (indicative of relative por­
poise density) across the study area varied by ADD loudness and
between years (Fig. 2; Table A2). The presence of harbour porpoises
within track segments showed no statistically significant difference to no
ADD noise for ADD loudness levels from 1 to 4 (Table 2). However,
harbour porpoise presence in track segments at a loudness of 5 was
found to be significantly different to no ADD noise (p = 0.009; 95 % CI of
intercept [− 0.98 to − 0.14]; Table 2) indicating a reduction in harbour
porpoises at this loudness level. The smoothed interaction of latitude
and longitude and smooth seasonal variable day of the year were also
found to influence porpoise presence (p ≤0.001), with results suggesting
an increase in porpoises closer to the coastline and a reduction in
harbour porpoises in June and July (Fig. A7). When compared to the
predicted relative density when no ADD noise was present derived from
the GAMM estimates (0.33; Fig. 2), the same relative density was pre­
dicted for ADD loudness category 1 & 2 (0.33; Table 2). For all other
ADD loudness categories a decrease in predicted relative density was
found (0.32–0.22; Table 2) when compared to when no ADD noise was
present, with the lowest occurring for an ADD loudness category of 5
(0.22; Table 2), the loudest category an ADD could be classified as by the
volunteers.

2.3. Approximate received levels for ADD loudness categories
Approximate received levels for each ADD loudness category were
derived by determining the distances between the locations of aqua­
culture sites known to use ADDs during a one-year period from 1st
February 2017 to 31st January 2018 (Findlay et al., 2021) and listening
stations with ADD noise from the same period. The distances between all
120 aquaculture sites and listening stations for each ADD loudness
category were calculated using the package sf (Pebesma, 2018), and the
minimum distances for each site and ADD loudness category were
selected. To further validate this approach and determine which aqua­
culture sites were the potential source of the ADD noise detected by
volunteers, all sites were visually assessed in relation to listening sta­
tions and manually selected in ArcGIS Pro (Fig. A6). Aquaculture sites
where noise was expected to have been blocked by land, islands, etc.
were removed from the distance analysis. The maximum distance for
each ADD loudness category was then derived.
To determine the received levels of ADD noise from aquaculture,
weighted for very high-frequency (VHF) cetaceans (Southall et al.,
2019) at distances associated with each ADD loudness category, an
energy-flux acoustic propagation model (Weston, 1971), implemented
in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA, R2021a) was used. The
model has previously shown good agreement between measured and
modelled aquaculture ADD noise in this region (Findlay et al., 2021). All
decidecade frequency bands (commonly referred to as approximate onethird octave bands) centred between 2 and 40 kHz were modelled,
assuming a constant depth of 40 m, which is the median depth within 5
km of the Scottish west coast shoreline. Additional details on the
bathymetric data, seabed acoustic and water column properties used can
be found in Findlay et al. (2021). Energy source levels (LS,E; dB re 1
μPa2s⋅m2) for three ADD types known to be used during the period of
this study (Ace Aquatec US2, Airmar dB Plus II, and Terecos Type DSMS4) were taken from the literature (Findlay et al., 2021; Lepper et al.,
2014). Received sound pressure levels (Lp; dB re 1 μPa RMS) were
calculated at each decidecade centre frequency using the passive sonar

In this study, no significant difference in harbour porpoise presence
was detected for ADD loudness categories 1–4 (Tables 2 & 3; Fig. 2) and
estimates of displacement indicated that some individuals would
tolerate noise within zones exposed to all ADD loudness categories
(Table 3), suggesting individual differences in tolerance during exposure
to ADD noise. Harbour porpoises have been shown to utilise areas close
to aquaculture sites using ADDs. Northridge et al. (2013, 2010) acous­
tically detected porpoises in close proximity (<200 m) to ten active
Airmar ADDs which had been used at a site for several months, and
found only weak to minimal responses to Terecos ADDs out to a distance
of 1.2 km; thus concluding that these devices did not completely
displace individuals from the study site. Animals within this population
are potentially making adaptive trade-offs to avoid or remain within a
prime habitat due to the presence of favourable prey resources and site
quality, even when exposed to ADD noise (Wirsing et al., 2008). This is
supported by a tagging study by Elmegaard et al. (2023) which noted
that one of the naïve porpoises did not demonstrate a measurable re­
action to Lofitech ADD pulses, which authors concluded was due to a
strong incentive to remain because of the presence of a patch of prey.
When assessing the potential for noise to alter the behavioural responses
of individuals, a dose-response relationship based on the received level
of noise expected to elicit a response is often utilised (Dunlop et al.,
2017; Harris et al., 2018). However, given that not all individuals will
respond to high levels of human-made noise, the environmental context,
animal biology, ‘foraging drive’, and prior experience with anthropo­
genic stressors should also be considered when assessing individual
differences in behavioural responses (Ellison et al., 2012; Gomez et al.,
2016; Harding et al., 2019; Hastie et al., 2021).

4. Discussion
We present evidence that harbour porpoises on the west coast of
Scotland responded to repeated and chronic exposure to acoustic
deterrent device (ADD) noise from aquaculture sites over a nine-year
period. The most intense ADD noise detected by volunteers during
acoustic line transect surveys (category 5) coincided with a statistically
significant reduction in the acoustic detections of harbour porpoises
when compared to areas with no ADD noise (Table 2; Fig. 2). Assuming
porpoises did not change their echolocation behaviour and avoided
portions of their habitats with loud ADD noise, 34 % of the predicted
total undisturbed population were estimated to be displaced from areas
exposed to this loudness level. Harbour porpoises have been displaced
during long-term exposure to ADDs deployed at aquaculture sites at
distances up to 4.3 km (Johnston, 2002; Northridge et al., 2010; Olesiuk
et al., 2002), which encompasses the 2 km distance predicted for ADD
loudness 5 in this study (Fig. 3). These results are further corroborated
by studies where ADDs with similar source levels (e.g., Lofitech (Lofi­
tech, Leknes, Norway): 189 dB re 1 μPa), have been used for short pe­
riods prior to impact pile driving. In this context, avoidance distances up
to 12 km were demonstrated (Brandt et al., 2013; Dähne et al., 2017;
Voß et al., 2023), and strong directional movements away from this ADD
type have been recorded (Elmegaard et al., 2023; Graham et al., 2023).
Alternatively, the reduction in porpoise presence reported in this study
may be due to individuals remaining within exposed habitats but
altering their echolocation behaviour making them less detectable
acoustically. For example, Elmegaard et al. (2023) found that four of six
tagged harbour porpoises, which were naïve to ADD noise, reduced their
echolocation click rate by an average of 54 % in response to the onset of
14 kHz pulses from a Lofitech ADD. Two of these individuals reduced the
click output level by 8 dB (95th percentile). However, it is important to
note that this type of anti-predator response of echolocation reduction
might be less strong in individuals which have previously been exposed
to this noise source, such as those inhabiting waters along the Scottish
west coast, and have potentially learned over time that the signal does
not constitute a direct threat. Although it was not possible in this study
to determine exactly what response porpoises made following exposure
to ADD noise, the reduction in porpoise acoustic detections in areas with
the most intense ADD noise suggests that harbour porpoises on the west
coast of Scotland were either excluded from biologically important
habitats and/or remained but altered their echolocation behaviour, in
response to widespread use of ADDs at aquaculture sites between 2011
and 2019.
Harbour porpoises are thought to forage nearly continuously to meet
their high energetic demands (Rojano-Donãte et al., 2018; Wisniewska
et al., 2016). As such, any changes in behaviour which impact foraging
activity (i.e., reductions in echolocation) or result in individuals being
displaced from otherwise suitable habitats, could be detrimental to
maintaining a consistent food intake, resulting in energetic imbalances
and fitness consequences for individuals (Wisniewska et al., 2016). On
the west coast of Scotland, harbour porpoises occur in high-densities
(Booth et al., 2013), but with slight variability in relative density at
different times of year (Fig. A7). Nonetheless, their distribution has
overlapped with the aquaculture sector and continuous ADD use for
nearly four decades (Ross, 1988). The acoustic degradation of habitats
used by harbour porpoises caused by ADD use at aquaculture sites over
extended periods and spatial scales is therefore especially problematic
and may have resulted in longer-term consequences for the wider
harbour porpoise population in this region.

‘deliberately or recklessly disturbed…’ by human activities (United
Kingdom Parliament, 1994). Given that the results of this study show
porpoise disturbance due to ADD noise over nearly a decade of obser­
vations, the use of ADDs at aquaculture sites on the west coast of Scot­
land has likely impacted the conservation status of this species. Our
results therefore support the 2021 implementation of stricter licensing
and regulation of ADDs at aquaculture sites to limit impacts to this
protected species.
ADDs have been recommended as a non-lethal method to attempt to
mitigate pinniped depredation at aquaculture and static fisheries sites
around the world (e.g., Güçlüsoy and Savas, 2003; Jacobs and Terhune,
2002; Lehtonen et al., 2022; Pemberton and Shaughnessy, 1993; Qui­
ñones et al., 2019; The Scottish Government, 2021). As such it is ex­
pected that impacts to cetaceans from exposure to ADD noise is not just a
Scottish problem. The long-term efficacy of ADDs at deterring pinnipeds
from aquaculture and static fisheries sites remains debated (Jacobs and
Terhune, 2002; Yurk and Trites, 2000), but it is clear that these devices
can cause widespread acoustic degradation of coastal marine habitats,
which may be negatively altering behaviour or displacing cetaceans,
such as harbour porpoises (Johnston and Woodley, 1998; Morton and
Symonds, 2002; Olesiuk et al., 2002). Attempts are being made to adjust
the acoustic outputs of ADDs to reduce these effects, with newer gen­
eration ADDs, often termed ‘cetacean friendly’, which have lower source
levels, duty cycles and frequencies (Götz and Janik, 2016; Götz and
Janik, 2015; McGarry et al., 2020; The Scottish Government, 2021).
However, similar to the older devices, rigorous evidence to support the
efficacy of these new devices in deterring pinnipeds and reducing
exclusion of cetacean species around aquaculture sites is limited to only
a few studies (e.g., Götz and Janik, 2016; Götz and Janik, 2015; Hiley
et al., 2021). Further, the use of lower-frequency devices may increase
disturbance in other cetacean species that are more sensitive to lower
frequencies, such as baleen whales (Boisseau et al., 2021; McGarry et al.,
2017).
Instead, aquaculture sites should continue to develop and improve
non-acoustic mitigation options to reduce pinniped depredation. These
include improving animal husbandry, reducing stock density, seal
blinds, anti-predator nets, maintaining correct net tensioning, and using
stronger and stiffer net materials (e.g., high-density polyethylene
netting; Thompson et al., 2021). The change in licensing requirements
and subsequent drop in ADD use on the west coast of Scotland also
provides a unique opportunity to determine whether reduced ADD use
leads to an increase in seal depredation events and whether acoustic
detections of harbour porpoises have increased in areas previously
exposed to ADD noise (Hampson et al., 2023; Marine Scotland, 2021).
Overall, a shift toward the use of non-acoustic seal deterrence methods
will reduce acoustic degradation of coastal marine habitats and help to
decouple the aquaculture industry from harmful effects on cetaceans.

5. Conclusions

Authors declare that they have no competing interests.

ADDs are used globally by the aquaculture industry in attempts to
reduce pinniped depredation. However, the widespread use of these
devices in habitats also used by cetacean species has led to conservation
concerns that individuals may alter their acoustic behaviour or become
displaced from habitats. Here we demonstrate, using nine years of
acoustic line transect data (2011–2019), that harbour porpoise acoustic
detections were reduced, and individuals may have been displaced from
areas exposed to the most intense ADD noise (volunteer defined category

Scotland. Changes in echolocation behaviour and/or displacement were
