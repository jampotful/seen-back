Assessment of goose-beaked whale responses to mid-frequency active sonar using a hierarchical hidden Markov model.

# Assessment of goose-beaked whale responses to mid-frequency active sonar using a hierarchical hidden Markov model

## Abstract
AbstractBackgroundAcoustic disturbance is increasingly recognized as an ecological concern, particularly at sea. In light of marine mammal stranding events associated with use of naval mid-frequency active sonar (MFAS), which often involve goose-beaked whales (Ziphius cavirostris), research has sought to detect and characterize Ziphius behavioral responses to MFAS.MethodsWe deployed Sound and Motion Recording and Telemetry (SMRT) tags on 13 Ziphius in the Southern California Bight. The study area includes the United States Navy’s Southern California Anti-submarine Warfare Range (SOAR), and deployments overlapped temporally with training exercises including MFAS use. We used hierarchical hidden Markov models (HHMMs), which provided a framework to model whale behavior and putative responses to MFAS at two relevant time scales: foraging dive cycles, which may last hours, and five-minute intervals, where finer-scale changes in movement and behavior are evident.ResultsWe analyzed 70.7 days of tag data (1697.6 h) representing 361 Ziphius foraging dive cycles, 52 with MFAS detections. Cumulative MFAS sound energy level (cSEL) per dive cycle was 69.9–160.3 dB re 1µPa2s (median 121.3, inter-quartile range (IQR) 21.7). We identified two dive-cycle states: “Typical” and “Variant.” In Variant state, durations of dive cycles, foraging dives, non-foraging dives, and echolocation periods were shorter on average but more variable, while time spent near the surface and net distance traveled were longer and more variable. According to Akaike’s information criterion (AIC), MFAS cSEL (rather than per-sound maximum received level) increased the probability of switching from Typical to Variant dive-cycle state, with MFAS cSEL also modulating transition rates between four fine-scale behavior states during Variant dive cycles only. Whales transitioned to Variant state in 12 of 52 MFAS-exposed dive cycles (23.1%), with observed Variant state dwell times ranging from 1 to 72.4 h (median 9.0, IQR 9.2).ConclusionsThis work has relevance for conservation and management, enhancing our understanding of Ziphius behavioral responses to operational MFAS in an area where naval training activities occur regularly. These analysis tools may prove useful in assessing responses to other acoustic disturbance and understanding how shorter-term behavior changes translate to longer-term consequences.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40462-026-00647-9.Keywords: Ziphius cavirostris, Mid-frequency active sonar, Hierarchical hidden Markov model, Behavioral response, High-resolution biologging, Acoustic disturbance, Biologging

## Background
Along with climate change, pollution, and evolving land-use, sounds generated by human activities are among the complex suite of environmental stressors arising in the anthropocene. For marine species, ocean soundscapes now often include sound sources such as vessel traffic, geophysical surveys, pile driving, fisheries (e.g., echosounders), and military activities (including active sonars used in anti-submarine warfare) [1–3]. To better understand the effects of these changes, particularly on species like whales and dolphins that rely on sound to find food, communicate, and sense their environment, research efforts have yielded a growing body of evidence on the effects of sound at sea on marine mammals (reviewed in [4–7]). Previous work on unusual marine mammal mass-stranding events associated with naval mid-frequency active sonar (MFAS) activity in particular (reviewed in [8–12]) has motivated ongoing efforts to understand how MFAS sounds may affect marine mammal physiology and hearing, as well as significant investments in studying behavioral responses to MFAS (synthesized in [13–16]). Across a range of whale and dolphin species, examples of documented behavioral responses to naval sonar sounds include changes in passively-detected vocalizations (indicative of changes in sound production and/or spatial avoidance) [17–25], altered group social behavior and movements [26–28], and changes in diving and foraging behavior [29–34]. There is an emerging consensus that the likelihood, intensity, and type of response is context-dependent, varying not only with signal characteristics and source distance but the animal’s physiological, social, and environmental context [15, 35–37].
Family Ziphiidae, the beaked whales, a group of primarily deep-water species whose natural history includes acoustic and behavioral crypsis [38–46], had been subject to relatively little scientific attention before goose-beaked whales (Ziphius cavirostris, hereafter Ziphius) and dense-beaked whales (Mesoplodon densirostris) featured prominently in sonar-associated strandings [8, 9, 47]. Ongoing work shows beaked whales are highly sensitive to acoustic disturbance by various sources including vessel noise [48, 49] and echosounders [50] as well as naval sonar. Behavioral responses associated with MFAS have been documented in northern bottlenose whales (Hyperoodon ampullatus) [51, 52], Baird’s beaked whales (Berardius bairdii) [53], dense-beaked whales [20, 25, 29, 54, 55], and goose-beaked whales [30, 56, 57], often including cessation of foraging, longer deeper dives, and spatial avoidance.
The risk-disturbance hypothesis is one conceptual framework relevant to behavioral responses to disturbance and subsequent fitness and population consequences. It draws an analogy between anthropogenic disturbance stimuli and predation risk and suggests that animals may react similarly to both, investing energy or foregoing opportunities to feed or mate in order to avoid risk [15, 58]. Animals may thus navigate through a complex, changing terrain of risks, reacting to perceived risk and mitigating it by accepting the costs of ceasing to feed or relocating to less-desirable habitat [59, 60]. The adaptive benefits of these behavioral responses depend on the accuracy of their risk perception and should vary with environmental, physiological, and social context [59, 61–64]. Thus, when animals perceive human disturbance as predation risk, they may bear the costs of response without the benefits, potentially leading to reduced individual fitness and ultimately negative population consequences [58, 65].
In this framework, responsiveness to human disturbance might correspond to responsiveness to predators, as has been suggested to be the case for whales and sonar [65]. All these ideas seem particularly relevant to the case of Ziphius and MFAS: sources that are unpredictable in time and space are thought more likely to be perceived as threats [66]; Ziphius perform long, deep foraging dives that may be physiologically demanding [39, 40, 42, 44, 67], and their hunting strategies are finely tuned (in terms of echolocation pulse characteristics and group foraging dive synchrony) to avoid the risk of predation by killer whales [46, 68, 69].
Previous work on how Ziphius may alter behavior in response to MFAS has included both controlled exposure experiments with simulated MFAS sound sources under researcher control [56, 57] and observational exposure studies in conjunction with real naval training exercises [30]. Responses of a small number of individuals have been recorded with high-resolution movement and acoustic recording tags [56, 57], and satellite tags have been used to assess responses at lower resolution, but over longer timeframes and with much larger sample sizes [30, 56]. To synthesize and build on these results, key questions to address include: clarifying how instantaneous sound level, cumulative sound energy, and/or distance to sound source modulate responsiveness [30, 70, 71]; whether the probability and intensity of response differ in areas where MFAS is used frequently versus rarely [52, 56]; how responses can be characterized to allow their reliable detection in lower-resolution data [72]; and how to connect shorter-term behavior changes to subsequent fitness and population consequences [15].
A long-held goal in understanding behavioral responses to acoustic disturbance has been to integrate data across timescales. While data related to foraging and energy expenditure (including echolocation and acceleration) and detailed sound exposure levels are best captured at very fine time resolution, periods of exposure and responses may last multiple hours, and data reflecting individual fitness and population health are generally realized at even longer timescales. Both the nature of responses and the means used to record them generally dictate analytical timescales, typically with a trade-off between archival biologging devices that record high-resolution data over hours or days, satellite tags that record lower-resolution data for weeks to months, and passive acoustic monitoring data that are captured at even larger spatiotemporal scales [15, 72], with few examples of analytical frameworks capable of integrating multiple data scales.
Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) are increasingly used in ecology to characterize and classify animal movements and behavior [73–76], including in the context of assessing response to human disturbance such as naval sonar [27, 32, 34, 65, 77, 78], either by including exposure metrics as candidate covariates affecting the rate of transitions between behavior states or in a multi-stage approach where behavior state estimates based on an HMM are modeled as a function of exposure. Standard HMMs are discrete-time models, necessitating the choice of a time-step for analysis, whether a fixed time duration or a biologically defined interval (for example, for marine mammals, a dive). Based on studies of Ziphius MFAS exposures to date, we may expect behavioral responses at several timescales: deeper or longer dives and increased inter-foraging-dive intervals that are measured in hours, but also finer-scale changes in movements such as cessation of foraging, more directed swimming, and altered gait that are reflected in data sampled on second or sub-second scales [30, 56, 57, 79].
Hierarchical Hidden Markov Models (HHMMs) allow modeling behavior at multiple timescales simultaneously, while incorporating biological knowledge about animal behavior to summarize tag data appropriately at each scale. Previous application of HHMMs in ecology has mainly employed the hierarchical structure in order to infer coarse-timescale behavioral states from fine-scale observations or to otherwise link several HMMs [80–83]. We applied HHMMs to characterize Ziphius behavior and putative responses to MFAS exposure at coarse and fine timescales simultaneously, allowing for two latent states at the coarse timescale, one perhaps including response behavior. We specified identical fine-scale behavioral states in both coarse states, but allowed rates of transition between coarse states, and between fine-scale states in the coarse state potentially including responsive behavior, to vary with MFAS exposure covariates.
Most ecological HHMM examples to date have not included any observed data streams at the coarse scale, except in the case of Sidrow and colleagues’ killer whale dive behavior example [82], which focuses on model formulation and validation rather than inference related to specific model parameters. Including observed data streams at the coarse scale, with coarse-scale dynamics a primary target of inference, may seem problematic if coarse- and fine-scale observations carry the same weight in the likelihood calculation, but more abundant fine-scale observations potentially dominate the likelihood. This scenario could evoke well-known challenges due to class imbalance in supervised learning or rare labels in semi-supervised classification [84, 85]. Thus, one might consider weighting the likelihood of coarse-timescale observations corresponding to their duration (that is, the number of fine-scale time-steps they contain) in hope of enhancing estimation of coarse-scale parameters. A likelihood-weighting approach has previously been shown to improve latent-state classification accuracy in semi-supervised HMMs, where some labelled data for which the underlying state is known are available [86]; however, given the goal of predictive modeling and classification, effects of the weighting scheme on the accuracy of model parameter estimates and corresponding inference were not detailed. Thus, as part of the process of formulating our models, we undertook a small simulation study to examine the effects of a likelihood-weighting approach on inference in a HHMM.
Here, we used HHMMs to model both coarse- (dive-cycle) and fine-scale behavior. This approach allowed us to estimate the probability of changes in behavior as a function of MFAS exposure at both timescales, while also providing a quantitative description of putative response behavior and estimates of response duration. We present analysis of a large high-resolution dataset collected from free-ranging Ziphius that includes exposure to real United States Navy training exercises using MFAS, functionally representing an opportunistic exposure experiment [15, 16]. This analysis has the potential to improve management of impacts in our study population, and more generally to advance our ability to model longer-term consequences of behavioral responses to acoustic disturbance.

## Methods

## Study area and tag deployments
Field work was carried out in the San Nicolas Basin, a deep basin west of San Clemente Island within the Southern California Bight (Fig. 1). This area includes the United States Navy’s Southern California Anti-submarine Warfare Range (SOAR), which is used for military training exercises that include MFAS operations, allowing us to collect opportunistic exposure data by deploying biologging tags on whales in the region. These training operations may include use of multiple sources with varied characteristics, such as high-power, surface-ship and mid-power, helicopter-deployed MFAS [30]. We deployed medium-duration, dart-attached archival Sound and Motion Recording and Telemetry (SMRT) tags (Wildlife Computers, Redmond, WA USA) on 13 Ziphius at SOAR between 2019 and 2024 (Table 1). These Type A tags attach to the dorsal surface of the whale’s body via four medical-grade titanium darts, each capable of penetrating up to 6.7 cm [87]. The tags can record high-resolution movement data, along with geolocation and acoustic data, for periods of days to weeks. SMRT tags record acoustic data from a single hydrophone sampling at up to 192 kHz with 16-bit resolution. On the tag, data were decimated and underwent lossless compression before being stored at 96 kHz (2019 and 2021 tags) or 48 kHz (2022–2024 tags) [88]. The tags employ a one-pole high-pass filter at 150 Hz in addition to anti-alias filtering, resulting in a -3dB bandwidth of about 100 Hz − 44 kHz with 96 kHz sampling (or 100 Hz − 22 kHz with 48 kHz sampling). These settings were chosen to maximize potential tag recording duration (since higher sampling rates use more power and storage space); although the effective recording bandwidth sometimes fell short of Ziphius echolocation pulses’ reported − 10dB bandwidth of about 31–53 kHz [68], tag records of tagged-animal echolocation sounds contain much more low-frequency energy than far-field recordings [89–91], meaning the recordings with lower sampling frequencies were adequate to detect times of echolocation pulse production by the tagged whale. Additional sensors on the SMRT tags (and their sampling rates) include a tri-axial accelerometer (100 Hz), tri-axial magnetometer (25 Hz), depth sensor (1 Hz), and internal (25 Hz) and external (1 Hz) temperature sensors. Fastloc GPS positions were also recorded up to 6 times per hour.

## Data calibration and processing
Data processing and analysis used PAMGuard (versions 2_00_16e – 2_02_15) [92], Matlab (version 2024b; The Mathworks, Natick, MA USA), and R statistical computing software (version 4.5.1 [93]), employing open-source tools for high-resolution biologging data [94] plus custom scripts (see: 10.5281/zenodo.17941561).
Acoustic recordings from the SMRT tags were reviewed by an analyst using the PAMGuard spectrogram module in ViewerMode [92], with a Hanning window duration of 10 s and a 2048-point fast Fourier transform incorporating a 50% overlap. These spectrogram parameters yielded a frequency resolution of 23.4 Hz and a time resolution of 42.7 ms. The analyst marked start and end times of discrete anthropogenic signals (including MFAS, echosounder pings, and impulsive sounds from explosive detonations or objects impacting the water surface), offshore killer whale (Orcinus orca) calls, and periods of echolocation pulse production by the tagged whale (defined as the time from the first to the final detected pulse during a single dive). Tagged whale pulses were distinguished from those of conspecifics based on the presence of more low-frequency energy (below about 20 kHz) and the relative consistency of inter-pulse intervals and amplitude over time within a pulse series [68, 89–91, 95].
Tag data calibration and pre-processing included calibration of accelerometer, magnetometer, and depth data, and conversion from tag to whale frame-of-reference as described by Johnson and Tyack, as well as dive detection [88, 94]. “Dives” were defined as any submergence to > 50 m depth; “foraging dives” were dives that included echolocation pulses produced by the tagged whale [40–42]. Fastloc GPS positions from the tag were retained for analysis if they were obtained via 4 or more satellites, had a residual value less than 35 [96], and had timing error less than 3 s.
For each detected MFAS sound, we computed a root-mean-squared sound pressure level (RMS SPL; dB re 1µPa) as well as a sound exposure level (SEL; dB re 1µPa2s). The RMS SPL calculation was consistent with previous studies and recommendations [13, 55, 97]. All processing was done in Matlab (The Mathworks, Natick, MA) using purpose-written code. We applied a set of ANSI standard ⅓ octave filters (each a 6th-order Butterworth bandpass filter) with center frequencies spanning the MFAS frequency range (from 1 to 9 kHz) [98–100]. In each band, we recorded the highest RMS SPL in any 200 ms window, omitting samples containing whale echolocation pulses or other transients (as in [55]). We compared each SPL value to the corresponding value in a 600 ms noise clip starting 1 s before the start of the measured signal (or the most recent such clip, if a previous signal was still occurring 1 s before the current one). SPLs are reported only for signals with signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) above 6 dB. To obtain a broadband SPL for each signal, values were summed over all relevant ⅓ octave bands where SNR exceeded 6 dB. SEL was then computed, for each detected MFAS sound and in each ⅓ octave band, over a time window from the first to the last time the SNR of RMS SPL exceeded 6 dB. Cumulative SEL (cSEL) at the dive-cycle scale was computed by summing all individual SEL values (across all the ⅓ octave bands between 1 and 9 kHz) over each dive cycle, while cSEL for each five-minute interval was summed from the start of the current dive cycle up to the current five-minute interval. For all RMS SPL and SEL summations, values were converted to a linear scale for summation and converted back to dB afterward.

## Statistical modelling

## Hierarchical hidden markov models
We fitted HHMMs to the tag behavior time-series data to identify behavioral states at two time-scales and quantify potential behavioral responses to MFAS exposure [80, 81]. The coarse-scale time-step for the models was the dive cycle, defined as the time from the start of one foraging dive until the start of the next one, including the foraging dive plus an inter-foraging-dive interval usually comprising several shorter dives to intermediate depths. As noted, we identified foraging dives based on detection of echolocation pulses on the tag acoustic recording, therefore only data through the end of the last foraging dive with acoustic data were included in the analysis. At the dive-cycle scale, input data streams were: dive cycle duration in hours, foraging dive duration in hours, echolocation period in minutes, total time spent at depths less than 5 m, longest non-foraging dive duration in hours, and net distance traveled (i.e., straight line distance from the initial to final GPS position in the dive cycle); all these were modeled using Gamma distributions. The fine-scale time-step for the HHMMs was 5 min, and at this scale the input data streams (and probability distributions used to model them) were: 90th percentile of the minimum specific acceleration (MSA) in m/s2. [101] (Gamma distribution), fluke strokes per second (zero-inflated Weibull distribution), and heading variability (measured as the circular standard deviation, which ranges from 0 upward [102, 103]) (Gamma distribution), and presence or absence of echolocation pulses (Bernoulli distribution). Fluke stroke rate was computed as in Martín López and colleagues [104, 105] using custom software implementation in the tagtools packages for R and Matlab [94]. We used a high-pass filter frequency of 0.28 Hz (corresponding to an expected dominant stroke frequency of about 0.7 Hz) and a threshold of 2 degrees for fluke-stroke detection, computing the average fluke stroke rate for each five-minute interval by dividing the total number of detected strokes by the interval duration.
Models included two states at the dive-cycle scale and four states at the five-minute scale. At the coarse scale, this allowed for the possibility of typical behavior and a “variant”, putative response state. At the fine scale, based on study goals and biological knowledge of the species’ natural history, four states could distinguish between foraging and nonforaging behavior and allow for a few distinct levels of activity, but not so many as to obscure biological interpretability [106]. In addition, inspection of lag plots ([82, 107], Figures S37 - S39) did not indicate a clear need for additional fine-scale states. HHMMs were constrained such that the state-dependent distributions at the fine (five-minute) scale were identical in both of the coarse-scale (dive-cycle) states, but probabilities of transition between the four fine-scale states could vary between them. This constraint is motivated by previously described beaked whale response to acoustic disturbance, in which fine-scale movements and diving change, but not to a new regime totally outside the typical range [52, 53, 55, 57, 79]. We fitted models with and without MFAS covariates on the between-state transition probabilities at both timescales. Candidate exposure covariates included MFAS RMS received level (RL) in the current timestep, cumulative MFAS SEL (cumulated over each dive cycle), a logical variable indicating whether or not any MFAS sounds were detected prior to the end of the tagged whale’s echolocation period, and (at the coarse scale only) an interaction between that logical variable and either MFAS RMS RL or MFAS SEL. We used AIC to compare models in conjunction with graphical model validation (lag plots and pseudo-residuals [107, 108]) and visualizations.
Models were fitted using the momentuHMM package [108] in R statistical computing software (version 4.3) [93]. Provision of appropriate starting values for all model parameters is crucial for increasing the chance to identify the global maximum of the HMM likelihood, but since choosing them is very difficult, the risk of ending up in local maxima is a real danger; current best practice includes re-fitting each model many times while varying the starting values [74]. We adopted an iterative model-fitting procedure, first fitting (non-hierarchical) HMMs to the coarse-scale and fine-scale data separately. Each model was re-fitted 100 times, perturbing starting values by adding samples from a normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 250, then 100 more times with a perturbation standard deviation of 10. Initial starting parameters were based on data quantiles for the first model fitted, but subsequent starting values were obtained from the best model fitted so far. The entire process was repeated 10 times, so that each model was fitted 2000 times. Parameter estimates from the coarse-only and fine-only HMMs were then used as starting values for fitting the HHMMs, which were repeatedly fitted using a similar procedure, except each individual HHMM was re-fitted 10 rather than 100 times and the entire process repeated 4 rather than 10 times (because the individual HHMM fits take much longer). After model fitting, the Viterbi algorithm was used to identify the most likely state sequence for each tag deployment [73] (Table S1 and Figures S3 - S30).

## Results

## Tag deployment summary
Table 1 summarizes metadata on the 13 SMRT tag deployments on Ziphius included in this analysis. Tags had a total deployment duration of about 87 days (2096.9 h) with 569 dive cycles, each of which included a foraging dive and the subsequent period until the start of the next foraging dive (Table 1). For HHMM analysis, only complete dive cycles with acoustic data were included, since input data for the models included MFAS levels and data on echolocation. Thus, HHMMs used data from all 13 deployments, totaling 70.7 days (1697.6 h) with 361 dive cycles, of which 55 included MFAS detections (Table 1). For the 52 dive cycles in which MFAS was detected at measurable levels (SNR above 6 dB), cumulative SEL per dive cycle ranged from 69.9 to 160.3 dB re 1µPa2s, with a median of 121.3 (IQR 21.7); this corresponded to RMS levels of 76.3–151.1 dB re 1µPa (median 119, IQR 20.2) (Table S2). Other anthropogenic sounds were also detected on tag records: 14 dive cycles had echosounders and 84 had impulsive sounds (Figures S3 - S17). One also had killer whale vocalizations (Figure S11). Supplemental materials include summary data for every analyzed foraging dive cycle (Table S1), MFAS metrics for each tag deployment and overall (Tables S2 - S3), and time-series plots for each tag deployment at fine and dive-cycle resolution (Figures S3 - S30).

## Hierarchical Hidden Markov Models
AIC-based model comparison clearly supported inclusion of MFAS effects in the model, with the best model outperforming the no-sonar model by about 43.5 AIC units (Table 2). The model with lowest AIC (by 3 + units) was one where rates of transition between states were affected by MFAS cSEL at both timescales, with cSEL models consistently having lower AIC than RMS ones (Table 2). AIC did not support the idea that the effect of MFAS on transition rates depends on the timing of exposure (before or after the end of echolocation (EOE) pulses) (Table 2). All results presented subsequently will be for the best model, with cSEL effects at both dive-cycle and five-minute scales.

At either timescale (coarse/dive cycle or fine/five-minute), the effect of MFAS is on the between-state transition probabilities. Models using both RMS and cSEL metrics used cSEL at the dive-cycle scale and RMS at the five-minute scale. For some models, at the coarse timescale, a binary variable indicating whether or not any MFAS was detected before the end of echolocation pulse production (“MFAS before EOE effect”) could also affect transition probabilities, perhaps interacting with MFAS intensity
The fitted HHMM characterizes two behavioral states at the dive-cycle scale, which we have termed the Typical and Variant states. The Variant state has dive-cycle durations, foraging dive durations, longest non-foraging dive durations, and echolocation periods that are all shorter on average, but more variable, than the Typical state; it also has longer and much more variable near-surface durations and horizontal distances traveled (Fig. 2). Transition to, and perhaps persistence in, the Variant state is more likely as MFAS cSEL increases, although Variant state can also occur in the absence of MFAS exposure, either as part of the animals’ standard behavioral repertoire or perhaps in response to other stimuli (Figs. 1 and 2 and S3 - S17). The longest dive-cycle durations, as well as the longest foraging and non-foraging dive and surface durations, tend to correspond to the highest cSEL exposures (Fig. 2). With increasing MFAS cSEL, transitions to the Variant state were more likely (and transitions back to the Typical state perhaps less likely), although even at the highest cSEL values recorded in this study (160 dB re 1µPa2s), transition to Variant state was expected to occur just under half the time (Fig. 3). Once in Variant state, the rate of transition back to Typical was lower, and persistence in Variant state higher, with higher MFAS cSEL; however, these estimates had very high uncertainty (Fig. 3) since MFAS exposure occurred when the whale was already in Variant state only six times (Table S3).

At the five-minute timescale, the characteristics of the four behavioral states a whale can be in are constrained to be identical in both Typical and Variant dive cycles (Fig. 4). Echolocation is nearly always present in state 1 and almost never in any other state (probability of echolocation (SE): state 1, 0.95 (0.0034); state 2, 4.4 * 10− 23 (1.9 * 10− 36), state 3, 0.00068 (0.00028); state 4, 3.3 * 10− 39 (4.6 * 10− 53)), so state 1 corresponds to echolocation-based foraging. State 1 is also characterized by more active swimming (highest average fluke stroke rate and 90th percentile of MSA values) and higher average heading variability than the other three states (Fig. 4). MSA is consistently lowest in state 4, a bit higher in state 2, and most variable in state 3 (Fig. 4). Fluke stroke rate is lowest in state 4, and intermediate and more variable in states 2–3 (Fig. 4). Expected heading variance is consistently low in state 2 and more variable in states 3–4 (Fig. 4). Overall, state 2 seems to indicate steady directed movement while state 4 seems the least active (Fig. 4). Relying on the decoded state sequences, state 1 occurs around the bottom phase of deep foraging dives (Figures S3 - S17). State 2 usually lasts a single 5-minute interval, and occurs primarily during ascent, especially from deep foraging dives, but its occurrence during non-foraging dives increases about 3-fold in Variant dive cycles (Figures S3–17, S34; Table S5). State 4 mainly occurs surrounding the bottom phase of non-foraging dives, and state 3 at other times (including ascents and descents of both foraging and non-foraging dives, and when near the surface between dives) (Figures S3 - S17).

Although the characteristics of each fine-timescale state remain constant, rates of transition between the four fine-timescale states are allowed to vary in Typical and Variant dive cycles; in addition, during Variant dive cycles, the fine-timescale transition rates also depend on MFAS cSEL (Fig. 5). Differences in fine-scale transition rates between Typical and Variant dive cycles are quite small in the absence of MFAS exposure, and the dependence of transition rates on MFAS levels is also minimal for most transitions (Fig. 5). However, notably, as MFAS cSEL increases, persistence in state 1 (echolocation-based foraging) declines, and transitions from state 4 to 3 also decline; in exchange, transitions from state 1 to 2 and 3 and persistence in state 4 are more common. This means that as current MFAS cSEL increases, whales tend to spend more time in states 3–4, which usually occur during non-foraging dives. At higher cSEL in particular, the least-active non-foraging state 4 (bottom of non-foraging dives) is more prevalent, essentially taking the place of the usual foraging and ascent behavior (Figs. 5, S32, S34, S36; Table S4). The depths at which fine-scale states occur may also differ between Typical and Variant dive cycles, especially with rising MFAS levels; both states 2 and 4 occur more broadly throughout the water column in Variant dive cycles, with increasing depth as MFAS levels increase (Figures S3-S17, S32-S34).

## Discussion

## Statistical models
The HHMM approach applied here allowed us to jointly summarize behavior at several distinct, biologically-relevant timescales. While some exciting methodological developments facilitate modeling data from high-resolution biologging tags more directly to characterize behavior and detect behavioral changes [75, 82, 109, 110], for now, it remains a formidable computational challenge to apply such methods without some kind of summarizing or downsampling, since the tags record information many times per second, from multiple sensors, for multiple days, with records from multiple individuals. Even if modeling full-resolution data were possible, using domain knowledge to identify biologically-relevant derived metrics (for example, computing fluke stroke rate from accelerometer or magnetometer data) should help reduce noise and limit focus to metrics most pertinent to the applied questions at hand, particularly for data streams like acceleration that comprise a mixture of signals with varying periodicity related to posture, gait, orientation, etc. [88, 90]. Pre-processing inputs in light of biological knowledge and hypotheses should increase the chances that identified behavioral states obtained from unsupervised classifiers like HMMs are biologically meaningful [74, 106]. In our case, for example, the sub-second resolution of acoustic and movement data from tag sensors was crucial to quantify behaviors such as echolocation pulse production, MSA, and fluke stroke rate, but these measures were then summarized at lower time resolution (here five-minute intervals), which both reduced the computational burden of model fitting and yielded more interpretable results.
The repetitive and time-consuming fitting process used here to try to ensure identification of a global likelihood maximum despite the difficulty of selecting adequate starting values for dozens of model parameters is not exemplary. However, it illustrates the challenges of model fitting for complex HMMs. As part of this analysis, we briefly considered applying a likelihood-weighting scheme to try to address some of these concerns about model fitting, although a simulation study clearly indicated the approach was not promising (as documented in the supplemental materials). Our experience highlights the true need for continued work on more efficient and robust optimization approaches for these and other complex ecological models [111–116], and also for reliable ways to determine whether a global maximum has really been found (which we cannot now affirm with true certainty).

## Biological interpretation
Behavioral responses of Ziphius to MFAS characterized in this study were broadly consistent with those observed in previous work, which have noted cessation of echolocation, extended deep dive duration, extension of dive cycle duration (or the time until re-initiation of echolocation-based foraging), and horizontal avoidance of the sound source, sometimes by fast or energetic swimming [30, 56, 57]. The Variant state identified by our HHMM can encompass all of those behaviors, since it typically has shorter echolocation periods and highly variable dive duration, dive cycle duration, and longer and more variable distance traveled, and indeed the dive cycles with the longest foraging dive, non-foraging dive, and dive cycle durations were all classed as Variant by the model (Fig. 2).
With “prolonged silencing or other cryptic behavior,” a switch to Variant state would score at least a severity 4 on Southall et al.’s severity scale for behavior changes affecting survival, with increased distance traveled hinting at possible avoidance (severity 5–6), but this effect is unconfirmed due to our uncertainty about MFAS source locations [14]. Similarly for responses related to feeding, Variant behavior would score, at minimum, a severity 4 due to extended time not spent foraging, although we did not have scope to quantify foraging success, energy expenditure, avoidance, or group cohesion adequately to judge whether higher severity levels were reached [14].
Variant behavior - whether initiated in response to MFAS or otherwise - typically lasts for multiple dive cycles (Fig. 6). This fact may help explain the somewhat surprising finding that the average dive cycle duration, foraging dive duration, and non-foraging dive duration were all shorter in Variant dive cycles (Fig. 2). Variant dive cycles that are coincident with MFAS exposure often more closely resemble the response behavior described in previous studies (i.e., with longer dives and dive cycles). In fact, the longest foraging dive (2.8 h) and non-foraging dives (1–1.6 h) observed had MFAS cSEL above 130 with only one exception - a 1.6 h non-foraging dive during which killer whale vocalizations were recorded (Table S1, Figure S11). However, the series of Variant dive cycles that follow an MFAS-exposed dive cycle often include fewer and shorter dives, with shorter echolocation periods, and are shorter overall (Figure S31). This pattern might reflect a compensatory increase in foraging rate after an initial disruption, a prolonged period of physiological limitation after a strenuous response, or factors related to displacement from habitat or altered social dynamics. While the current analysis doesn’t address these hypotheses, they certainly suggest avenues for further investigation.
Examination of the characteristics of the fine-scale states from the HHMM adds nuance to our understanding of Variant behavior and how it changes with MFAS: the model indicates that when in Variant state and exposed to MFAS, whales are increasingly expected to transition away from state 1 and into states 2 or 3 with rising cSEL (i.e., to cease echolocation-based foraging). In unexposed Variant dive cycles, whales are more likely to transition from state 4 (low activity) to state 3 (mixed activity) than they are in Typical, and less likely to persist in state 4. However, in Variant dive cycles with MFAS, individuals become less likely to transition to state 3 and more likely to persist in state 4 as cSEL rises (Fig. 5). An MFAS response with increased persistence in low-activity state 4 initially seems counter-intuitive, especially relative to the findings of prior behavioral response research which suggest whales tend to transition into more active states (e.g., higher swim speeds, prolonged directional travel) when exposed [52, 53, 57, 117]. However, since state 4 occurs predominantly in the bottom phase of non-foraging dives, the increased persistence in state 4 is likely related to the occurrence of more, longer non-foraging dives in MFAS-exposed Variant dive cycles, an effect that is most pronounced at higher cSEL values (Fig. 2). In the data, extended bouts in state 4 often followed the exposure period in the dive cycle (Figures S3-S17). Thus, the prevalence of this pattern does not preclude a more active short-term response and may in fact lend credence to the theory that response may include a brief but energetically costly response and an extended recovery period before foraging can resume.
This idea may, in fact, be evidenced by more subtle changes in the occurrence of fine-scale state 2 in Variant dive cycles. State 2 is a relatively infrequent, transient state characterized by directional movement with moderate, possibly bimodal fluke rate that typically occurs intermittently during ascents from foraging dives (Figs. 3, S3-S17, S34). State 2 might reflect the mixed gait that several beaked whale species perform during foraging-dive ascents as described by Martín López and colleagues, in which short periods of high-power “B-strokes” and glides are interspersed with periods of normal strokes, and which has been postulated to be a behavioral adaptation allowing beaked whales to surface efficiently with diminishing oxygen supplies following long, active submergences [103]. In a small sample of data, they found that B-strokes were more likely to occur when whales were exposed to MFAS, including on descent, suggesting beaked whales may engage in oxygen-saving strategies early in response and not just when their oxygen stores are already depleted [79]. While our model suggested that whales may become more likely to transition from state 1 into state 2 with rising MFAS cSEL, transition probabilities into state 2 from other states showed no obvious trend with cSEL. However, bouts of state 2 are brief and infrequent relative to other states, and the occurrence of state 2 varied considerably among and even sometimes within individuals. These facts may have diminished our ability to identify responses that include differential use of state 2. However, bouts of state 2 were notably more common outside of foraging dive ascents during Variant dive cycles, occurring on descent and at varying points in non-foraging dives, both with and without MFAS (Figures S3-S17, S34; Table S5). A more focused investigation of swimming gait and energetics as a function of exposure is warranted, given the findings of prior research, the changing context of state 2 during Variant dive cycles in our data, and its potential physiological implications.
Our models allowed us to estimate with uncertainty how much the chances of switching to Variant behavior rise with increasing MFAS cSEL: from an expected value of about 14% in the absence of detectable MFAS to about 46% at the highest MFAS cSEL observed here, 160.3 dB re 1 µPa2*sec (Fig. 3). Using the Viterbi algorithm to compute the most likely state sequence for each tag deployment, we can also estimate that of 52 dive cycles with MFAS exposure, whales switched to Variant behavior in 12 (23.1%), with an additional 6 instances where the whale was already in Variant state before the exposed dive cycle began (Table S3). If we consider only dive cycles where the MFAS cSEL was above 110 dB re 1µPa2s, then whales switched to Variant in 12 out of 40 MFAS-exposed dive cycles (30%). While it is difficult to compare across studies that detect responses and measure exposure intensity by differing methods, the proportion of behavioral responses detected here is quite a bit lower than the rate estimated in a study that exposed Ziphius to simulated MFAS sounds in an area where MFAS use is much less common [56]. Differences in responsiveness to real and simulated sources may play a role, as may the interplay between sound intensity, cumulative energy, source proximity, and the number and type of active sources; the training exercises that occur at SOAR are often complex, including more than one sound source of more than one type, most of which are mobile and capable of transmitting variable signals [30]. Past work has indicated greater responsiveness to mid-power, helicopter-deployed MFAS than to surface-ship MFAS, perhaps because of more unpredictable movements or source-level differences [30]; however, combined effects of multiple sources remain unknown, and unfortunately source-type details could not be analyzed in the current observational study. Alternatively, this result may hint that Ziphius near SOAR, at least some of which appear to be year-round residents to the area [118, 119], display adaptation or habituation to MFAS sounds.
It has been argued that responsiveness should be highest in areas where an anthropogenic stimulus (such as MFAS sounds) are completely novel, and that animals may eventually habituate, or at least learn to respond less often or intensely [65, 120]. But even if the whales in this study are truly less responsive to MFAS than whales in more pristine habitats, it does not always imply there is lesser impact, since animals weigh the cost and feasibility of going elsewhere, which may depend on how much they have invested in establishing territory and knowledge of their current habitat [121]. Long-term, low-level feeding reduction could impact reproduction and offspring survival [64, 122], and prey quality and availability may be highly heterogeneous across Ziphius habitats in our study region, such that relatively small shifts in habitat use have disproportionately high energetic cost [123, 124]. The recurrent cost of frequent responses could also decrease an animal’s reactive scope, limiting their ability to react to additional threats, including predators, and eventually leading to chronic stress symptoms [125]. Thus, chronic exposure to acoustic disturbance with low-level reactions may also affect individual fitness via impacts on interconnected physiological, genetic, and cellular processes [1, 126, 127].
The Variant state characterized by the HHMM is not constrained to occur only during and after exposure to MFAS, and in fact, according to the model, transitions from Typical to Variant dive cycles can be expected with a probability of about 0.14 (Fig. 3) in conditions without known or detectable MFAS exposure. At least some of these transitions may be associated with other anthropogenic activities that were not included in the model; for example, many of these tags captured impulsive sounds that are likely from in-water or near-surface explosions or high-velocity projectiles striking the water surface during Variant dive cycles (S3-4, S9-13). This possibility leads to a subtle distinction between the probability of state transition as a function of MFAS cSEL (Figs. 2 and 4) and a traditional dose-response curve. In a traditional dose-response curve, any response observed is assumed to be caused by the stimulus of interest. Additionally, in marine mammal behavioral response studies, the response is often defined to be 0 when the stimulus is not present (or is below some threshold level not expected to lead to response) [54, 72, 128, 129]. In contrast, the HHMM transition-probability approach here allows estimation of how often Variant state occurs outside known exposure to MFAS, and thus relies on the fundamentally different assumption that behavior indistinguishable from response can occur in the absence of the stimulus of interest. This assumption might be more reasonable in cases where data are from opportunistic exposure studies like ours, and even in an experimental setting where control or knowledge of conditions is not sufficient to justify the assumption that observed responses are only caused by the stimulus [15, 16]. Regardless, it is important to draw a distinction between the curves quantifying transition rates as a function of MFAS cSEL in our study and true dose-response curves.
A particularly noteworthy example of the potential for stimuli other than MFAS to alter behavior is the one dive cycle in our dataset where killer whale calls were detected on the tag recording (and other anthropogenic sounds, including MFAS, were not). The HHMM indicated a switch from Typical to Variant state during this dive cycle (Figure S11), consistent with the risk-disturbance hypothesis that suggests animals may respond to anthropogenic disturbance in a manner that is consistent with their response to predators [15, 58, 65]. However, while the model classified the dive-cycle as atypical, the fine-scale states suggest this response to predator calls also differed from responses to MFAS. The whale was on descent into a shallow dive when the first calls were received; it entered state 4 and leveled out at ~ 350 m depth, as is typical for a non-foraging dive. The calls continued, and about 30 min into the dive the whale returned to state 3, but rather than surfacing, it began to descend. At ~ 1000 m the whale entered state 4 and appeared to drift to the bottom at 1800 m, ultimately conducting a 99-minute silent, deep, low-energy dive - the only dive of its kind in the dataset - returning to the surface about 15 min after the final call was received. The dive cycle was unusually long, at 8.1 h, but the whale displaced less than a kilometer over that time. In contrast, many MFAS exposed dives, especially unusually long, deep dives that are coincident with MFAS signals (as opposed to the dives that follow them in the same cycle), feature unusual use of state 2 and higher displacement rates (Figures S3-S30).
Comparing models using MFAS RMS level and cSEL as exposure metrics indicated clearly that the cSEL models fit better. This intriguing finding suggests that whale behavior changes integrate recent exposure information to some extent, rather than depending strongly on concurrent or instantaneous exposure intensity. The finding that total sound energy better correlates with response probability and intensity is consistent with recent work suggesting that sperm whales (Physeter macrocephalus) respond similarly to continuous and pulsed naval sonar with equal total energy per pulse [71]. However, use of a cumulative SEL metric presents challenges, because it requires researchers to choose an interval over which to cumulate exposure. In the context of this observational study where exposures sometimes consisted of multiple sources overlapped in time and defining discrete bouts of exposure was somewhat arbitrary, we chose the dive cycle as a cumulation interval with clear potential for biological relevance. However, we recognize that compared to approaches that cumulate cSEL over 24 h or over known exposure bouts, our approach might result in lower cSEL if whales continue to forage (by resetting cSEL at the start of each dive cycle even if MFAS transmission continues from the previous cycle). However, the interval that whales actually attend or respond to, and an interval amenable for potential regulatory or mitigation purposes, remains a subject for further work. Meanwhile, exposure levels in terms of both RMS and cSEL are reported for all individuals studied here (Supplemental Materials).
One strength of the HHMM approach is that it facilitates estimation of the duration of bouts of Variant behavior. Since at least some Variant behavior is apparently within the natural behavioral repertoire of Ziphius even in the absence of anthropogenic disturbance, and it can and does occur even without MFAS or other detectable anthropogenic sounds, Variant duration is not equivalent to MFAS response duration. However, Variant duration does provide a useful benchmark, with our estimated median expected Variant duration of 2 dive cycles (IQR 2) or 9 (IQR 9.2) hours, consistent with previous supposition that beaked whale MFAS behavioral responses typically last several hours but usually less than 24 h [52, 55–57].
A longstanding challenge in the study of whale behavioral responses to acoustic disturbance is the need to balance data resolution and tag deployment duration, with many longer-duration satellite tags collecting position and depth data (typically at lower temporal resolution) over weeks to months, and high-resolution movement tags (such as DTAGs, CATS tags, Acousondes and others) collecting data many times per second and sometimes incorporating acoustic recordings and cameras, but typically limited to a day or less of recording time. These differences mean that different tag types capture data at very different spatial and temporal resolutions [130], as do other possible data sources that may be used to detect responses, such as passive acoustic recorders or surface visual observations. Leveraging the complementary strengths of such different data types in a common analytical framework is an appealing prospect, but simply summarizing or down-sampling higher-resolution data to the lowest common sampling rate may not make best use of the highest-resolution data streams. The SMRT data analyzed here, recorded at sub-second resolution but over days to weeks, begins to bridge the gap between shorter-duration, higher-resolution tags and longer-duration, lower-resolution tags, and leveraging the richness of the SMRT data while coping with such large datasets is both an analytical challenge and opportunity.
Recent simulation studies have indicated that sample size (number of individuals and exposure events) matters more than tag type and resolution in accurately estimating dose-response relationships, assuming that responses are equally detectable across different types of tags and exposure level can be estimated with reasonable accuracy [72]. But those conditions remain quite restrictive, since development of methods to detect and characterize marine mammal behavioral responses remains an active research area [109, 110, 131], and depending on whether tags record acoustic and geolocation data and whether sound sources are under experimental control, source-animal range and/or received sound levels often have notable uncertainty [132, 133]. The HHMM approach might offer one way to integrate data from both high-resolution movement tags and location/depth only tags, by treating the fine-scale behavior observations that are not available from lower-resolution tags as missing data [73], although the challenge of identifying foraging dives without acoustic and/or accelerometer data would remain [95], and such integration might best be implemented in a Bayesian framework able to accommodate varying uncertainty in input data and covariates.
Key areas for future work building on this study include analysis of additional datasets to differentiate effects of sonar duty cycle (i.e., pulsed versus continuously-active mid-frequency sonar [71]), and to compare and contrast MFAS effects with those of other signals detected on the tags, such as the impulsive sounds generated by explosives and objects impacting the water surface [134], and echosounders, some of which have been shown to alter beaked whale behavior elsewhere [50, 135, 136]. In addition, it will be important to explore whether and how sound-source to whale distance, sound level, or both modulate responsiveness [5, 35]. Such work will likely require models capable of dealing with measurement error and missing data, since for observational studies, associating each exposure detected on a tag recording with a specific sound source of precisely known location is very difficult, and both sound sources and whales may be in motion for extended periods. Even in controlled experiments where accurate source positions are available, whale location and received sound level may not be precisely known (especially if tags without acoustic recording capability are used), underscoring the need for methods that deal with potential measurement uncertainty or missing data in both source-whale range and sound level [132].
A benefit of HMM approaches is that they are generative, allowing simulation of hypothetical datasets based on the fitted model, and perhaps facilitating the crucial work of linking shorter-term behavioral changes in response to disturbance with expected longer-term changes in vital rates, fitness, and population consequences [137]. The HHMM presented here could provide useful long-run average estimates of key inputs such as probability and duration of response or lost feeding time [138, 139], including crucial information about variation and uncertainty [140, 141]. However, model-based simulations would be less realistic at finer temporal resolution, since the model ignores physical and physiological constraints and does not account for all patterns in the input data streams (Figure S40). If greater fidelity in simulation were desired, directions for exploration could include introduction of feedback and constraints, or inclusion of additional covariates with perhaps non-linear effects [113, 142–144]. Previous studies of Ziphius illustrate inter-individual differences in dive characteristics including dive durations and inter-deep-dive intervals [39, 40, 44]. It is also notable that the prevalence of Variant behavior differed considerably among whales, with some individuals rarely entering the Variant state and others seldom leaving it (Table 1). Thus, an interesting model extension might be to account for whale-to-whale variation in either state-dependent characteristics, rates of transition between states, or the magnitude of the MFAS effect; however, modeling such variation would require an even larger dataset, and in previous work, the variance in dive features explainable by individual random effects was comparable to the variance explained by factors such as site and time of day [40]. So, as more data become available, consideration of additional contextual covariates might be as fruitful as incorporating individual random effects while potentially yielding more mechanistic insight; such covariates might include diurnal and lunar cycles, habitat characteristics, individual characteristics and life history, and social context [145].

## Conclusions
In this study, we analyzed high-resolution movements and acoustic recordings from 13 individual Ziphius that were tagged on a United States Navy training range. This unique dataset has allowed us to examine the behavioral repertoire of these whales at multiple scales, for days to weeks at a time, as they move through an environment which includes many anthropogenic sounds, not least of which is a military sonar previously associated with mass strandings of this species elsewhere in the world. The richness of this dataset presented both opportunities and challenges, but finally allowed a nuanced characterization of goose-beaked behavior with and without MFAS.
Both the data and HHMM framework presented here have relevance for conservation and management, enhancing our understanding of Ziphius behavioral responses to realistic, operational MFAS exposures in an area where naval training activities occur regularly. Going forward, these analysis tools can aid in assessing responses to other acoustic disturbance, as well as linking shorter-term behavior changes to longer-term consequences, in this and other species.

## Figure Fig. 2: HHMM dive-cycle characteristics. The HHMM defines two states (Typical and Variant) at the dive-cycle scale. Grey lines show characteristics of these states according to the fitted HHMM; for each state in each panel, 100 traces were obtained via a parametric bootstrap and are overlaid to indicate uncertainty in the state-dependent distributions for each input data stream. Also overlaid on each distribution are the observed values from the data, shown as grey circles for dive cycles with no detected MFAS and colored by cSEL otherwise. Each observed dive cycle was classed as either Typical or Variant via the Viterbi algorithm

## Figure Fig. 3: HHMM transition probabilities at dive-cycle timescale as a function of MFAS cSEL. Black lines show expected relationships, with grey shaded areas indicating 95% confidence intervals. Black tick-marks on the horizontal axes show observed cSEL values from the data. Dots at cSEL of 0 indicate the expected transition probabilities with no detected MFAS, with error bars indicating 95% confidence intervals

## Figure Fig. 4: HHMM five-minute-interval characteristics. The HHMM defines four states at the five-minute scale, whose state-dependent distributions are identical in Typical and Variant states. Grey lines show characteristics of these states according to the fitted HHMM; for each state in each panel, 100 traces were obtained via a parametric bootstrap and are overlaid to indicate uncertainty in the state-dependent distributions for each input data stream. Also overlaid on each distribution are the observed values from the data, shown as grey circles for intervals with no detected MFAS (so far in the current dive cycle) and colored by RMS RL otherwise. Each five-minute interval was classified as belonging to one of the four states via the Viterbi algorithm

## Figure Fig. 5: HHMM Transition probabilities at five-minute timescale. In the best model, the rates of transition between the four states depend on cSEL in the Variant state; these relationships are shown by the black lines, with grey shaded areas indicating 95% confidence intervals. The grey ticks along x-axes indicate the cSEL values observed in the dataset (at five-minute scale). Rates of transition between states for cSEL = 0 are shown for the Typical dive-cycle-scale state (+ symbols) and the Variant dive-cycle-scale state (open triangles) (for these, 95% confidence intervals are omitted for clarity as they barely extend beyond the symbols)
