Intraspecific variation of cochlear morphology in bowhead and beluga whales.

# Intraspecific variation of cochlear morphology in bowhead and beluga whales

## Abstract
AbstractThe bony labyrinth of the petrosal bone, a distinctive feature of mammal skulls, is often identified in micro‐computed tomography imaging to infer species' physiological and ecological traits. When done as part of a comparative study, one individual specimen is normally considered representative of a species, and intraspecific variation is considered low. Yet tests of intraspecific variability have been performed on few species and on limited morphological traits. Studies of intraspecific variability are not only valuable to help us assess the need for multiple specimens in comparative work, but relative levels of variability can also be used to reveal insights into a trait's functional significance. In this study, we report measurements of intraspecific variation on two cetaceans with vastly different auditory specializations, a low‐frequency specialized mysticete and an echolocating odontocete. We examine the internal structures of the cochlea in beluga and bowhead whales and relate this to their hearing abilities. Overall levels of intraspecific variability are higher in the bowhead than the beluga, reflecting the more specialized auditory system of the latter. However, the levels of variation differ through the length of the cochlea (base to apex) and these appear to reflect known frequency specializations of the species, with the bowhead having lower variation in some measurements at the low‐frequency apical end than the beluga.

## INTRODUCTION
The bony labyrinth of the petrosal bone is one of the most distinctive, easily recognizable features of the mammal skull, including in cetaceans. As such, it is often identified in micro‐computed tomography (μCT) imaging and used to infer knowledge of a species' physiological and ecological traits (see Racicot, 2022). The bony labyrinth makes up the outer wall of the inner ear, from which information can be gleaned on the hearing and vestibular systems via analysis of the shape of the cochlea and semicircular canals, respectively (see Ekdale, 2016). The inner ear is filled with fluid and contains specialized cells (hair cells) that respond to movement of the fluid. The anatomy and function of the inner ear are well‐studied and described (e.g., see Bohne & Harding, 2008; Mescher, 2021).
Within the cochlea, there is a row of cells running from the base to the apex which are arranged such that the more basal cells respond to lower frequency movements, the apical cells to higher frequencies, and all others in a continuum in between (Von Bekesy, 1960). These cells sit in the endolymph‐filled scala media, which is separated from the perilymph‐filled scala vestibuli by a soft tissue membrane (Reissner's membrane) and by the perilymph‐filled scala tympani (ST) by the basilar membrane (BM) and by bony intrusions into the cochlea known as the spiral laminae (see Figure 1a–c for illustrations).

While the detection of fluid motion is performed by soft tissue structures that are not normally preserved and do not fossilize, much information on cochlear function can be gleaned from the bony structures, including in cetaceans (e.g., Mourlam & Orliac, 2017; Nummela et al., 2007). Features that are known to be functionally important include the laminar gap (which shows the width of the BM and other supporting tissue) and the area of Rosenthal's (spiral ganglion) canal. The width of the laminar gap (or BM) is important since this helps determine its stiffness (Von Bekesy, 1960): all else being equal, a wider membrane is more responsive to lower frequencies and a narrower membrane to higher frequencies. The area of Rosenthal's canal can tell us about the number of ganglion cells, which relay information from the hair cells to the auditory brainstem. A larger canal means more cells (Johnson et al., 2011; Sensor et al., 2015) and potentially better ability to discriminate between frequencies. Other features that may be functionally important include the relative dimensions of the ST and scala vestibuli + scala media (SVM). Since pressure waves travel through these canals, their size and shape may have functional effects that are possibly different for different frequencies. All of these features have previously been measured in a broad range of mammals, including cetaceans and terrestrial artiodactyls (Peacock, 2025; Peacock et al., 2025), and are readily visible in μCT scans when optimized with suitable resolution and contrast.
The morphology of the ear is well‐studied in modern and fossil taxa, but the function of its many complex features is not fully understood. One method that may help determine the functional importance of a feature is to measure and quantify levels of intraspecific variation. The degree of variability in a trait can potentially tell us how tightly constrained a morphology is: more variability indicating a lack of selective pressure, a hypothesis discussed by Darwin (1859). A few studies exist that have exploited inner ear variation to explore inner ear function, including studies of xenarthrans showing increased variation in the vestibular system in slow‐moving sloths (Billet et al., 2012), and differences in the degree of variability in slow‐moving versus agile primates (Gonzales et al., 2019; Perier et al., 2016). Exploration of intraspecific variability to examine cochlear function has been more limited, but we would hypothesize lower levels of variability in species that are auditory specialists, and in morphological features that have a more direct function in audition. In other words: we hypothesize that features with strong links to specific functions will have lower intraspecific variability than those that do not, and thus, if a feature displays low variation, it could be hypothesized that it is under tight functional constraints. This could suggest avenues for further study. In particular, many palaeontological studies examine the evolution of hearing specializations (or other features) via principal component analysis (PCA) on morphological data. While this can be useful to determine patterns of variation and find correlations, it is often done without linking these morphologies to physiology. Thus, while these studies may find correlations, the lack of any direct link to function can limit the usefulness of the results and potentially lead to erroneous conclusions. Efforts at better understanding how morphology determines physiology would benefit the interpretation of fossil data, and examination of intraspecific variation in modern taxa is one method we can use to help clarify functional importance.
Intraspecific variation in the cochlea has been examined by a few authors, mainly to test the assumption of low variability between species and determine whether a single specimen can be considered representative for the species. This is particularly important in paleontological studies where only a single specimen may exist for a species or in studies of harder‐to‐acquire species such as cetaceans where small sample sizes are normal (Churchill et al., 2016; Ekdale & Racicot, 2015; Luo & Marsh, 1996; Mourlam & Orliac, 2017; Park et al., 2016; Peacock et al., 2025; Racicot et al., 2025).
Studies of intraspecific cochlear variation generally support the low variability assumption, and thus that the use of a single specimen to represent a species can produce meaningful comparative results (Ekdale & Rowe, 2011). A few authors have examined intraspecific variation in gross cochlear morphology of cetaceans. Martins et al. (2020) reported low coefficient of variation (CV) values for the harbor porpoise (Phocoena phocoena), and further showed how the level of variation between odontocete species was considerably larger than within their sample. However, in terms of principal component scores, the range of variation for a principal component was commonly a quarter to a third of the total variation across all odontocetes, indicating that studies based on one cetacean cochlear specimen could significantly bias the position of its species within the cetacean morphological diversity cloud. Racicot et al. (2018) studied several measurements of the monodontid cochlea (narwhals [Monodon monoceros] and belugas [Delphinapterus leucas]), and found all specimens to occupy a tightly constrained area of the PCA morphospace, separate from mysticetes but overlapping with other odontocetes. Schnitzler et al. (2017) examined the sperm whale cochlea (Physeter macrocephalus) and found that the variation allowed specimens from different geographical regions to be distinguishable.
Age‐related variation is another possible concern in morphological studies. Although evidence supports that the ear is generally assumed to show limited postnatal development (see, e.g., Lancaster et al., 2015), no broad comparative surveys examining age are available, and changes in most morphological features with age have not been examined. Commonly, it is difficult to estimate the age of wild‐caught specimens, and this potential source of intraspecific variation cannot be addressed. Overall, the number of species and morphological features examined for intraspecific variation remains low and, given the difficulties in acquiring cetacean material, studies in other species would be useful.
The bowhead (Balaena mysticetus) and beluga (D. leucas) are two species of arctic whale. The beluga is an odontocete (toothed whale) with a known hearing range extending as high as 150 kHz (Castellote et al., 2014; Finneran et al., 2005; Mooney et al., 2018), while the bowhead is a mysticete (baleen whale) known as a highly vocal species producing complex songs (Stafford, 2022), and which has a hearing range that may not exceed 20–30 kHz, although recent measurements have suggested mysticete hearing may extend to higher frequencies than previously thought (Houser et al., 2024; Peacock, 2025). Cochleas of mysticetes and odontocetes differ morphologically in consistent ways, most notably with the odontocetes having a looser, less tightly coiled cochlea (see, e.g., Fleischer, 1976; Ketten, 2000). Features of bowhead and beluga ears have been described by various authors (Ekdale & Racicot, 2015; Morell et al., 2020; Peacock, 2025; Racicot et al., 2018; Rehorek et al., 2019; Sensor et al., 2015, 2018; Waugh et al., 2023).
All odontocetes are specialized for using high‐frequency echolocation, and we would thus predict them to display less intraspecific variability in the anatomical areas of importance for high frequency perception than non‐echolocating mysticetes. High frequencies are detected at the base of the cochlea and low frequencies at the apex; thus, differences in morphological variation at different locations may reflect the frequency specialization of different taxa. An examination of the variability in these structures may allow us to assess a correlation that may be suggestive of a causal relation.
In this work, we made use of μCT scanning to extract quantitative data on the internal structure of the cochlea in dried and fixed ears of bowhead and beluga whales. We use this to quantify the level of intraspecific variation in these features, and examine what this reveals about the hearing ability of each species.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
We made use of bowhead and beluga specimens harvested by the Iñupiat people of Utqiagvik and Point Lay. These animals were taken as part of the indigenous subsistence harvest, with petrosal bones being collected by the Department of Wildlife Management of the North Slope Borough under NOAA‐NMFS 17350‐02. All specimens were isolated ear bones that had been taken from the animal within hours of death. Specimens were immediately fixed in neutral buffered 2.5% glutaraldehyde or (rarely) in 10% neutral buffered formalin. Two beluga specimens were isolated petrosals that were recovered from a bone pile, and no information is known about these animals (age, sex, length, etc.).
Our sample included 10 bowheads and 10 belugas; with one of each species being from a near‐term fetus. A full list of specimen ID numbers, age, length, and sex data are given in Table 1. For the bowheads, age is estimated from baleen plate length, and beluga age is estimated from counts of growth layer groups in their teeth. Note that bowheads only become fully mature around 20–25 years, compared to ~10 years for belugas, and thus our bowhead sample may not contain any fully mature specimens. Taking the specimens for which we have data, half were female and half were male.
Specimen ID numbers, age, length, and sex (where available). Both fetuses were near‐birth. All specimens and scan data are currently curated at Northeast Ohio Medical University.
We scanned each specimen using a Skyscan 1273 μCT scanner (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) with scanning parameters optimized to achieve maximum resolution and contrast for each individual specimen. Scan cubic voxel sizes are given in Table 1. From these, we extracted linear measurements using both Avizo 3D Pro (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and ImageJ (Schindelin et al., 2012).
We used the same data extraction process as described by Peacock et al. (2025) and Peacock (2025). Using Avizo's spline function, we fit a line to the distal tip of the primary spiral lamina (PSL) along its full length, and took the full length of this as length of the BM. This allows us to make use of bony landmarks to define BM length and allows for greatest consistency between specimens. This will be longer than the true functional length of the membrane, which will not extend to the most basal or apical portions. However, we also measure the inner curve of the spiral, which will be shorter than the outer curve, thus potentially underestimating its length compared to other possible methods.
After fitting the line to the PSL's tip, we used Avizo's trajectory function to extract 50 equally spaced slices lying perpendicular to this line along its entire length. From these, we made the following linear and area measurements:Laminar gap: The distance from the distal tip of the PSL to either the distal tip of the secondary spiral lamina (SSL) or the shortest distance to the wall of the cochlea (Geisler & Luo, 1996).Rosenthal's canal area: The cross‐sectional area of Rosenthal's (spiral ganglion) canal.PSL width: The width of the PSL measured from its distal tip to the edge of Rosenthal's canal.SSL width: The width of the SSL measured from its distal tip to it merges with the wall of the cochlea.PSL thickness and SSL thickness: The thickness of the PSL and SSL is measured halfway along and perpendicular to its width measurement.SVM area: The cross‐sectional area of the combined SVM. These two areas are combined since the boundary between them, Reissner's membrane, cannot be determined from bony morphology. The boundary between the SVM and the ST is defined based on the laminar gap.ST area: The cross‐sectional area of the ST, with the boundary between it and the SVM defined as above.

All measurements were made by the lead author for greater consistency. An example CT slice with each of the measurements marked is shown in Figure 1d–f. To quantify variation, we calculated the CV: standard deviation/sample mean, and expressed as a percentage. A higher CV value indicates higher variability. CV is a useful measure since it is normalized to the mean for each species, which allows for comparison between populations with very different means. In our case, the beluga and bowhead differ greatly in overall body mass, and this could be expected to lead to much larger values in one than in the other.
For each individual animal, we calculated the mean values of each measure over five slices covering 10% of the distance from base to apex, that is, we calculated the mean laminar gap for the basal 10%, the next 11%–20% and so on. The CV was then calculated from the grand mean of all individual animals, giving 10 CV values covering the full length of the BM. We see jumps in CV in the most basal and apical areas in some measurements, and part of this may also be due to measurement “error.” It can be difficult to define exactly where the PSL ends and begins, and these are often areas where more rapid changes in certain features take place (as the BM appears and disappears). These may also not be functionally important. It is not possible to ensure that each specimen is precisely aligned at the base or apex, and this may account for a portion of the increased variability. For this reason, we chose to ignore the first and last slice in all calculations. A better method of determining the start and end of the BM would prove useful to minimize any such errors.
All data analysis was performed in R (R Core Team, 2023), and all figures were plotted using ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016).

## RESULTS
All measurement data can be found in Table S1.

## Basilar membrane length
The length of the BM in bowheads ranged from 49.3 to 57.9 mm, with a mean length of 53 mm, while the beluga BM ranged from 42.5 to 51.8 mm, with a mean length of 48 mm. The coefficient of variation in the BM length was calculated as 5.7% for both species. Curiously, the bowhead fetus had the longest BM out of the 10 individuals measured (1.4 mm longer than the second longest), while the beluga fetus has by far the shortest BM length of all belugas, being 3.5 mm shorter than the next shortest. However, no overall patterns between length and age were apparent in either species.

## Laminar gap and Rosenthal's canal area
The width of the laminar gap shows distinctly different, non‐overlapping patterns in the two species (Figure 2a). In bowheads, there is a rapid increase in width near the base, which then plateaus at 25% of the distance to the apex, above which is a more gradual increase. In belugas, we see a more gradual increase until 75% of the distance toward the apex, above which is a more exponential width change. The CV shows more variability is present at the base in the bowhead than the beluga (Figure 2b). However, the bowhead CV decreases rapidly and is lower than that of the beluga for most of the cochlea's length. At the apex, the bowhead thus shows less variability than the beluga.

The area of Rosenthal's canal is overall greater in beluga than bowhead (Figure 2c). It follows a typical pattern in beluga where it increases in size from the base to a maximum, plateaus at this maximum value, then decreases toward the apex. This is not exactly what is seen in the bowhead: while there is an increase in size at the base, it becomes messier toward the apex and does not show a typical decrease in area. Based on calculation of CV, the beluga canal area is considerably less variable than the bowhead (Figure 2d). CV is ~15% at the base in beluga but falls to less than 10% for most of the BM length, while in the bowhead, it is never below 15% and approaches 25% at the base.

## Primary and secondary spiral lamina
The PSL width remains constant for much of the BM's length in both species (Figure 3a). In the bowhead, the PSL width is greater than that of the beluga at the apex, but this decreases until both are of equal width at around 40%–50% distance. Above this, the bowhead PSL appears to increase slightly in width, while the beluga continues to show a gradual decrease until ~80% distance, where the width falls dramatically toward zero. Similar patterns can be seen in the CV data (Figure 3b), where the bowhead CV is around 15% at the base, decreases to a minimum of <5% at around 40%–50% distance, and then increases again to a maximum of 15%–20% at the apex. The beluga has lower CV values of 6%–7% until around 75% distance, when it increases dramatically to above 20% at the apex.

The overall thickness values for the PSL overlap between both species (Figure 3c). In the bowhead, it is thicker at the base and gradually thins toward the apex, while the beluga is thinner than the bowhead at the base, thickens to a maximum at the center, then thins toward the apex. The variability in the bowhead PSL thickness is consistently greater than that seen in the beluga (Figure 3d). In the bowhead, the CV is never below 10% and approaches 20% at the apex, while in the beluga it is never greater than 10%. In both species, there appears to be a trend toward greater variability toward the apex.
An ossified SSL is not present along the full length of the BM in either species. Based on its visibility on CT slices, an SSL is present for ~20% of the length in the bowhead, and ~75% of the length in the beluga (Figure 4a); this percentage extent of the SSL is also somewhat more variable in the bowhead than the beluga. The peak SSL width values are greater in the bowhead (at the base) but then decline rapidly as we move along the length of the BM and the SSL disappears. In the beluga, the SSL width remains fairly constant at ~0.5 mm until around halfway along the BM, above which it declines in width before disappearing. The variability in the SSL width measurements is very high for the bowhead, where CV is 20%–45%, but much smaller in the beluga, where it is ~5% at the base, then increasing to 15% before it disappears (Figure 4b).

## Scala vestibuli/scala media, and scala tympani
The combined area of the SVM is greater in the bowhead than the beluga along the entire length of the BM (Figure 5a). It decreases from 5 to 10 mm2 at the base to ~3 mm2 and then remains relatively constant for the remainder of the BM length. The belugas all show the same pattern whereby the SVM area sharply declines to a minimum at 5%–10% distance, then increases slightly to reach a peak at 50% distance, before declining toward a minimum at the apex. Variability in the bowhead SVM area is also consistently higher than the beluga; however, they do converge at the apex (Figure 5b). SVM is below 12.5% for the full length of the BM in the beluga, while in the bowhead it is around 18% at the base and falls to around 12.5% at the apex.

The ST area appears more similar in absolute size between species, although at any given distance along the BM the beluga ST is always smaller than the bowhead (Figure 5c). The ST area was only recorded where a fully enclosed area could be defined in a given CT slice; this meant that for the first few slices no area was recorded for both species: no bowhead data are recorded for the basal 20% and no beluga data for the basal 10%, mainly due to the large size of the perilymphatic duct. At the point where the first fully enclosed area could be measured, we see an area of 10 mm2 in both species, which then decreases at a seemingly constant rate until we reach 75% of the distance along the BM. Then, for the apical most quarter, the bowhead ST area remains constant at around 3 mm2, while in the beluga it drastically declines to less than 1 mm2. The variability in the ST area shows a similar pattern to SVM area (Figure 5d). CV is near constant around 10% in the beluga but then shows a sizable increase to nearly 25% at the apex, is larger (20%–25%) at the base in the bowhead but then declines to a minimum of around 12% at the apex.
The ratio of SVM to ST area shows a similar pattern between both species (Figure 5e) in that the ratio starts small (i.e., relatively large ST) but increases toward the apex (as the ST reduces in area much more than the SVM). In the bowhead, the SVM/ST ratio seems to settle at a value of around 1 as we approach the apex; however, in the beluga it settles at a lower value before increasing rapidly at around 75% of the BM length (corresponding to the sudden shift in ST area seen in Figure 5c). The variability in the SVM/ST ratio (Figure 5f) unsurprisingly bears similarity to the variability in both SVM and ST area. In the beluga, CV values are below 10% except at the most basal and apical parts, where it increases to greater than 20%. In the bowhead, there is a steady decrease in SVM/ST variability when moving along the BM, with higher variability (CV of ~30%) at the base and lower variability (CV close to 10%) at the apex.

## DISCUSSION

## Intraspecific variation
Our sample included animals with a wide range of ages (including a near‐term fetus for each species), lengths, and included a sample of both sexes. We could not find any pattern in any measurement we made that allowed us to distinguish specimens based on any of these parameters.
While it is always preferable to make use of multiple individuals in biological studies, by necessity only a single specimen is often available. This study provides data on the bowhead and beluga whale useful to determine whether multiple specimens are necessary for comparative work on some structures of the inner ear; however, whether a CV value is low enough to justify single specimens will have to be determined for each individual study depending on the species sampled, morphological traits being examined, and research questions being asked. For most measures, our calculated CV values were less than 30%, and generally even lower. For the beluga, calculated CV values are typically below 10%, while variability is mostly greater in the bowhead. We suspect this lower variation will be a shared feature of all odontocetes (see Section 4.2).
Our sample included one fetal specimen from each species, neither of which showed any consistent pattern of divergence from the adult specimens. The bowhead fetus had the longest cochlea in our bowhead sample, and the beluga fetus the overall smallest in our sample; however, we found no correlation between size and age. It is generally assumed that the functional parts of the ear show little to no postnatal development, and this has been confirmed in studies of dolphins (Lancaster et al., 2015); however, at least one study (Bisconti, 2001) has suggested significant postnatal growth in the external dimensions of the fin whale ear (Balaenoptera physalus), while the cochlea was not studied. Indeed, bowhead tympanics increase in size with age by depositing annual growth layers (Sensor et al., 2018). We were unable to find consistent patterns between any part of our data and the animal age. Thus, our data further support the conclusion of minimal changes in the internal structure of the cochlea between fetal and adult bowhead and beluga whales. One limitation of our study is that we only measure individuals from one specific population of each species. It may be the case that different populations diverge slightly from our sample as with the sperm whale (Schnitzler et al., 2017).
An additional point of note is the length of the beluga BM, which appears to be remarkably long for the animal's body size. In comparing BM length to body mass, a previous study of 51 mammal species showed the beluga as a positive outlier (Peacock, 2025), and all 10 of the animals measured for this paper would lie above the regression line reported in that work. Peacock (2025) reported the BM length in the bowhead as being consistent with its body mass (i.e., it did not deviate significantly from the regression line across all mammals), and the bowhead data reported in this paper do not alter this result.
While the mean BM length is clearly longer in the bowhead than the beluga, the range of lengths does overlap. This is despite the bowhead being considerably more massive than the beluga. We also find that the bowhead has a much wider laminar gap than the beluga, but it is well known that odontocetes have unusually narrow gaps for their body size (see Peacock, 2025) and that this is an adaptive trait to facilitate higher frequency hearing. In our other measurements, the smaller beluga has a larger Rosenthal's canal than the bowhead, while the PSL and SSL dimensions partly overlap between the species. SVM area is clearly larger in the bowhead, but ST areas also overlap. Thus, body size does not appear to be a clear predictor of these measurements.

## Variation and hearing ability
We find greater overall variability in the bowhead in all features studied, and this is perhaps not surprising since the beluga, like all odontocetes, is well recognized as an auditory specialist: odontocete whales all echolocate; they have adapted to hear extremely high frequencies and process this sound in sophisticated ways to extract information on a target. Lower morphological variability would be expected in a species that is specialized to make more precise use of sound. In this instance, greater variation in a feature could indicate lower levels of functional importance, and thus, weaker selective pressure to maintain a particular morphology.
Comparing the laminar gap between the two species, the bowheads show less variation at the apex, while the belugas show less variation at the base. This could be interpreted as being in keeping with their known hearing specializations: the most important frequencies for bowheads are detected at the apex, while the opposite is true for belugas. The general trend in the laminar gap may also tell us something about the auditory specializations of each species. A narrower laminar gap is maintained across a longer length of the BM for odontocetes, where it only shows significant widening above 75% of the distance to the apex. In bowheads, the gap rapidly expands and is wide for most of its length. This may indicate that each species is devoting a longer segment of the membrane to the higher or lower frequencies. This different laminar gap pattern between mysticetes and odontocetes has been noted in other studies (Peacock, 2025), which have also shown that the whale laminar gap profile differs from all other mammal species. However, the bowhead laminar gap was also measured by Ekdale and Racicot (2015) and, while the pattern they show is similar to ours, this was different from other mysticete species they measured. This highlights the importance of broad comparative surveys to limit misinterpretation of results.
We also see some similar patterns in the area of the two scalae and their ratio, where the variability in the bowhead shows a steady decline toward the apex, and the beluga shows a sharp increase in variability at the apex. For the bowhead, the variability in both scalae actually appears to fall rapidly until ~50% of the distance toward the apex, above which it either plateaus or decreases slightly. This could indicate a greater importance in maintaining this fixed area (and thus volume and mass) around the apical end, where the most important frequencies for the species are detected. The mass of fluid would play a role in determining the behavior of the BM. However, it remains unclear how the changing areas of the scalae along the cochlea's length would matter.
A relatively large Rosenthal's canal is well known in odontocetes where it bulges into the ST (Fleischer, 1976; Geisler & Luo, 1996). This is regarded as an adaptive trait to improve frequency discrimination by increasing the ratio of ganglion cells to hair cells. We would expect the area to at least be relatively larger in odontocetes, and counts of cells within the canal have shown a greater number per mm in beluga as opposed to bowheads (Sensor et al., 2015; Waugh et al., 2023). However, we might also expect the canal to show less variability in odontocetes due to the greater importance of accurate frequency discrimination as opposed to mysticetes, and this is what our data show. Rosenthal's canal in odontocetes has an extremely well‐defined ossified wall, while in mysticetes the canal walls are much more difficult to precisely determine.
Rosenthal's canal contains the cell bodies of neurons that make up the spiral ganglion, which extends along the length of the organ of Corti throughout all cochlear whorls. Just like the organ of Corti, the spiral ganglion is tonotopically organized: high frequencies are perceived near its base, low frequencies near its apex. The cell bodies of the spiral ganglion (examples shown in red circles in Figure 6) cluster together. In bowheads, the canal is more irregular in general and also along its length, as seen by comparing apex (Figure 6a,c) and base (Figure 6b,c) for the same individuals. In bowhead also, the lumen of Rosenthal's canal appears to change with age, with body septa (Figure 6a–d, blue arrows) increasingly dividing the lumen in older animals, as can be seen by comparing a 1‐year‐old whale (Figure 6a,b) to a 14‐year‐old whale (Figure 6c,d). Such narrow body septa cannot be seen on the CT scans we studied, and the precise effect of aging can thus not be studied by our data. The cell bodies form a well‐defined cluster inside the oval cross‐section of Rosenthal's canal in belugas (Figure 6e,f), with very occasionally a cell body outside of this oval (yellow arrow).

The difficulty in defining a clear wall to the canal may account for a portion of the increased variability in bowheads, but this itself can be taken as further indication that the morphology is less tightly constrained. Improvements in the accuracy of the measurements may still be possible with greater contrast and resolution in CT imaging.

## Functional significance
One peculiar finding is that SSL width, PSL thickness, and SVM Area all show some similar characteristics in the beluga, where there is an increase in variability from the base toward the midway point and then a decrease toward the apex. The reasons for this are unclear, but all three have very low variability, suggesting they are functionally important.
Differences between species in the width of the SSL are as would be expected: the SSL serves to reduce the width of the BM as an adaptation to better high‐frequency perception. A more extensive SSL will allow a longer length of the cochlea to be dedicated to higher frequencies. A long SSL is known in odontocetes and other high‐frequency specialists such as bats, and a shorter SSL is known in species with poor high‐frequency hearing. The extent of the SSL has been used by many authors to infer high‐frequency hearing (Luo et al., 2011; Ruf et al., 2009), although this relationship is inexact.
PSL thickness might be expected to show a relationship with the number of nerve fibers. The PSL is really a bony plate perforated by canals carrying these fibers, and thus a thicker PSL could have a bigger space to hold more. However, it could also be the result of less tightly packed nerve fibers or thicker bone. While the PSL thickness is comparable between the species, Rosenthal's canal area appears smaller in the bowhead, which indicates a smaller number of ganglion cells and, presumably, fewer nerve fibers. This is consistent with published cell counts from both species (Sensor et al., 2015; Waugh et al., 2023). In other sense organs, the bony separators between sensory nerve fibers thicken with age, but we did not note any pattern between thickness and age in our sample. Alternatively, the PSL thickness might be correlated to the BM thickness (as suggested in Peacock, 2025), which is also functionally important in determining the stiffness of the BM. A relationship between the thickness of the membrane and PSL has not been established; however, we do know that the membrane generally becomes thinner toward the apex just as the PSL does. The other side of the BM would be supported by the spiral ligament and SSL, and we also see SSL thickness decrease toward the apex.

## CONCLUSION
It is known that whales have highly derived ear morphologies and that mysticetes and odontocetes are easily distinguishable: mysticete cochleas are more tightly coiled, with more whorls than odontocetes. It is no surprise then that we also find differences between the bowhead and beluga in the morphology of the cochlea's internal structures. For each morphological feature, the coefficient of variation is generally below 30% and usually much lower. However, depending on the features in question, the two species may or may not overlap, and whether the variation is low enough to justify the use of a single specimen in any future study will depend on the features being measured, the chosen taxa, and the research questions being explored.
Differences in the levels of intraspecific variation are in keeping with the known auditory specializations of each species, where the greater precision in the hearing system of odontocetes is reflected in the overall lower levels of variability we see in the beluga as compared to the bowhead. Additionally, the way in which the cochlea's structure changes from the base to the apex is important for determining frequency perception, and these measurements illustrate how variability through the cochlea's length reflects known frequency specializations: we see less variability in the bowhead near the cochlea's low‐frequency apical end as compared to the base. Overall, these measurements illustrate the value of measuring intraspecific variation to explore relationships between form and function.
