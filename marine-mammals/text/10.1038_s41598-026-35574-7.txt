Aversive behavioural responses of killer whales to sounds of long-finned pilot whales.

# Aversive behavioural responses of killer whales to sounds of long-finned pilot whales

## Abstract
Interactions are common among marine species which use sound as the primary form of communication, yet the role of acoustic signals in mediating these interactions remains poorly understood. Long-finned pilot whales (Globicephala melas) are attracted to killer whale (Orcinus orca) sounds, leading to antagonistic interactions. To test whether these interactions are acoustically mediated in both directions, playback experiments (n = 15) using pilot whale and control sound stimuli were conducted on eight killer whales equipped with multi-sensor tags. To assess behavioural responses to the playbacks, we applied hidden Markov models (HMMs) to movement and acoustic data, and fitted univariate regression models to a horizontal movement reaction score, calling rate, and group behaviour variables. The tagged whales exhibited an avoidance response to pilot whale sounds, evidenced by fast, directed movement away from the sound source and increased cohesion and alignment of group members. Calling rate often increased initially, followed by a pronounced decrease. These findings demonstrate that killer whales, the oceans’ apex predators, respond to acoustic signals of pilot whales and likely perceive their presence as a threat, similar to naval sonar. This study provides important insights into the complexity of cetacean behaviour and acoustic mechanisms shaping multi-species community dynamics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-35574-7.

## Introduction
Interspecific interactions encompass a range of fascinating behaviours that are important from an ecological perspective to explain the stable co-existence of multi-species communities and ultimately to understand the evolution of complex ecosystems. In competition or predator-prey interactions, species may either approach or avoid one another. For instance, a competitor might approach an opponent to intimidate and displace it from a prey source patch or habitat. In a predator-prey context, the predator must approach its prey to feed, while targeted prey will generally avoid predators. However, in some cases prey actually approach the predator as a defensive strategy to deter the threat1. Such harassing approaches, often referred to as ‘mobbing-like’ behaviour, play a key role in both competitive and predator-prey interactions and have been described in a variety of taxa2,3. They typically involve multiple individuals that approach, harass or attack predators or competitors to drive them away. Whereas several hypotheses have been proposed regarding the benefits to the mobbing species, behavioural responses of the mobbed species and their associated biological costs remain largely unexplored4. This gap prevents a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms driving interspecific interactions from both perspectives.
Mobbing-like behaviours are commonly associated with increased calling activity, which may serve a dual function: alerting and recruiting others (conspecifics and/or heterospecifics) for a social defence response, and deterring intruders or predators through intimidation5. Sound travels efficiently underwater and is a major sensory modality for cetaceans. Therefore, acoustic communication also plays a key role in their interspecific interactions. Playback experiments have shown that cetaceans detect the sounds of potential predators, prey or competitors6–9 and respond by exhibiting movement responses7,10,11, acoustic responses8, changes in group organisation12 and alteration of fitness-enhancing activities10,13.
Killer whales (Orcinus orca) are the oceans’ apex predator and a highly vocal species14. Several ecotypes have been described, with some specialising in fish and others in marine mammal prey15. Many cetaceans show avoidance responses to playbacks of killer whale sounds7,10,11,16,17. To date, the only species known to be attracted to killer whale sounds are short-finned pilot whales (Globicephala macrorhynchus18), long-finned pilot whales (G. melas19), and humpback whales (Megaptera novaeangliae11). Specifically, humpback whales feeding on herring (Clupea harengus) in Norway regularly form mixed-species associations with killer whales targeting the same prey and approach sounds of these local herring-eating killer whales, likely to take advantage of feeding opportunities11,20. However, they avoid sounds of unfamiliar mammal-eating killer whales, simulating potential predator presence7,11. In contrast, long-finned pilot whales approach both fish- and mammal-eating killer whale sounds, potentially as a form of mobbing behaviour8,19.
Interactions between long-finned pilot whales (hereafter pilot whales) and killer whales have been documented across the North Atlantic21–23. In these interactions, pilot whales typically approach killer whales once or several times, often at high speed and in large groups. The killer whales usually respond by moving away, sometimes at very high speed as indicated by whales porpoising out of the water23. Playbacks of fish-eating killer whale and mammal-eating killer whale sounds elicited strong attraction responses in pilot whales, but differences in behaviour suggest that pilot whales discriminate between the two stimuli8,19. In response to mammal-eating killer whale sounds, pilot whales exhibited a cryptic approach strategy: they stopped foraging, tightened their groups and spent less time near the surface, while approaching the sound source, potentially to inspect and evaluate the threat level8. When hearing fish-eating killer whale sounds, pilot whale groups joined, increasing their overall group size and calling activity while approaching the sound source8,19. This response is consistent with a mobbing-like behaviour towards a potential predator or perceived competitor8,22,23. Predation by killer whales on pilot whales is rare24. Competition for prey also seems an unlikely driver, as there is little evidence of trophic niche overlap between both species in the areas where the interactions have been observed22. However, possible spatial overlap in prey species habitat might still lead to competition for feeding areas. Thus, mobbing towards a competitor sharing the same habitat or to a low threat predator are currently the most likely explanations for the pilot whales’ behaviour8,22.
While playback experiments showed that the mobbing-like behaviour of pilot whales can be triggered by the detection of killer whale sounds8,19, it remains unclear whether and how the interactions are also mediated in the other direction. An acoustic mechanism seems likely as killer whales have acute hearing across a range of frequencies, including those of pilot whale vocalisations25. The variation in intensity of observed killer whale responses to the presence of pilot whales in their habitat23 indicates that they may differentiate threat levels. However, it remains unclear why this apex predator avoids a smaller, seemingly inferior aggressor. The large group sizes of approaching and actively vocalizing pilot whales may play a role21,23. The resulting cacophony of pilot whale sounds could signal a high threat level to the killer whales, interfere with their own communication, or simply irritate them. The energetic cost of moving away at high speed coupled with potential loss of feeding opportunities, may impact killer whales, especially if these interactions occur repeatedly and over long time periods. Thus, detecting pilot whale sounds could help killer whales anticipate and balance these costs by determining the most appropriate response ahead of time.
The aim of this study was to test whether sounds produced by pilot whales influence the behaviour of killer whales and thus to investigate whether the interactions are acoustically mediated from both sides. This was achieved by conducting playback experiments of pilot whale and control sounds to tagged killer whales. Experiments were conducted in a herring spawning ground where killer whales gather in large numbers during the summer months26. Pilot whales also regularly occur in the area and interactions between the two species have been observed23. We hypothesized that killer whales would adjust their behaviour in response to pilot whale sounds to anticipate a flight. Specifically, we expected killer whales would (1) switch to a travelling behaviour, move away from the sound source and increase speed, (2) modify their own sound production to reduce the risk of detection by pilot whales, and (3) increase group cohesion and synchrony. Therefore, we tested for state-dependant behavioural changes using a hidden Markov model, investigated changes in horizontal movement direction and compared call production and social parameters before, during and after the experiments.

## Results

Generally, two experiments were conducted per tag deployment (one of pilot whale sounds and one with either the noise control or the 1–2 kHz upsweep control), but for one deployment (oo23_181b) no control playback could be conducted because of deteriorating weather conditions. Due to the low sample size (n = 2) of the 1–2 kHz upsweep control stimulus playbacks, these were not included in statistical analyses; however, descriptive analyses of the responses are provided.

## State-dependent behavioural responses
A hidden Markov model (HMM) was fitted to the time series data derived from the movement and acoustic data from the Dtags. The HMM had three states (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S1): state #1, labelled ‘travelling’, was characterised by fast, directed horizontal movement, shallow to intermediate dive depths, and low probabilities of calling and tail slaps; state #2, labelled ‘feeding’, was characterised by slow, undirected horizontal movement, deep dives and high probabilities of calling and tail slaps; and state #3, named ‘other’, represented slow, undirected horizontal movement with shallow dives and intermediate probabilities of calling and tail slaps. The model fitted the data well, but some autocorrelation remained in the residuals for step length, mean depth and presence/absence of calls (Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2).

The probability of switching from the ‘other’ state (#3) to the ‘travelling’ state (#1) significantly increased for both pilot whale sound and noise control playbacks compared to periods without playback (Fig. 2b). This transition probability was predicted to increase from 0.04 (CI: 0.03–0.06) when there was no playback to 0.19 (CI: 0.06–0.46) during pilot whale sounds and 0.29 (CI: 0.06–0.74) during noise control. Switching from the ‘feeding’ state (#2) to the ‘travelling’ state (#1) was not affected by the playback (Fig. 2a), but the ‘feeding’ state was rarely assigned to time periods immediately before, or during, a playback.

The probability of calling was predicted to be 0.25 (CI: −0.06–0.56) during pilot whale sound playback compared to 0.20 (CI: 0.16–0.24) when there was no playback and 0.09 (CI: −0.08–0.27) during playback of the noise control (Fig. 2d).

## Horizontal movement responses
The pilot whale stimulus and the second playback corresponded to the lowest (i.e. most negative, indicating avoidance) horizontal movement reaction scores (Fig. 3). The best-fitting general estimating equation (GEE) model retained the explanatory variables Stimulus and Order (Supplementary Table S1), and both had a significant effect on the movement reaction scores (Stimulus: p = 0.02; Order: p = 0.004, Table 2). The mean horizontal movement reaction score was − 82.2 (SD = 106.0) for pilot whale sound playbacks and − 25.5 (SD = 88.5) for noise control playbacks. The mean value for the noise control playback was particularly influenced by the score for one individual (oo21_184a) and would have been positive without this data point. This individual kept its overall trajectory but was already relatively close to the sound source at the start of the playback, which led to a negative reaction score (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Four out of eight tagged whales exposed to pilot whale sounds were judged to change direction away from the source during the playback (Supplementary Figs. S3-S10). Two whales (oo21_199a, oo23_188a) were already moving away from the source when the playback began and kept swimming away, one increasing its speed. Another two tagged whales (oo21_189a, oo22_228a) did not make a clear turn away from the sound source but both increased speed, while moving past the sound source. In contrast, four of the five whales tested with the noise control did not show a clear response to the playback and only one whale showed a clear turn away from the sound source (oo22_228a). Another one (oo21_184a) slightly increased speed, while the other tagged whales decreased speed or showed no changes. In both experiments with the 1–2 kHz upsweep control (oo21_199a, oo21_202b) a turn away from the sound source was observed, as indicated by slightly negative horizontal movement reaction scores (Fig. 3).

## Acoustic responses
The total number of high-quality calls recorded in the eight pilot whale sound playbacks was 284 (npre = 49, nduring = 220, npost = 15), 123 for the five noise control playbacks (npre = 22, nduring = 22, npost = 79), and 115 for the two upsweep control playbacks (npre = 46, nduring = 42, npost = 27). The mean number of calls adjusted for group size was 4.6 calls/individual in the pre-phases of the pilot whale sound playbacks, 4.7 calls/individual in the during-phases, and 0.2 calls/individual in the post-phases. The small difference between the mean number of calls/individual in the pre- and the during-phases was primarily due to one experiment (oo23_181b) with a high number of calls in the pre-phase. However, in most other experiments the number of calls increased or showed only small changes in the during-phase (Fig. 4). In the post-phase, in five out of eight deployments, whales did not emit any calls. For the noise control playback, the mean number of calls adjusted for group size was 0.9 calls/individual in the pre-phases, 0.7 in the during-phases, and 2.8 in the post-phases. Most of these calls (n = 109) stem from one experiment (oo23_188a), in the other experiments whales were either silent and stayed silent or made just a few calls (Fig. 4). For the upsweep control playbacks, a mean of 7.7 calls/individual was recorded in the pre-phases, 8.2 calls/individual in the during-phases, and 4.0 calls/individual in the post-phases. The selected GEE retained Stimulus, Phase and the interaction Stimulus:Phase as explanatory variables (Supplementary Table S1) and predicted a reduction in call production in the post-phase compared to the pre-phase when the playback was of pilot whale sounds (Stimulus:Phase: p < 0.01, Table 2; Fig. 4).

Measurements of fundamental frequency showed an increase in the mid, end, and maximum frequency of calls during the pilot whale sound and noise control playbacks, as did the duration of calls for the noise control playbacks (Supplementary Fig. S11). These increases might be driven by individual differences between tag deployments or call types used, however, too few calls were recorded in the pre-phases across experiments to allow for statistical comparisons.
In total, 21 different call types and subtypes were recorded in the pre-, during- and post-phases of the experiments (Supplementary Fig. S12). Most of these (n = 11) were recorded during the pilot whale sound playbacks, which also contained a large proportion of variable and some aberrant calls. The low numbers of calls recorded during noise and upsweep control playbacks were reflected in few call types being present.

## Group level behavioural responses
The best GEEs for the mean number of individuals in the focal group as well as the maximum number of individuals in the focal area did not retain any of the explanatory variables, although, both tended to increase during pilot whale sound compared to noise control playbacks (Figs. 5a and b). Group organisation, however, significantly differed between pilot whale and noise control playbacks. Individual spacing decreased significantly during pilot whale sound playback (Stimulus: p = 0.004, Table 2; Fig. 5c) and during the second playback (Order: p = 0.03, Table 2). Killer whales also lined up more often in response to pilot whale sounds compared to noise control playbacks (Stimulus: p = 0.005, Table 2; Fig. 5d) and this effect reduced during the post-phase (Phase: p = 0.01, Table 2). Milling on the other hand, occurred less frequently during pilot whale sound playback and over the post-phase of the noise control playback (Stimulus:Phase: p = 0.02, Table 2; Fig. 5e). During the upsweep control playbacks, individuals increased group spacing, whereas line swimming and milling remained unchanged (Fig. 5).

## Discussion
Interspecific interactions between cetaceans are widely documented, yet their mechanisms remain poorly understood. Our results showed that killer whales exhibit an avoidance response to pilot whale sounds, characterised by a switch to travelling behaviour, increased speed and directed movement away from the sound source. Killer whales initially increased calling rates shortly after the start of the playback but then became silent. They adjusted their group behaviour to enhance cohesion, by tightening the spacing between individuals, aligning side by side and coordinated their travel in the same direction. These results are consistent with observations of real interactions, in which killer whales avoid pilot whales and sometimes flee at high speed21–23. As previous work demonstrated that pilot whales are attracted to killer whale sounds8,19, our study further demonstrates that acoustic signalling can mediate these interspecific interactions in both directions.
The movement response observed here aligns with previously documented killer whale avoidance of naval sonar. As in our experiments, killer whales experimentally exposed to sonar typically switched to a travelling behaviour, with directed movement away from the sound source and increased speed28,29. Avoiding a perceived threat by moving away (i.e. negative phonotaxis) is a common threat-response across taxa, shown in birds and mammals4, including several cetacean species7,11,17,30–33. Such avoidance is often accompanied by increases in speed7,31,33–35, and reflected in behaviour state changes36,37. The movement responses observed in our study show evidence of an aversive response, demonstrating that killer whales perceive pilot whale sounds as potentially threatening.
Based on our observations in the field and responses of other cetaceans to threatening acoustic stimuli16,38, we predicted killer whales to turn silent in response to pilot whale sound playbacks, either to avoid detection or localisation by pilot whales, or as part of the switch to travelling behaviour, during which Icelandic killer whales tend to be silent39. While we found no significant change in call production in the ‘travelling’ state during playbacks, killer whales increased their calling rate during pilot whale sound playback in four out of eight experiments (including a large proportion of variable and aberrant calls) and significant decreases in calling rate were detected in the post-phase. We also observed increases in the frequencies measured but could not confirm whether these were true changes in call features, individual differences, or related to changes in call type usage. Similar responses were observed during controlled sonar exposure experiments40. Samarra et al.40 suggested that the initial increase in calling may be a way to coordinate behaviour in the group before moving away. It is likely that at the start of an interaction there is little benefit in becoming quiet as pilot whales would have already detected and localised killer whales at this point. Instead, it might be more advantageous to alert conspecifics and coordinate behaviour. Alternatively, the increase in calling rate may be related to the tightening of the group during exposure, with more individuals vocalising near the tagged whale leading to more high-quality calls being detected. However, increases in call frequency, variable and aberrant calls are thought to indicate excitement14. Thus, alarming conspecifics is a plausible function of this response.
The lack of change in focal group size and number of individuals in the area suggested that alarming conspecifics did not result in the recruitment of other groups. Predation risk is thought to be a major reason for group living across taxa41 with several species showing grouping responses to perceived threats8,9,12,42. However, large groups may be more conspicuous and if part of the strategy of killer whales avoiding pilot whales involves minimizing attention, then increasing group size may be disadvantageous. We observed group tightening and more synchronised behaviours, such as line swimming and reduced milling. These changes likely reflect the switch to travel behaviour, which involves more directed movement. Nevertheless, synchronisation is an important threat response in many group-living species43,44 and reduction of spacing was observed, for example, in pilot whales approaching killer whale sounds8,12, as well as in several baleen whales in response to killer whale attacks45. Killer whales exposed to sonar also responded with tightening of the group and increased synchrony29. Tightening and synchronised movement offer several benefits, such as reduced individual vulnerability, confusion of an attacker, as well as rapid information transmission to aid collective response46,47.
While we cannot rule out the possibility that the presence of the tracking boat during experiments influenced the behaviour of the killer whales, we aimed to keep a distance of 100–200 m and manoeuvre in a way to minimise the impact on whale behaviour. If an effect of the boat was present it should be similar for all playback stimuli and across all phases of the experiment and thus differences in behaviour observed should be due to factors other than the presence of the tracking boat. Overall, few responses were observed in response to the noise control playbacks. The probability of switching from the ‘other’ to the ‘travelling’ state increased, even more so than during pilot whale playback. However, the probability showed large variation, likely due to the relatively small sample size of noise control playbacks. Nevertheless, killer whales might switch to a travelling state in response to the noise control playback in order to avoid the unknown sound source or avoid masking of their communication. In other cetacean species such lower responses to control sounds have also been reported, indicating that these sounds are detected by the whales7,8,42. The lack of a significant increase in horizontal speed (i.e. step length), no turn away from the sound source, and lack of responses in the acoustic or social parameters indicate though that the noise stimulus is not perceived as a clear threat and does not elicit the same urgency in the response as the pilot whale sounds.
According to the risk-disturbance hypothesis, both human disturbance and predation threats are driven by the same trade-off between perceived risk and the cost of reducing fitness-enhancing activities48. Recent comparisons across cetacean species confirmed that responses to a high-level predation threat represented by mammal-eating killer whale sound playback are closely correlated to responses to perceived risk from sonar and depend on whether the species typically exhibit fight- or flight-behaviour13. The responses we observed indicate that killer whales either perceive pilot whales as a high-level threat, or that cost of avoidance is low. If killer whales perceive pilot whales as a threat, it remains unclear why. Despite interactions being observed in several locations and over several years, physical attacks or predation of pilot whales on killer whales have never been reported21–23. Larger group sizes of pilot whales led to stronger responses by killer whales, suggesting that group size could play a role23. In our experiments, responses rarely lasted beyond the end of the playback. Killer whales typically reduced speed, ceased moving away from the sound source and the group returned to less aligned swimming behaviour within the 15-minute post-phase. Only one killer whale was in a ‘feeding’ behaviour state directly before the start of the playback. While it immediately stopped feeding in response to the pilot whale sounds, it resumed relatively soon after the second playback ended (oo21_199a, Supplementary Fig. S5). The study area is a known herring spawning ground, where herring is abundant during summer but patchily distributed. Notably, killer whales never left the general area in response to a playback, indicating that a short-term avoidance may be of little cost to them as they might be able to easily locate new feeding locations. Observations of interactions show that pilot whales eventually abort a pursuit with the two species often remaining a few hundred meters apart until either the pilot whales leave the area or approach again23. These findings highlight the complexity of these interactions. While individual responses may appear to have minimal costs to killer whales, the cumulative impact of repeated disturbances remains uncertain. Indeed, an order effect was present for the horizontal movement reaction score and individual spacing, with the second playbacks generating more pronounced horizontal avoidance and tighter spacing between individuals. Such order effects might indicate sensitisation to acoustic stimuli and suggest that repeated approaches by pilot whales could lead to stronger responses.
Unlike in reports of naturally-occurring interactions23, we never observed killer whales surfacing at very high speed (porpoising) during playback experiments. This could indicate that they realised that the simulated group of pilot whales was not approaching or following them. It also illustrates that detecting the acoustic signals of pilot whales early on might help killer whales adjust their behaviour in time to avoid energetically costly behaviour such as porpoising. Response thresholds are likely influenced by multiple factors including contextual variables32,37, such as group size, current behaviour, prey availability, or presence of calves, as well as information carried by the acoustic signals of the pilot whales, such as perceived number of individuals, threat level, distance, or movement direction. Indeed, we observed variation in killer whales’ responsiveness. For example, weaker or no responses were recorded in the two playbacks of one of the three versions of pilot whale sound stimuli (version 2). Given that pilot whales increased calling rates in response to killer whale sounds8,12, we prepared our stimuli by carefully selecting recordings that were collected during such mobbing-like approaches. The observed variation in the response to the different versions of pilot whale sound stimuli indicates that killer whales may perceive subtle variations in the sounds of pilot whales, reflecting the variability observed in responses to actual pilot whale approaches23. Future playback experiments using acoustic stimuli from pilot whales recorded in different behavioural states (e.g. during socialising, which might be more neutral for killer whales), from other populations and to killer whales engaged in different behavioural contexts or under varying prey availability could help to determine variation in response thresholds and the potential costs of avoidance. Such findings would be valuable for predicting the broader implications and impacts of these interspecific interactions for killer whales.
Two playbacks of series of 1–2 kHz upsweep control sounds were conducted but were excluded from statistical analyses due to the too low sample size. The movement and acoustic responses we observed during the upsweep playbacks were similar to those documented in previous controlled exposure experiments with naval sonar28,29 and to those observed in the present study in response to pilot whale sounds. However, differences were noted at the group behaviour level. For example, individual spacing increased during exposure to the upsweep signal, contrasting with the group tightening observed in response to the pilot whale sound playback. Thus, our data suggest that killer whales did not exhibit all the same behavioural components in response to pilot whale sounds and to the upsweep control. This preliminary result suggests that they can discriminate between pilot whale sounds and a synthetic modulated signal in similar frequency range, adjusting their behaviour according to the perceived type and severity of threat even though it remains unclear precisely what type of threat the sonar or the upsweep control is perceived as.
In summary, our results demonstrate that killer whales exhibit an aversive response to long-finned pilot whales sounds, marked by avoidance and increased group cohesion. Combined with previous studies on pilot whales8,19, this shows that interactions between the two species are acoustically mediated in both directions with each species detecting and responding to the other’s calls. The response of killer whales encompassed their movement, acoustic and social behaviour. While individual responses did not seem to extend much beyond the sound exposure, cumulative long-term impacts of the interactions between the two species remain unknown. Killer whales may interrupt feeding and expend substantial amounts of energy during avoidance, potentially leading to energetic costs. Repeated interactions between the species may alter behaviour and habitat use, possibly resulting in population-level effects. While our study demonstrates the behavioural mechanism of the interaction, its underlying drivers remain unclear. Future research on the trophic ecology, site fidelity and habitat use of both species could clarify whether competition or an anti-predator strategy drives the interactions. In many animal species, the severity of responses to human disturbance is assessed by comparison with anti-predator responses, which typically serve as a reference response to a natural high-level threat13,30,48. The behavioural response to the pilot whale sounds provides such a yardstick for the killer whale, representing an alternative to the risk-disturbance hypothesis which is not applicable to this top predator and help to interpret and predict human impacts on this species.

## Methods
The study was conducted around the Vestmannaeyjar archipelago in South Iceland in June-August 2021–2023. The protocol, detailed below, included the deployment of multi-sensor tags to a killer whale, visual data collection of the tagged whale and its group before, during and after playback experiments, and tag detachment and recovery. Playback experiments started after a post-tagging recovery period of at least 2 h. A 6-m RHIB boat was used for tagging and later to deploy the sound source (the ‘playback source boat’). Visual tracking and observational data collection during playback experiments were conducted from a 12-m vessel (the ‘tracking boat’).

## Tagging and behavioural data collection
Digital acoustic recording tags (Dtags50) were attached to killer whales with suction cups, using a carbon fibre pole or pneumatic tag launcher (ARTS51). Dtags contained a pressure sensor, 3D magnetometer, 3D accelerometer (all sampled at 250 Hz), VHF transmitter, integrated GPS-ARGOS transceiver with snapshot GPS (F6G 134 A, Lotek NZ Ltd), and two hydrophones (240 kHz). Visual and ARGOS-relayed GPS locations of the whale, and VHF signals, were used to track the whale during experiments and recover the tag.
Once a tag was attached to a whale, this whale became the focal whale. Observers on the tracking boat visually tracked the focal whale and its group. The software Logger 2010 (International Fund for Animal Welfare, IFAW) was used to calculate the focal whale’s location using the estimated distance to the whale, vessel’s course-over-ground, and azimuth of the whale (relative to the vessel’s heading) measured using an angle board, and plotted the track in real time.
Visual observations of the surface behaviour of the focal group were collected from the tracking boat throughout the playback experiments. These behavioural observations followed the protocol described by Visser et al.52 and included group size, individual spacing, number of individuals in the focal area (within 200 m), milling index, and line swimming (details in Supplementary Table S2). The observations were recorded every two minutes or as soon as the tagged whale surfaced thereafter.

## Playback experiments
The experiments followed a protocol previously used with other cetacean species7,8,19. The playback source boat was positioned 500–1000 m in front and 45–60° to the side of the focal whale’s track. This position was specified by the tracking boat based upon the focal whale’s real-time track.
The playback system consisted of a Tascam DR-40 or DR-40X recorder, a SONY XM-N502 amplifier, and a Lubell LL9162T underwater loudspeaker (frequency response: 0.2–20 kHz) submerged at 8 m depth. Sounds were broadcast underwater from the loudspeaker with the boat engine off. To ensure faithful playback, sounds were monitored using a calibrated hydrophone (Bruel & Kjaer 8104) attached at 1 m above the speaker. The hydrophone was connected to a pre-amplifier (Bruel & Kjaer Nexus 2635) and a recorder (Tascam DR-680MKII) to save acoustic recordings. The playback level settings were tested and adjusted ahead of the experiments at the start of each fieldwork season using a sound level meter (Bruel & Kjaer 2250) previously calibrated with the hydrophone (Bruel & Kjaer 8104). The aim was to transmit sound stimuli at an average root-mean-square sound pressure level of approximately 150 dB re 1 µPa, within the natural sound level range of pilot whales calls53.
Three types of acoustic stimuli were used: long-finned pilot whale sounds, a broadband noise control and a 1–2 kHz upsweep control signal (Supplementary Fig. S13). Each stimulus had a duration of 15 min. Pilot whale sounds were natural sound sequences previously recorded in the study area using a towed hydrophone array or a Dtag. These recordings, accompanied by visual observations, indicated that pilot whales have higher call rates and produce more overlapped sounds when approaching killer whales during mobbing-like behaviours compared to other contexts. Therefore, only sound sequences recorded in this context were used as playback stimuli. Any potential killer whale calls as well as surfacing noises on tag recordings were removed from the stimuli. For each pilot whale stimulus version, a broadband noise control stimulus was created using periods without pilot whale sounds from the same recordings. These were amplified to the same averaged root-mean-square (rms) sound level as the pilot whale stimuli. The broadband noise control was used as a negative control (CTRL-) to ensure that killer whales specifically respond to pilot whales calls and not any other unspecific sounds broadcast by the playback system. Three versions of pairs of pilot whale sound and noise control stimuli were generated from recordings of different pilot whale groups to reduce the risk of pseudoreplication54.
To test whether any frequency modulated sounds would trigger similar responses as the pilot whale sound stimulus, an upsweep control stimulus composed of the continuous repetition of a 19 s, 1–2 kHz upsweep, synthetic sound signal was used. This signal matches the frequency characteristics and duty cycle of a continuous active sonar (CAS) used by navies, though naval sonar source levels are much higher. As killer whales have been shown to avoid other sonar signals28,29, this stimulus was intended as a positive control (CTRL+), testing whether other frequency modulated sounds would trigger similar responses as the pilot whale sound stimuli. However, the use of this stimulus was ceased after year one to maximise data collection for the other two stimuli and, due to the low sample size (n = 2), playbacks with the upsweep control were not included in statistical analyses.
Each playback experiment consisted of a 15 min pre-exposure phase, a 15 min sound exposure (during-phase), and a 15 min post-exposure phase. Usually, each focal whale was subject to two experiments (i.e. two stimulus types) separated by a minimum of 15 min between the end of the first post-exposure phase and the start of the second pre-exposure phase, resulting in at least 45 min interval between the two sound exposures. Stimulus types were presented in an alternating sequence to control for any potential order effect (Table 1).

## Ethics declaration
Tagging and experiments were approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee at the University of St Andrews, carried out in compliance with local regulations and under an institutional permit of the Marine and Freshwater Research Institute, Iceland.

## Data processing
Pressure data from the Dtags were converted to depth, correcting for temperature effects. The body orientation of the whale was described in terms of pitch, roll and heading, which were based on acceleration and magnetic field strength50. Speed was calculated from flow noise in the 66–94 Hz band for depths greater than 5 m55.
The horizontal track of the whale was estimated using a path reconstruction method, which combines dead-reckoning with the locations from visual sightings or GPS (offloaded from the tag after retrieval) to estimate location at 1-second intervals55. Tag oo23_181b malfunctioned, preventing the derivation of the whale’s body orientation. Therefore, the track of this whale was based upon linear interpolation between the visual and GPS positions. One large outlier in the GPS locations was identified using the sdafilter function (default speed threshold 2 m/s) of the argosfilter package56 in R 4.3.057. This outlier was removed before track interpolation.
Audio recordings from the Dtags were inspected visually and aurally in the software Audacity version 3.3.3 (www.audacityteam.org). Spectrograms (Hann window, NFFT = 8192) were generated and killer whale calls and tail slaps were marked and given a quality rating of 1 (low), 2 (medium), or 3 (high) based on the perceived signal to noise ratio and overall clarity of the frequency contour. High-quality calls and tail slaps were assumed to originate from the tagged whale or a nearby individual and were thus used in subsequent analyses. High-quality calls were classified to call type based on an existing catalogue58,59. ‘Aberrant’ calls were identified as belonging to a discrete call category but showed alterations in frequency or duration; ‘variable’ calls were those that could not be assigned to a call type14. A custom Matlab60 routine was used to display spectrograms (Hann window, NFFT = 4096, 87.5% overlap) and take measurements of the low frequency contour at the start, middle, end, maximum and minimum points, as well as of the duration of the call61.

## State-dependent behavioural response
To identify potential changes in behaviour, hidden Markov models (HMMs) were applied to time series data derived from the high-resolution movement and acoustic data from the Dtags. HMMs identify the most likely underlying, non-observable (i.e. hidden) process. The states of the Markov chain can be interpreted as behavioural states and HMMs have been widely used to characterise animal movement, including in cetaceans33,37,62. However, patterns identified by these models may not relate to biologically meaningful behaviours, and states may include multiple behaviours or the same behaviours may occur in more than one state63. Therefore, these models are typically informed by knowledge on the behaviour of the study species and population.
We created a multivariate HMM with five variables at 5-minute resolution: step length, turning angle, mean depth, acoustic presence/absence of calls, and acoustic presence/absence of tail slaps. Step lengths and turning angles were calculated from the horizontal movement track. Mean depth were the mean depths of the whale over the 5-minute time intervals. The presence/absence of (one or more) calls and presence/absence of (one or more) tail slaps were extracted from the acoustic recordings of the tag. Icelandic killer whales have the highest calling rates when feeding and are mostly silent when travelling39. During feeding on herring, killer whales also use underwater tail slaps to debilitate fish64. Therefore, calls and tail slap sounds can aid in the identification of behavioural states. Gamma distributions were used to model step length and mean depth. Turning angles were modelled as a von Mises distribution, and the presence/absence of calls and tail slaps as Bernoulli distributions.
A model with three states was selected over models with two or four states based on Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) and biological interpretability. This 3-state model separated the data into a ‘travelling’ state (state 1), a ‘feeding’ state (state 2), and a third state that likely included several behaviours, such as resting or socialising, and was named ‘other’39. Assuming an avoidance response would involve a switch from the ‘feeding’ or ‘other’ state to the ‘travelling’ state, we included a covariate ‘playback’ affecting state transitions to the travelling state. The playback covariate was a factor with three levels: Off (all periods without playback), Noise (noise control), PW (pilot whale sounds). Observations during the 1–2 kHz upsweep control stimulus were excluded. We expected step length (and thus speed) to increase and calling to decrease in the ‘travelling’ state, which was tested by incorporating the same covariate on the state-dependent observation distributions. Model-based estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used for inference and the most likely state for each time step was predicted using the Viterbi algorithm. All analyses were conducted using the momentuHMM package65 in R 4.3.057.

## Horizontal movement, acoustic and group behaviour responses
We expected killer whales to move away from the pilot whale sound playback source, which was tested using a horizontal movement reaction score. This reaction score is a relative measure, comparing the observed horizontal track during playback to a projected course of the whale based on its direction of movement prior to the start of the playback19; negative values represent an overall avoidance response (the animal ending farther from the sound source compared to the projected position), positive scores represent an attraction response. A 5-minute time interval was chosen to create the projection.
Acoustic and group behaviour responses were tested by investigating changes between the 15-minute pre- and during-phases (PRE_DUR) and changes between the 15-minute pre- and post-phases (PRE_POST). PRE_DUR captures the quantitative change occurring during the exposure, whereas PRE_POST assesses whether a potential change that started during the exposure is prolonged over the post-phase or whether a change started during the post-phase rather than the during-phase. We tested for changes in the calling rate, group size and organisation. Changes in calling rate were tested using the number of calls corrected by focal group size. Changes in group size were investigated using the mean number of individuals in the group of the tagged whale and the maximum number of individuals in the focal area (i.e. within 200 m of the tagged whale). To calculate the PRE_DUR and PRE_POST scores for changes in calling, focal group size and individuals in the focal area, we subtracted the value of the pre-phase from the value of the during- or post-phase, respectively. Changes in group organisation were tested by investigating changes in the spacing between individuals, changes in line swimming value (presence of lined up geometry), and changes in the milling index (surface heading into different directions). These categorical change variables were converted into reaction scores, which represent increases, decreases or no change of the behavioural variables observed but are not the actual magnitude of the derived variables. To capture any potential tightening or spreading of the group members, individual spacing was recorded as a categorical value based on the distance in body lengths (BL) between the focal individual and the nearest individual. <1 BL is translated to a score − 1 (very tight), 1–3 BL to a score 0 (tight), 3–15 BL to a score 1 (loose), > 15 BL to a score 2 (very loose), and no other whales to a NA score because the animal was solitary. The maximum individual spacing score value was recorded for each 15-minute phase. Milling and line swimming were recorded as present or absent. Given an absence of milling (all group members heading into the same direction) is the most common behaviour, we assessed whether there was at least one milling event recorded over each 15-minute phase (Yes or No). Swimming on the same horizontal line was variable and therefore we assessed for each phase whether the majority of observations contained line swimming (Yes or No). The change scores PRE_DUR and PRE_POST for milling and line swimming were then obtained as follows: change from No over the pre-phase to Yes over the during- or the post-phase was given a score value of + 1, whereas Yes to No was scored as -1. No change (from No to No or from Yes to Yes) was scored as 0 (no change).

## Statistical analysis of reaction score variables
To analyse differences between pilot whale sound and noise control stimuli, general estimating equations (GEEs) were used, as the data included repeated measures of the same individuals. Each reaction score variable (horizontal movement, acoustic and all group-level parameters) was tested in a separate GEE model. Focal animal ID was used as a blocking unit to account for correlation between observations. We tested effects of the explanatory variables Stimulus (pilot whale or noise control), Order (1 or 2), Phase (PRE_DUR or PRE_POST), and an interaction term Stimulus:Phase. Response variables were the horizontal movement reaction score, change in the number of calls, change in focal group size, change in maximum number of individuals in the focal area, change in individual spacing, change in line swimming, and change in milling. For each response variable we first ran a full model with all explanatory variables and then selected models using backwards selection based on p-values given by an ANOVA (sequential Wald test, detailed methods in Curé et al.7). GEEs were run in R 4.3.0v using the geepack package57, and results were considered statistically significant at the 5% level.
