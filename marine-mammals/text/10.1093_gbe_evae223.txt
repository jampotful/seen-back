Parallel Spectral Tuning of a Cone Visual Pigment Provides Evidence for Ancient Deep-Sea Adaptations in Cetaceans
Hai Chi, Linxia Sun, Na Li, Yue Zhan, Jinqu Guo, Lei Lei, David M. Irwin, Guang Yang, Shixia Xu, Yang Liu (2024). Genome Biology and Evolution. https://doi.org/10.1093/gbe/evae223. doi:10.1093/gbe/evae223. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Parallel Spectral Tuning of a Cone Visual Pigment Provides Evidence for Ancient Deep-Sea Adaptations in Cetaceans

## Abstract
AbstractDichromatic color vision is mediated by two cone visual pigments in many eutherian mammals. After reentry into the sea, early cetaceans lost their violet-sensitive visual pigment (short wavelength-sensitive 1) independently in the baleen and toothed whale ancestors and thus obtained only monochromatic cone vision. Subsequently, losses of the middle/long wavelength-sensitive (M/LWS) pigment have also been reported in multiple whale lineages, leading to rhodopsin (RH1)-mediated rod monochromatic vision. To further elucidate the phenotypic evolution of whale visual pigments, we assessed the spectral tuning of both M/LWS and RH1 from representative cetacean taxa. Interestingly, although the coding sequences for M/LWS are intact in both the pygmy right whale and the Baird's beaked whale, no spectral sensitivity was detected in vitro. Pseudogenization of other cone vision-related genes is observed in the pygmy right whale, suggesting a loss of cone-mediated vision. After ancestral sequence reconstructions, ancient M/LWS pigments from cetacean ancestors were resurrected and functionally measured. Spectral tuning of M/LWS from the baleen whale ancestor shows that it is green sensitive, with a 40-nm shift in sensitivity to a shorter wavelength. For the ancestor of sperm whales, although no spectral sensitivity could be recorded for its M/LWS pigment, a substantial sensitivity shift (20 to 30 nm) to a shorter wavelength may have also occurred before its functional inactivation. The parallel phenotypic evolution of M/LWS to shorter wavelength sensitivity might be visual adaptations in whales allowing more frequent deep-sea activities, although additional ecological differentiations may have led to their subsequent losses.

## Significance
In the early evolution of cetaceans, their short wavelength-sensitive visual pigment was lost, leaving only middle/long wavelength-sensitive (M/LWS) (bright-light vision) and rhodopsin (RH1) (dim-light vision) pigments retained. However, our understanding of the phenotypic evolution of these two visual pigments in cetaceans is still not fully resolved. Using functional assays, we first identified ongoing cases of pseudogenization for the whale M/LWS gene and then revealed a shorter wavelength shift for this pigment in the ancestors of both sperm whales and baleen whales. The blueshift in M/LWS occurred independently in three cetacean clades, yielding a pigment with a spectral sensitivity closer to their RH1, which could be adaptive to increased deep-diving activities in the blue deep sea.

## Introduction
Vision, which is firstly mediated by visual pigments expressed in photoreceptor cells (cones and rods) of the retina, is important for the adaptations of vertebrates to diverse environments (Davies et al. 2012; Hauser and Chang 2017). Visual pigments consist of a chromophore and an opsin, with the opsin genes experiencing several duplication events since the origin of the lampreys to generate five major kinds of pigments (Collin et al. 2003; Collin 2010). Early ancestral eutherian mammals are inferred to have retained three of these visual pigments based on the opsin repertoire from living species, with two of them, short wavelength-sensitive 1 (SWS1) and middle/long wavelength-sensitive (M/LWS), being expressed in cone photoreceptor cells for color vision (Hunt et al. 2009). Instead, prototherian mammals lost the SWS1 but possess SWS2 pigment (Sakamoto et al. 2024). The SWS1 pigment in the eutherian ancestor was inferred to be ultraviolet sensitive, with a maximum absorption sensitivity (λmax) of ∼360 nm (Yokoyama et al. 2016), with violet- or blue-sensitive pigments subsequently emerging in some diurnal lineages, such as the ancestor of the order Cetartiodactyla (Emerling et al. 2015). The M/LWS pigment reconstructed from the early eutherian ancestor was considered to be yellow sensitive (552 nm), while the spectral sensitivity of this pigment in extant species ranges from 490 to 560 nm (Yokoyama and Radlwimmer 1999; Liu et al. 2018; Chi et al. 2020). For rod-mediated dim-light vision, the ancestral mammalian rhodopsin (RH1) reconstructed by sequences from living taxa mediates a maximum absorption sensitivity at ∼500 nm, which is conservative in most vertebrates but has been blue sensitive (∼480 nm) shifted in species that have adapted to deep-sea environments (Yokoyama, Tada, et al. 2008; Xia et al. 2021).
Although all eutherian mammals have retained a functional RH1 for dim-light vision, independent losses of the cone-expressed SWS1 and/or M/LWS pigment have been reported in diverse lineages, which leads to a degeneration of color vision (Emerling and Springer 2014, 2015; Meredith et al. 2013; Emerling et al. 2015; Springer et al. 2016). For fully aquatic cetaceans, including whales, dolphins, and porpoises (Thewissen et al. 2007), independent losses of the SWS1 pigment in ancestral branches of sperm whales and baleen whales have been identified, and thus all living species of this group have lost violet sensitivity (Meredith et al. 2013; Emerling et al. 2015). Subsequently, the M/LWS pigment was also independently inactivated in several cetacean lineages, including sperm whales, some baleen whales, and a beaked whale, indicating a total loss of cone pigments and rod-monochromatic vision for these species (Meredith et al. 2013). In addition to M/LWS, some other cone vision-related genes, e.g. GNAT2 and CNGA3, possess inactivation mutations in the sperm and baleen whale clades (Springer et al. 2016).
Color vision has been proposed for echolocating toothed whales, which might be mediated by M/LWS and RH1 pigments acting together (Griebel and Schmid 2002; Griebel and Peichl 2003). Several reports have suggested that M/LWS and RH1 of early mammals have experienced adaptation to the dusk and dawn light niches, a time period when both of these pigments could be active (Davies et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2018, 2019; Guo et al. 2023). Interestingly, for M/LWS, two predicted blueshifts (shorter wavelength-shifted) in spectral sensitivity have been reported in the ancestral branches leading to delphinoids and baleen whales (Meredith et al. 2013), yielding proteins with spectral sensitivities closer to that of RH1, although the exact shift of sensitivity is not known. Phenotypic assays of visual pigments have been carried out for many cetacean species (Fasick et al. 1998; Fasick and Robinson 2000; Newman and Robinson 2005; Bischoff et al. 2012; Dungan and Chang 2017; Xia et al. 2021; Gai et al. 2023). Despite this knowledge, a systematic evolutionary study on the spectral tuning of both whale visual pigments, especially M/LWS, is still lacking, which hinders our understanding of the early ecological adaptations of this fully aquatic mammalian group. Moreover, to compare the difference in spectral tuning between M/LWS and RH1 pigments in individual species, we conducted in vitro functional assays across representative cetacean taxa in this study.

## Results

## Spectral Tuning of M/LWS Pigments from Extant Cetaceans
Living cetacean species, such as the Indus River dolphin (Platanista minor), franciscana dolphin (Pontoporia blainvillei), and baiji (Lipotes vexillifer), possess M/LWS pigments that are sensitive at ∼550 nm, which is similar to the inferred value of ancestral mammals (Liu et al. 2018). For the Amazon River dolphin (Inia geoffrensis), although an A308P substitution has been identified in its M/LWS sequence, no obvious λmax shift (549 nm) was observed. In contrast, the M/LWS pigments from the Yangtze finless porpoise (Neophocaena asiaeorientalis) and the long-finned pilot whale (Globicephala melas), both of which are from the superfamily Delphinoidea, have measured λmax values of 521 and 520 nm, respectively (Fig. 1a and supplementary fig. ## Amino Acid Substitutions of M/LWS Pigment in Whales
To infer ancestral sequence for this gene in sperm whale clade (superfamily Physeteroidea), all available M/LWS sequences from multiple individuals were used, and amino acid substitutions at critical sites were identified in either ancestral branch leading to Physeteroidea (S107G and S134A) or Physeteridae (Y277H) (Fig. 2a). We also sequenced the variable parts of the coding sequences from both the sperm whale (Physeter catodon) (site 134) and the pygmy sperm whale (Kogia breviceps) (exon 4). Our results add further evidence that site 134 of the sperm whale M/LWS is alanine (Fig. 2b), and the later part of exon 4 from the pygmy sperm whale was deleted (Fig. 2c). Although the ancestral Physeteroidea M/LWS gene had an intact coding sequence, our functional assay shows that this pigment has no light sensitivity in vitro (Fig. 1a and supplementary fig. ## Phenotypic Evolution of M/LWS during the Evolution of Whales
After inferring and resurrecting the ancestral M/LWS of Cetacea, our results show that this pigment is yellow sensitive (λmax = 551.7 ± 0.6 nm). Moreover, the maximum absorption sensitivity (550 nm) of the M/LWS of Delphinida is close to that of the cetacean ancestor. However, the M/LWS pigment of the baleen whale ancestor was found to be shifted to green-sensitive, with a λmax of 512.3 ± 0.6 nm (P < 0.001, Student's t-test) (Fig. 1a and supplementary fig. S1 and table S1, Supplementary Material online). This blueshift (40 nm) in sensitivity is mediated by two critical amino acid substitutions at S107G and A308S (Fig. 3).
Mutagenesis of critical sites in ancestral visual pigments. Site-directed mutagenesis of the M/LWS pigment from the ancestor of Cetacea. The λmax values for both the dark spectrum and the dark minus light spectrum are shown.
Notably, the S107G substitution occurred in parallel in the stem lineages of both sperm whales and baleen whales, which could lead to an 8-nm λmax shift to a shorter wavelength, and the S134A replacement can result in a 13-nm λmax shift. The Y277H substitution can lead to a 13-nm λmax shift, which may have also occurred in the ancestral lineage of Physeteroidea within the missing sequence in the pygmy sperm whale gene (Figs. 1a and 2a). Thus, additional mutagenesis experiments to generate multiple-mutant sequences were undertaken that showed a λmax of 532 nm for sequences with S107G + S134A and 521 nm for sequences with S107G + S134A + Y277H (Fig. 3).

## Selection Pressures on Cetacean M/LWS
Selection analyses on M/LWS genes from sperm whales further support the elevated estimated level of selection pressure (neutral evolution) (both branch and clade model C), but not positive selection (branch-site model), acting upon the sequences in this clade (Table 1). The M/LWS gene in the baleen whale clade also exhibits a class of sites experiencing a higher ω (dN/dS) value than seen in other lineages, although still significantly less than 1 (clade model C; see Table 1). ## Molecular Evolution and Spectral Tuning of Cetacean RH1
After inferring the ancestral RH1 sequences for cetaceans (Fig. 1b), spectral sensitivities of visual pigments from focal living taxa were subsequently determined. The RH1 pigments from the Yangtze finless porpoise, beluga (Delphinapterus leucas), baiji, pygmy sperm whale, and pygmy right whale, had measured λmax for this pigment of 491, 500, 494, 482, and 484 nm, respectively (Fig. 1b and supplementary fig. S3 and table S1, Supplementary Material online). The λmax difference between M/LWS and RH1 was observed to be significantly smaller in cetacean species possessing green-sensitive M/LWS pigments than in species with yellow-sensitive ones (P < 0.001, Student's t-test) (Fig. 4).
λ max differences between M/LWS and RH1 pigments from extant cetacean species. λmax difference between the two pigments is significantly smaller in species with shorter wavelength-shifted M/LWS (31.4 ± 4.5 nm) compared with species with unshifted form of this pigment (59.3 ± 7.4 nm) (P < 0.001).

## Discussion
For the deep-diving beaked whales (family Ziphiidae) (Tyack et al. 2006; Minamikawa et al. 2007), in addition to the reported loss of the M/LWS gene in the Sowerby's beaked whale (Mesoplodon bidens), due to a unique 4-bp insertion (Meredith et al. 2013), the Baird's beaked whale may also have lost cone-mediated vision despite possessing an intact coding sequence. No measurable light sensitivity was detected for the Baird's beaked whale M/LWS, which suggests that it is affected by the specific in vitro conditions utilized or an unstable pigment similar to a previously reported case (Sun et al. 1997), although the specific mechanism responsible for the lack of light sensitivity needs further functional verification. Multiple lines of evidence revealed here suggest recent losses of cone-mediated vision in this deep-diving clade, which is also supported by fossil evidence that the stem beaked whale could be epipelagic (Lambert et al. 2015). Interestingly, echolocation is only used during deep diving by beaked whales (Tyack et al. 2006); thus, vision might still be useful for some of these species when they periodically appear on the ocean surface. Although the M/LWS pigment from Cuvier's beaked whale still shows a conserved light sensitivity at 550 nm, whether it has a biological function needs further elucidation.
Within baleen whales (Mysticeti), the pygmy right whale is the only species with an intact coding region for M/LWS (Meredith et al. 2013). However, in our experiments, no detectable light sensitivity was detected for this pigment. In addition to the reported inactivation of the GNAT2 and PDE6C (McGowen et al. 2020), several other cone vision-related genes (e.g. PDE6H, CNGA3, and CNGB3) were also found to be inactivated in the genome of this species, strongly suggesting a loss of cone-mediated vision. Evidence suggests that the green sensitivity retained from the baleen whale ancestor was lost independently (Meredith et al. 2013). This conclusion is further supported by our selection analyses, which show a class of sites possibly experiencing relaxed purifying selection after the divergence of the baleen whale species.
The M/LWS gene has been reported independently inactivated in the sperm whale and pygmy sperm whale lineages after their divergence (Meredith et al. 2013). Our in vitro assay on the ancestral M/LWS of Physeteroidea suggests that this pigment may have already lost its light sensitivity in the common ancestor of these two lineages, although this gene still had a complete coding region. Relaxed purifying selection found in this ancestral branch adds further evidence that this gene was not functionally critical for the sperm whale ancestor. Furthermore, a cone-specific gene CNGA3 has also lost its function in the common ancestor of sperm whales (McGowen et al. 2020).
Interestingly, the ancestral M/LWS sequence revealed several critical amino acid substitutions at the origin of sperm whales that could have led to a λmax shift. Our functional assays of these mutants suggest that shorter wavelength shifts could have occurred before the pigment became inactivated in the ancestor of the sperm whales. Moreover, both the S107G and S134A substitutions found in whales show a greater impact on spectral tuning (8 and 13 nm) than previously reported (Chi et al. 2020). These results further suggest that both of these sites could be critical for the spectral tuning of M/LWS in addition to the known “five-site” rule (Yokoyama, Yang, and Starmer 2008) and thus should be considered when predicting λmax values.
Our functional assays on cetacean visual pigments first report an ancient blueshift of sensitivity for M/LWS pigment on the lineage leading to Physeteroidea, in addition to the reported cases for the ancestral baleen whales and Delphinoidea (Meredith et al. 2013). Moreover, a 40-nm λmax shift of M/LWS toward a shorter wavelength sensitivity has been observed during the origin of Mysticeti. Strong purifying selection on this ancestral lineage leading to baleen whales further indicates a critical role for visual adaptation in early baleen whales. Published λmax values for species from Delphinoidea, such as harbor porpoise (Phocoena phocoena) and bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops truncatus), are 522 to 524 nm, which is mainly determined by the A308S substitution (Fasick et al. 1998; Newman and Robinson 2005). Our in vitro assays for M/LWS from the Yangtze finless porpoise and the long-finned pilot whale show that both pigments are green sensitive (∼520 nm), with both being close to their predicted values based on residues at critical sites (Yokoyama, Yang, and Starmer 2008).
Evidence from the evolution of RH1 phenotypes supports that the common ancestor of all living cetaceans could dive into the deep sea (>200 m) (Dungan et al. 2016; Xia et al. 2021; Dungan and Chang 2022), where the environment is enriched with blue light (∼480 nm) (Warrant and Locket 2004; Yokoyama 2008). Thus, the independent shorter wavelength shifts of the M/LWS pigments could indicate ancient adaptations of these early whales (ancestors of Physeteroidea and Mysticeti) to further increased deep-sea activities, also supported by fossil evidence (Kimura and Hasegawa 2022; Bisconti et al. 2023), although these pigments could still be light sensitive during surface activities such as breathing. In contrast, the retained ancestral yellow sensitivity of M/LWS from river dolphins (Indus River dolphin, franciscana dolphin, baiji, and Amazon River dolphin) could be adaptive to their riverine or estuarine/coastal habitats (Cassens et al. 2000), where turbid water may be enriched with long wavelength light (Costa et al. 2013; Shi and Wang 2020). Likewise, the West Indian manatee (Trichechus manatus), which is from the other group of fully aquatic mammals, has a M/LWS pigment sensitive to 556 nm and also has a functional SWS1, both of which are in line with their shallower diving habits (Newman and Robinson 2005). For pinnipeds (semiaquatic mammals), no shorter wavelength shift of M/LWS pigment was observed, possibly due to their terrestrial activities (Newman and Robinson 2005; Levenson et al. 2006).
By adding more phenotypic values for RH1 of living species, a systematic comparison of λmax values for both M/LWS and RH1 pigments in each species could then be conducted, considering potential conditional color vision between cones and rods (Davies et al. 2012). Interestingly, a divergence between the λmax difference for M/LWS and RH1 pigments was observed among cetacean species. Similar to the primate M/LWS pigments (Nathans 1999; Carvalho et al. 2017), a smaller difference between the wavelength sensitivities of M/LWS and RH1 (∼30 nm) in cetacean species possessing green-sensitive M/LWS pigments may allow mesopic dichromatic vision under blue environments of the deep sea (Griebel and Peichl 2003; Davies et al. 2012), as suggested in the case of shark M/LWS and RH1 (Theiss et al. 2012). This suggestion is also supported by evidence of color vision seen in dolphins (Griebel and Schmid 2002). Although possibly due to either extremely increased deep diving (Collum and Fritts 1985; McAlpine 2018; Xia et al. 2021) or night predation by the baleen whales (Meredith et al. 2013), it is still not clear why all modern sperm whales and baleen whales have lost their M/LWS pigments independently.

## Materials and Methods

## Opsin Sequence Collection and Sequencing
All cetacean and outgroup opsin sequences (M/LWS and RH1) were obtained from the NCBI database (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Our data set includes coding sequences of M/LWS and RH1 genes from 35 and 34 species, respectively, representing 14 cetacean families that cover diverse ecologies, with detailed information shown in supplementary table S2, Supplementary Material online.
For the variable site (position 134 for opsin) identified in the sperm whale M/LWS gene, we sequenced this region from another individual by performing PCR (forward primer: 5′ TCT CCT CGC TCC GTG TCT 3′ and reverse primer: 5′ GAG CGC ACG TAC TTC TGG 3′). Part of exon 4 sequence of pygmy sperm whale M/LWS gene was also sequenced by PCR amplification (forward primer: 5′ CCC TGC CCA CTT CAG GAT G 3′ and reverse primer: 5′ CTA AAG GCA TGT GGG CCG TA 3′). Genomic DNA from both species was obtained from Nanjing Normal University, where they were extracted from tissue samples of dead individuals found in the wild.

## Ancestral Sequence Reconstruction of M/LWS and RH1
Amino acid sequences of M/LWS and RH1 were aligned using ClustalW in MEGA X (Kumar et al. 2018) (supplementary data S1 and S2, Supplementary Material online). Best-fitting models (JTT + Γ for M/LWS and JTT + I + Γ for RH1, according to the corrected Akaike information criterion) for the aligned opsin sequences were selected using ProtTest 3 (Darriba et al. 2011). Ancestral sequences for M/LWS and RH1 pigments in whales were then reconstructed using PAML 4 (Yang 2007) (supplementary data S3, Supplementary Material online). The topology of the species for the focal species was from the published literature and used for the ancestral sequence reconstructions (Upham et al. 2019; Lloyd and Slater 2021) (supplementary data S4, Supplementary Material online).

## Spectral Tuning of Cetacean Visual Pigments
Complete coding sequences of cetacean M/LWS and RH1 were synthesized and ligated into the expression vector pcDNA3.1 (+) (Invitrogen), with an expression tag (ACA GAG ACC AGC CAA GTG GCG CCT GCC) added to the 3′ ends. Plasmids were transfected into human embryonic kidney (HEK293T) cells using Xfect transfection reagent (Clontech) for 48 h. By incubating the collected cells with 11-cis-retinal (chromophore), visual pigments were regenerated, which were then, solubilized using n-dodecyl β-D-maltoside (Macklin). The regenerated visual pigments were finally purified using the Rho 1D4 antibody (The University of British Columbia). The λmax values for each purified visual pigment were measured with a U-3900 spectrophotometer (Hitachi) both before (dark spectrum) and after light bleaching (difference spectrum from dark minus light spectrum). The dark spectra were used for the comparisons among the RH1 pigments, while the difference spectra were compared for the M/LWS pigments given an unreadable dark peak, although both dark and difference peaks are similar (supplementary table S1 and data S5 and S6, Supplementary Material online). Detailed purification and measurement procedures were carried out following published protocols (Liu et al. 2018; Xia et al. 2021). According to published literature (Sun et al. 1997; Yokoyama and Radlwimmer 1999; Newman and Robinson 2005; Yokoyama, Tada, et al. 2008; Gai et al. 2023), a single purification for each of most visual pigments of cetaceans was measured given the high repeatability for spectral tuning, although for M/LWS, pigments from the two ancestors (Cetacea and Mysticeti) that show a drastic λmax shift were verified by three independent experimental replicates.

## Mutagenesis
To determine the functional effect of specific sites on spectral tuning of the ancestral M/LWS pigment, we conducted site-directed mutagenesis at key sites to introduce the S107G, S134A, Y277H, S107G + A308S, S107G + S134A, and S107G + S134A + Y277H mutations into the ancestral pigment sequence of Cetacea (Yokoyama, Yang, and Starmer 2008; Chi et al. 2020). All mutagenesis experiments were performed using FastPfu DNA polymerase (TransGen Biotech) followed by the original plasmids being digested by DpnI (New England Biolabs). After transformation, mutants from single colonies were verified by sequencing on an ABI 3730 (Applied Biosystems).

## Selection Pressure Tests on M/LWS Gene
To test whether shorter wavelength-shifted clades experienced different selection pressures compared with other lineages, clade model C was applied to the M/LWS gene sequences with PAML (Bielawski and Yang 2004). Sperm whale and baleen whale clades were set as the foreground individually and were assumed to have a class of sites having divergent ω values. The results of this model were compared with the null hypothesis generated using M2a_rel (Weadick and Chang 2012). To test whether M/LWS genes experienced changed selective pressure on the ancestral lineages leading to focal cetacean clades (Physeteroidea and Mysticeti), the two-ratio model, which assumes a different ω compared with other branches, was applied, which were then compared with the results obtained with the one-ratio model (the same ω value for all lineages) using PAML (Yang 1998). Moreover, the branch-site model was used to test whether any positively selected site(s) existed in either the ancestor of Physeteroidea or Mysticeti (Zhang et al. 2005).
