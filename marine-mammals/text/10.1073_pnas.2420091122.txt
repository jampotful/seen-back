Genetic ablation of the TET family in retinal progenitor cells impairs photoreceptor development and leads to blindness.

# Genetic ablation of the TET family in retinal progenitor cells impairs photoreceptor development and leads to blindness

## Abstract
SignificanceRod and cone photoreceptors are the most important light-sensing retinal neurons, and disturbances in their development lead to various retinal dystrophies from which millions of people in the world go blind. To date, many signaling cascades have been identified that are required for the development and function of photoreceptors. In our study, we show that DNA methylation, as an epigenetic mechanism, controls the activity of these signaling cascades. We found that DNA methylation suppresses the function of these signaling cascades, while active DNA demethylation controlled by the Ten-Eleven Translocation (TET) family of dioxygenases is required for photoreceptor development and function. Disturbances in the process of DNA demethylation during photoreceptor development lead to retinal dystrophy and blindness.Keywords: TET proteins, DNA methylation, epigenetics, photoreceptors, development
The retina is a tissue containing several layers of neurons supported by Müller glia, which are responsible for converting light into electrical signals and transmitting them to the brain to create the sensation of vision (1, 2). Impaired retinal development and function invariably lead to blindness that has a devastating impact on the normal lives of millions of people around the world (3–5). Understanding the mechanisms regulating retinal development allows us to restore visual function by imparting activity to abnormal retinal cells or via the regeneration of lost retinal cells. While significant progress has been made in understanding the signaling cascades that regulate retinal development, the contribution of epigenetic mechanisms to these processes is poorly understood (6–10). Meanwhile, understanding epigenetic mechanisms is critical since they can simultaneously influence multiple signaling cascades that regulate retinal development. The objective of this study was to explore the role of DNA methylation, as an epigenetic mark, and DNA demethylation, as an epigenetic mechanism, in retinal development.
DNA methylation refers to the modification of cytosines into 5-methylcytosines (5mC) (8, 11). DNA methylation in regulatory elements (e.g., promoters, enhancers) is often associated with reduced expression of the corresponding gene, while DNA demethylation should occur to promote gene expression (8, 11–13). However, this association is not strict and requires experimental confirmation every time (14). The mammalian retina is unique from an epigenetic perspective since the promoters of many genes responsible for its development and function are hypermethylated in retinal progenitor cells (RPCs) and in some retinal precursor cells (15–17). Meanwhile, these promoters are hypomethylated and the expression of the corresponding genes is high in mature retinal cells (15–17). However, the pathway responsible for changes in DNA methylation patterns during RPC differentiation into mature retinal cells and its significance in retinal development and function were not understood until this study.
The patterns of methylated cytosines in DNA are established by the DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) family of enzymes that catalyze the transfer of a methyl group to the 5th position in the cytosine nucleotide (8, 11). The Ten-Eleven Translocation (TET) family of dioxygenases is responsible for DNA demethylation, which occurs in several steps involving the participation of additional signaling cascades and ending with the appearance of unmodified cytosines (12, 13). In this study, we investigated whether the TET-dependent DNA demethylation pathway is involved in retinal development and function. We found that genetic ablation of the TET enzymes in RPCs leads to developmental abnormalities in various retinal cell types. However, the photoreceptors experienced the most significant pathological changes, which was accompanied by their death and retinal dystrophy. These findings indicate the contribution of epigenetic mechanisms to retinal development and function.

## Results

## Genetic Ablation of the TET Family in RPCs Impedes Retinal Development and Function.
The TET family, composed of the Tet1, Tet2, and Tet3 genes, is highly expressed at all stages of retinal development, including the earliest stage when the retina consists mostly of RPCs (Fig. 1 A and B) (18). To study the role of the TET-dependent DNA demethylation pathway in retinal development and function, we used transgenic animals in which exon 3 of Tet3 and the exons encoding Tet1 and Tet2 catalytic domains were flanked by loxP sites (Fig. 1C) (19, 20). These animals were crossed to produce triple Tet1/Tet2/Tet3-floxed animals (TET, Fig. 1D). To inactivate the TET family in RPCs, we used Chx10-Cre mice (Chx10/Vsx2 is a marker of RPCs) that have been used extensively by many investigators to study retinal development (Fig. 1D) (21). It was shown that Cre recombinase-expressing Chx10 positive RPCs differentiate into all types of retinal neurons and Müller glia (21). We found no significant morphological and functional differences between TET and Chx10-Cre animals (Fig. 1 E–I). However, when we crossed TET and Chx10-Cre to produce Chx10-TET mice in which RPCs lacked the activity of TET enzymes, the differences were apparent. While the size of TET eyeballs was relatively smaller than those of Chx10-Cre, the size of the eyeballs of the Chx10-TET mice was significantly smaller than those of TET and Chx10-Cre (Fig. 1E). The retinas of Chx10-TET mice had virtually no photoreceptor outer segments (OS) and outer plexiform layers (OPL) (Fig. 1F). The virtual absence of the OPL resulted in the cells of the outer nuclear layer (ONL) and the inner nuclear layer (INL) mixing (Fig. 1F). Electroretinogram (ERG) tests conducted on dark-adapted animals showed ten times less Chx10-TET rod photoreceptor activity compared to TET and Chx10-Cre [b-wave (mean ± sem uV): TET 266 ± 14 vs. Chx10-Cre 256 ± 14 vs. Chx10-TET 21 ± 4; a-wave: TET −123 ± 11 vs. Chx10-Cre −104 ± 7 vs. Chx10-TET −17 ± 3; n = 10 to 20 eyes tested]; the Chx10-TET ERG traces were almost flat (Fig. 1G). Similar results were obtained in the light-adapted (cone) animals [b-wave (mean±sem uV): TET 50 ± 3 vs. Chx10-Cre 53 ± 6 vs. Chx10-TET 14 ± 2; flicker: TET 35 ± 2 vs. Chx10-Cre 28 ± 3 vs. Chx10-TET 6 ± 1; Fig. 1 H and I]. All these results indicate that, unlike TET and Chx10-Cre animals, Chx10-TET mice were functionally blind.

## TET-Deficient Photoreceptors Have Underdeveloped OS and Synapses or Lack OS and Synapses Completely; They Die Overtime.
We undertook more detailed studies of Chx10-TET and TET animals since Chx10-Cre animals were previously well investigated and did not differ much from TET mice. First, we examined the thickness of the retinal layers of postnatal-day (P) 14, 1-, 3-, and 6-mo-old Chx10-TET and TET mice. The thickness was measured every 200 µm starting from the optic nerve head (SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2). This thickness was then averaged (Fig. 2A). We found that OS and the OPL (photoreceptor ribbon synapses are in this layer) were poorly developed or absent in the retinas of very young Chx10-TET animals (P14 and 1-mo-old), while OS and the OPL of TET animals were well developed (Fig. 2A). A more detailed analysis of our IHC data indicated the presence of underdeveloped OS of cones in P14 and 1-mo-old Chx10-TET mice, but their virtual absence in 3- and 6-mo-old Chx10-TET animals (the conclusion was made based on staining with PNA, which is a marker of cone OS; Fig. 2A). The OS of the rods were absent in Chx10-TET mice of all ages (Fig. 2A). We found some differences in the thickness of the ONL (all photoreceptors are located in this layer) in P14 and 1-mo-old Chx10-TET compared to TET mice. However, these differences began to become more significant with age, reaching as few as three lines of nuclei in some parts of the ONL of 6-mo-old Chx10-TET animals (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Thus, TET-deficient photoreceptors die over time. However, we found only a few dead cells in 3-mo-old Chx10-TET retinas using TUNEL assay (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). These findings suggest that cell death occurs slowly in Chx10-TET retinas and surrounding cells manage to successfully phagocytose the dead cells. While we did not find significant changes in the thickness of the INL and inner plexiform layer (IPL) in retinas of Chx10-TET and TET mice of all ages, the structure of the INL of the Chx10-TET retinas was severely disorganized (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). We found that the size of Chx10-TET retinas was smaller than TET (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). These observations are consistent with the fact that Chx10-TET mice have smaller eyeballs than TET mice (Fig. 1E).

Since the most significant changes were observed in the OS, ONL, and OPL, we used transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to examine the changes that occurred in them at high resolution. TEM examination revealed well-developed OS in photoreceptors of TET animals (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Meanwhile, the OS were underdeveloped or completely absent in photoreceptors of 1-mo-old Chx10-TET mice (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). We found a clear separation in TET retinas of the ONL from the INL by the OPL in which photoreceptor ribbon synapses were present (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The cells of the ONL and INL were mixed in the retinas of the Chx10-TET mice; synapses were difficult to find in these retinas (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). All these abnormalities in the structure of TET-deficient photoreceptors led to the fact that even 1-mo-old Chx10-TET mice were already functionally blind according to ERG tests (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). We would also like to note that Chx10-Cre animals exhibit mosaicism in the expression of Cre recombinase in RPCs, which leads to the fact that the effect of TET genetic ablation can be stronger or weaker in different parts of the retina (21). We found this effect during the TEM examination. While in most Chx10-TET retinas the OS and synapses were poorly developed or absent, small areas could be found where the OS and synapses were well developed (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). This could explain the presence of weak photoreceptor activity in Chx10-TET animals assayed by ERG.

## Disruption of the TET-Dependent DNA Demethylation Pathway in RPCs Affects the Development of Nonphotoreceptor Cells in the Retina.
We also evaluated the contribution of the TET-dependent DNA demethylation pathway to the development of other RPC-derived retinal cell types. First, we analyzed the density of RGCs in the ganglion cell layer (GCL) of 3-mo-old mice. We found that density was lower in Chx10-TET compared to TET mice (RGC per mm2: 3,044 ± 109 vs. 3,557 ± 91, P value < 0.01, n = 5; Fig. 3A). The retinal area was also smaller in Chx10-TET mice (13.2 ± 0.9 vs. 16.3 ± 0.2 mm2, P value < 0.01, n = 5, 3-mo-old mice; Fig. 3B). A smaller retinal area and RGC density result in a smaller total number of RGCs and, as a result, a smaller number of axons and, accordingly, a smaller thickness of the Chx10-TET optic nerves (Fig. 3 C and D). We also assessed the density of ChAT-positive amacrine cells (ACs) in the GCL and the INL of 3-mo-old mice. We found that the density of TET-deficient ACs was lower in the GCL (Chx10-TET vs. TET: 637 ± 26 vs. 933 ± 15, P value < 0.0001) and higher in the INL (1,344 ± 119 vs. 1,078 ± 27, P value < 0.05, n = 5; Fig. 3E). It is important to note that the lamination (stratification) of AC dendrites in the IPL was already severely disrupted in the retinas of very young (1-mo-old) Chx10-TET mice (Fig. 3F). The linear density of bipolar cells (BCs) in the INL was lower in Chx10-TET than in TET mice (0.17 ± 0.02 vs. 0.25±0.01, P value < 0.01, n = 5, 3-mo-old; Fig. 3G). BCs were disorganized in the INL of Chx10-TET mice (Fig. 3G). We were unable to perform a detailed analysis of horizontal cells due to their low density and disorganization in the INL of the TET-deficient retinas. However, we can state that horizontal cells are present in the retinas of Chx10-TET mice (Fig. 3F). Besides changes with retinal neurons, we found that the morphology of Chx10-TET Müller glia differed from that of control TET cells (Fig. 3H). At the molecular level, expression of a Müller glia marker Glul was reduced in Chx10-TET vs. TET retinas, likely reflecting the lower number of these cells in TET-deficient retinas (n = 3, 3-mo-old; Fig. 3I). At the same time, the expression of a reactive glia marker Gfap was significantly increased in Chx10-TET retinas (Fig. 3I). The results of the IHC analysis indicated some colocalization between Glul and Gfap in apical processes of TET-deficient Müller glia (Fig. 3H). An increase in the thickness of the Müller glia processes can also be seen in Chx10-TET retinas (Fig. 3H). These findings suggest that reactive gliosis may be occurring in TET-deficient retinas.

## Expression of Genes Required for Photoreceptor Development and Function Is Significantly Reduced in TET-Deficient Retinas.
The changes we observed in the morphology and function of TET-deficient retinas should be reflected in changes in gene expression. To this end, we investigated gene expression in the retinas of Chx10-TET and TET mice using RNA-seq analysis. The retinas were collected from P14 and 1-mo-old mice (1 m) and used for preparation of RNA-seq libraries for next-generation sequencing (NGS; P14, n = 3; 1 m, n = 4). We chose P14 and 1-mo-old animals because the thicknesses of the retinal layers differed to a lesser extent in Chx10-TET and TET mice at these ages. Meanwhile, we observed significant differences in gene expression between Chx10-TET and TET retinas (Fig. 4 A and B and Dataset S1). We were somewhat surprised by the lack of difference in the expression of Tet1, Tet2, and Tet3. However, when we considered the expression of only those TET transcripts in which all exons are retained, we found a significant decrease in the expression of these genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). These results indicate the stability of the Tet1, Tet2, and Tet3 transcripts even in the absence of several exons in them. To identify biological processes and cellular components that were affected by the inactivation of the TET-dependent DNA demethylation pathway in RPCs, we analyzed genes whose expression was statistically significantly (FDR < 0.05) changed (up or down) twofold or more in the Chx10-TET vs. TET retinas. Our results indicate that neurogenesis and synaptogenesis are increased in Chx10-TET retinas, which may reflect their incomplete development (Fig. 4C, up). At the same time, we found reduced expression of genes involved in biological processes and coding cellular components important for photoreceptors (Fig. 4C, down). A more detailed analysis revealed that the expression of genes required for phototransduction, as well as OS, inner segment, cilium, and synapse development and function, was significantly reduced in Chx10-TET retinas compared to TET (Fig. 4D and Dataset S1). These results can explain the morphological and functional changes we observed in Chx10-TET retinas (Figs. 1 and 2). One of the important observations was the significantly reduced expression of 56 genes, each of which leads to a form of either retinitis pigmentosa, cone or cone–rod dystrophy, congenital stationary night blindness, or Leber congenital amaurosis (Fig. 4D and Dataset S1). Our RNA-seq data agreed with the results of western blot analysis: Nr2e3, Pde6a, Pde6b, Prph2, Rho, Rcvrn proteins were virtually undetectable in Chx10-TET retinas (Fig. 4E). Our results indicated that most of the photoreceptor-related genes whose expression was decreased are critical for rod specification (Nrl, Nr2e3, Esrrb, and Samd7) and for rod and cone maturation (e.g., Rho, Opn1mw, Pde6a, Pde6b, Pde6g, Pde6h, Gnat1, Rcvrn, Grk1, Prph2, Rom1, Slc24a1, Mpp4, Cplx4; Fig. 4D and Dataset S1). It is important to note that the expression of some genes necessary for cone maturation was increased (e.g., Opn1sw, Pde6c, Gnat2, Gngt2, Arr3, Slc24a2, Cnga3, Cngb3, Fig. 4D and Dataset S1). Since genes involved in biological processes or coding components important for RPC-derived nonphotoreceptor retinal cell types did not appear in our analysis, we directly analyzed the expression of a number of genes responsible for the development and function of these cells. We found decreased and increased expression of several of these genes (Dataset S1). However, the number of such genes is much smaller compared to those involved in the development and function of photoreceptors (Fig. 4D and Dataset S1).

## Genetic Ablation of the TET Family in RPCs Prevents Demethylation of the Promoters of Genes Essential for Rod and Cone Specification and Maturation.
Impaired TET enzyme activity in RPCs should lead to alterations in DNA methylation patterns in the retina. To study DNA methylation patterns in Chx10-TET and TET retinas, we used P14 mice and whole genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS). The DNA samples isolated from retinas (Chx10-TET, n = 3, and TET, n = 3) were used in the preparation of 6 WGBS libraries for NGS. To analyze our NGS data, we used various bioinformatics tools. The PCA and CpG methylation clustering indicated a clear difference between the methylation patterns in DNA isolated from retinas of Chx10-TET vs. TET mice (Fig. 5 A and B and Dataset S2). By combining the capabilities of methylKit and annotatr Bioconductor packages, we formed a list of genes in which all promoters and first exons were hypermethylated in the retinas of Chx10-TET animals; methylation of at least one promoter or the first exon of these genes should be low in the retinas of TET mice (Dataset S2) (22, 23). We used this list to analyze the biological processes and diseases in which these genes are involved. We also investigated what cellular components of the proteins encoded by these genes could be a part of. We found that many genes whose promoters were hypermethylated in Chx10-TET retinas and hypomethylated in TET retinas were responsible for the specification and maturation of photoreceptors (Fig. 5 C–F and Dataset S2). Virtually all diseases were various forms of retinal dystrophies (Fig. 5D). Analysis of the DNA methylation and expression levels of these genes showed that high levels of methylation are associated with low levels of expression of these genes in Chx10-TET retinas (Fig. 5E). Conversely, low DNA methylation levels are associated with high expression levels of these genes in TET retinas (Fig. 5E). We also found significant changes in mean DNA methylation levels and methylation of individual cytosines in Chx10-TET vs. TET mice at locations close to the transcription start sites (TSS) of studied genes (Fig. 5F and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). These results suggest that high levels of cytosine methylation in TSS may interfere with the efficient initiation of transcription of genes necessary for photoreceptor specification and maturation, causing retinal abnormalities in Chx10-TET mice. We also found that a significant number of genes whose promoters remain hypermethylated in Chx10-TET retinas are important for rod specification and maturation (e.g., Nr2e3, Rho, Pde6a, Pde6b, Pde6g, Cnga1, Grk1; Fig. 5 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Dataset S2). Of note, there is a high level of methylation of Nr2e3 transcription factor, without which the specification of rods is not possible (Fig. 5 E and F) (7, 24). The high methylation level of this transcription factor in Chx10-TET retinas is associated with its low expression level (Figs. 4 D and E and 5 E and F). However, hypermethylation of promoters of genes that are important for the maturation of both types of photoreceptors (e.g., Prph2, Rcvrn, Cplx4, Aipl1) should affect cone function in TET-deficient retinas. We did not find hypermethylated promoters of genes important for RPC-derived nonphotoreceptor retinal cell development and function in Chx10-TET retinas. However, we cannot exclude that those hypermethylated genes that play an important role in the development and function of photoreceptors may also play some role in the development and function of other retinal cell types. One such example is Rcvrn, which plays an important role in photoreceptors and BCs. This could partly explain the changes that occur to RPC-derived nonphotoreceptor retinal cell types in Chx10-TET retinas.

## TET Enzyme Inactivation in RPCs Significantly Reduces DNA Hydroxymethylation Levels in the Retina.
The TET enzymes demethylate DNA by oxidizing 5mC into 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), 5-formylcytosine (5fC), and 5-carboxylcytosine (5caC) (12, 13). While 5fC and 5caC are quickly replaced by cytosines, 5hmC is stable in nondividing cells and can be detected (12, 13). We assess the effect of TET enzyme inactivation in RPCs on 5hmC levels in the retina. To this end, we used DNA isolated from retinas of P14 Chx10-TET (n = 3) and TET (n = 3) animals and the 5hmC-Seal technology that allows collecting DNA fragments enriched with 5hmC (25). To test specificity and efficiency of the 5hmC enrichment, we used spike-in DNA containing an equimolar mixture of 5mC- and 5hmC-rich DNA fragments. Analysis of our NGS data showed a significant excess of spike-in reads containing 5hmC over 5mC (more than 200-fold for TET and more than 500-fold for Chx10-TET). These results indicated a high level of specificity for our NGS data. We used spike-in normalization to correct for global changes in our NGS data (26). We found significant differences between Chx10-TET and TET samples using clustering and PCA (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B). The observed differences between the samples were due to the low levels of 5hmC in the retinas of Chx10-TET compared to TET mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C and Dataset S3). Our data also indicated that 5hmC levels were low in TSS and in CpG islands of TET retinas (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 D and E). The patterns of methylation and hydroxymethylation were very similar, which was not surprising since WGBS cannot distinguish 5mC from 5hmC (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 E and F). However, the pattern in the TET retinas corresponds to a combination of 5mC and 5hmC, while the pattern in the Chx10-TET retinas is formed only by 5mC, which can affect gene expression. We found that nearly 7,000 genes of TET retinas contain 5hmC-rich regions in their promoters or first exons (Dataset S3). Most of them could be classified into the group of housekeeping genes based on the GO analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S8G). Among these genes, we also found genes essential for photoreceptor development and function (SI Appendix, Fig. S8H). As we noted in the previous section, mostly unmodified cytosines are present in TSS of photoreceptor genes in TET (control) retinas. Meanwhile, cytosines located at some distance from TSS and considered to be methylated according to WGBS are, in fact, hydroxymethylated in the retinas of TET animals (SI Appendix, Fig. S8H). We can assume that TET enzymes complete their work in areas critical for the initiation of gene expression. Cytosines remain hydroxymethylated in areas less important for the onset of gene expression.

## TET-Deficient Retinas Are Depleted of Rods, While S-Cones in These Retinas Are Underdeveloped.
The results of the TEM examination indicated the presence of underdeveloped OS or the complete absence of OS in the photoreceptors of 1-mo-old Chx10-TET mice (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The presence of PNA and Opn1sw, and the absence of Rho and Opn1mw immunohistochemical staining in 1-mo-old TET-deficient retinas indicated that the underdeveloped OS were OS of underdeveloped S-cones (Figs. 2 A and 6 A–C). The results of IHC, RNA-seq, and western blot analyses of Rho expression confirmed the absence of rod OS in Chx10-TET retinas (Figs. 2 A, 4 E, and 6 A and Dataset S1). The presence of rod OS in some parts of TET-deficient retinas is a result of the mosaic nature of the Chx10-Cre strain (Fig. 6A′ and A″). Increased expression of Opn1sw and decreased expression of Opn1mw in TET-deficient retinas of 1-mo-old mice supported the finding that the underdeveloped OS belonged to S-cones (Figs. 4 D and 6 B and C). Underdeveloped OS were hard to detect in retinas of 3-mo-old and older Chx10-TET mice (Figs. 2 A and 6 A–C). However, S-opsin (Opn1sw) can be found in the body of many cells located in the ONL and partly in the INL of TET-deficient retinas (Fig. 6C). One of the characteristic features of rods is their specific inverted architecture of nuclei (27). This is expressed in the inverted organization of chromatin in the nucleus. At the same time, cone nuclei have a conventional chromatin organization: central euchromatin and peripheral heterochromatin. Our TEM results indicated that chromatin in the nuclei of the ONL of 1-mo-old Chx10-TET mice was less condensed compared to TET mice (Fig. 6 D and E). The number of nuclei with rod-specific inverted architecture was significantly lower in the ONL of Chx10-TET mice compared to TET animals (Fig. 6 D and F). The totality of the facts presented here indicates a low content of rod-like cells and a significant predominance of S-cone-like cells in Chx10-TET retinas. This observation can explain our RNA-seq finding that the expression of some cone-related genes is increased in Chx10-TET retinas compared to TET retinas.

## Discussion
Retinal cells convert light into electrical signals, which, upon traveling to the brain, create the sensation of vision (1, 2). Disturbances in retinal development lead to a variety of retinal dystrophies, which cause millions of people in the world to go blind (3–5). In this study, we describe an epigenetic pathway that regulates retinal development and how disturbances in its activity lead to retinal dystrophy. We found that the TET-dependent DNA demethylation pathway is involved in the development of many retinal cell types. However, it is the most significant contributor to rod and cone photoreceptor development. We found that genetic ablation of the TET family in RPCs prevents demethylation of the promoters of genes essential for rod specification and for rod and cone maturation. Preservation of methylated cytosines in the promoters of these genes significantly reduced their expression in photoreceptors. All these events interfere with the development of photoreceptor OS and synapses, depriving the retina of functioning rod and cone photoreceptors and resulting in blindness. Over time, the number of TET-deficient photoreceptors decreases, leading to retinal dystrophy.
The differentiation of RPCs into photoreceptors includes a specification stage when the photoreceptor precursors decide whether they will be rods or cones, and a maturation stage when the OS, synapses, and other components of the photoreceptors necessary for their function are formed (7, 24). Otx2 transcription factor is necessary for RPCs to adopt the fate of photoreceptor precursors (7, 24). Onecut1 and Onecut2, in turn, are responsible for cone specification in the presence of Otx2 (7, 10, 24, 28–30). Our findings indicate that DNA methylation does not affect the activity of Otx2, Onecut1, and Onecut2 and, thus, does not interfere with cone specification and appearance in the retina (Datasets S1 and S2). Nrl and Nr2e3 are the master regulators that determine the fate of photoreceptor precursors to become rods (7, 31–33). Nrl activates Nr2e3, whose role is to prevent cone specification and to promote rod specification in photoreceptor precursors (7, 31–33). Nrl is activated very early in the developing murine retina (embryonic day [E] 12), which should lead to early activation of Nr2e3, thus resulting in the early appearance of rods and a complete absence of cones in the retina (7, 31–33). However, this does not happen because Nr2e3 is activated very late (E18), just before the birth (7, 31–33). This delay most likely allows for the specification of the cone phenotype in the photoreceptor precursors early in retinal development. Our finding that the Nr2e3 promoter is hypermethylated in RPCs and its demethylation is necessary to activate Nr2e3 expression can explain the delay: Hypermethylation suppressing the activity of Nr2e3 early in retinal development allows for the formation of cone precursors in the retina, whereas demethylation of Nr2e3 late in retinal development results in rod specification. Besides Nr2e3, we found that Samd7, which is required for rod specification, remained hypermethylated in Chx10-TET retinas (Dataset S2) (34). Our findings also indicate that a further wave of promoter demethylation of at least Rho, Pde6a, Pde6b, Pde6g, Prph2, Cplx4, Grk1, Cnga1, Rcvrn genes is required to trigger the maturation of both rods and cones in the developing retina (Datasets S1 and S2). These results help to explain the fact that TET-deficient retinas are depleted of rods, while cones in these retinas are underdeveloped (Figs. 2 and 6). Thus, spatiotemporal activation of the TET-dependent DNA demethylation pathway is required for optimal development of cones and rods in the retina. However, in which cell types (RPCs, photoreceptor precursors, rod or cone precursors, etc.), when, and how often this pathway is activated during retinal development are problems that will require much effort to solve.
We have shown that impaired activity of the TET-dependent DNA demethylation pathway in RPCs results in reduced expression of many genes, mutations in which lead to one or more forms of either retinitis pigmentosa, cone and cone–rod dystrophy, congenital stationary night blindness, or Leber congenital amaurosis (Fig. 4D and Dataset S1). Thus, it is not surprising that we found significant alterations in the development and function of photoreceptors resulting in their death and retinal dystrophy. However, mutations in either TET1, TET2, or TET3 leading to retinal dystrophies have not been described to date, possibly due to the functional redundancy of these genes (12, 13, 19, 20). Meanwhile, global knockout of all three genes is lethal for embryos (35). Could, then, any retinal dystrophy be caused by disturbances in the process of DNA demethylation? TET enzymes cannot bind to hypermethylated DNA alone and require partners (transcription factors with a DNA binding domain) to initiate targeted DNA demethylation (36–39). If there are no such transcription factors or their activity is impaired, then the DNA demethylation process cannot be started even in the presence of TET enzymes. Mutations in transcription factors that prevent TET enzymes from binding to promoters can prevent demethylation of one or many genes necessary for the development and function of photoreceptors (e.g., Nr2e3, Rho, Pde6a, Pde6b, Pde6g, Prph2, Grk1, Cnga1, Cngb1, Nxnl1, Ush2a), leading to a form of retinal dystrophy. Phenotypically, it may be similar to the form caused by mutations, but this form of retinal dystrophy will be caused by epigenetic mechanisms in this case. Hard work lies ahead to identify partners of TET enzymes that promote demethylation of genes necessary for the development and function of photoreceptors.
We demonstrated in our study that the TET-dependent DNA demethylation pathway is required to a greater or lesser extent for the development of all retinal cell types. Since different retinal cell types are in constant interaction with each other, changes that occur in them as a result of TET enzyme inactivation may affect the development and function of their neighbors and vice versa. This imposes certain limitations on the interpretation of our data. To avoid these limitations, it would be necessary to inactivate TET enzymes in such a way that this inactivation affects one retinal cell type and does not affect the rest. We would like to point out the challenges that may arise in implementing this plan. First and foremost, TET enzymes must be inactivated in the population of RPCs or precursor cells of a given retinal cell type in which the process of DNA demethylation has not yet begun. There is no point in inactivating TET enzymes in cells where DNA demethylation has already occurred. In the case of photoreceptors, for example, we must inactivate the TET family in those RPCs or photoreceptor precursors in which the promoters of genes essential for photoreceptor development and function are still hypermethylated. We know for sure that the promoters of genes necessary for the development and function of photoreceptors are hypermethylated in the DNA of RPCs and hypomethylated in the DNA of mature photoreceptors. However, we have no information about whether DNA demethylation begins in RPCs or photoreceptor (rod and cone) precursors. We do not know whether DNA demethylation occurs in a single step or in multiple steps during photoreceptor differentiation. We do not even know when DNA demethylation begins. The same is true for all other retinal cell types. Thus, the study of the mechanisms of DNA demethylation during differentiation of RPCs into different retinal cell types is very important. Understanding these mechanisms will, in turn, allow us to evaluate the contribution of the TET-dependent DNA demethylation pathway to the development of each individual retinal cell type. However, we strongly believe that our results are of independent interest by integrating the development of different retinal cell types from RPCs and demonstrating which cell types are more and which are less affected by the inactivation of TET enzymes in RPCs.
Finally, our study has shown the significance of the TET-dependent DNA demethylation pathway in retinal development and function. However, because this is an extensive task, we still do not understand how DNA demethylation occurs during the differentiation of RPCs into various types of retinal cells. While genetic ablation of the TET enzymes in RPCs affects the development of all retinal cell types, the most dramatic changes were seen in photoreceptors. Our results indicate that DNA methylation, as an epigenetic mechanism, inhibits the specification and maturation of rod photoreceptors, possibly preventing their appearance at an early stage of retinal development. While there is no barrier to cone specification, DNA demethylation is required for cone maturation. However, given that rods are necessary for the survival of cones due to Nxnl1 activity, even a smaller influence of the TET-dependent DNA demethylation pathway on cone development and function will still inevitably lead to cone death (40). Thus, the contribution of retina-specific epigenetic mechanisms to the pathogenesis of retinal dystrophies may significantly change current approaches to diagnosing and treating these diseases.

## Generation of TET Conditional Knockouts.
To generate TET triple-floxed animals, we crossbred Tet1-floxed, Tet2-floxed, and Tet3-floxed animals (these animals are gifts from Dr. Anjana Rao, La Jolla Institute for Immunology). TET mice served as controls. To inactivate TET enzymes in RPCs, we used Chx10-Cre mice (strain 005105, the Jackson Laboratory). To generate triple conditional Chx10-TET knockout animals, we crossed Chx10-Cre and TET mice. Subsequently, we crossed Chx10-TET and TET mice in order to obtain Chx10-TET and TET littermates.
