Genome Assembly of Gangetic Dolphin (Platanista gangetica) Reveals Signature of Regressive Evolution.
Sharma SP, Ghazi MG, Das GC, Usmani AA, Badola R, Hussain SA. (2025). Genome biology and evolution. . doi:10.1093/gbe/evaf227. Licence: cc-by-nc. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Genome Assembly of Gangetic Dolphin (Platanista gangetica) Reveals Signature of Regressive Evolution

## Abstract
AbstractThe Gangetic dolphin (Platanista gangetica), an endangered freshwater cetacean endemic to the Indian subcontinent, exhibits distinctive morphological and physiological adaptations to riverine habitats. Here, we present a high-quality draft genome assembly of P. gangetica. The assembled genome is 2.53 Gb in size, with a scaffold N50 of 116.1 Mb and a scaffold L50 of 9. Genome completeness assessed using BUSCO against the mammalia_odb10 dataset indicated a genome completeness of 97.73%. We predicted 27,983 protein-coding genes, and 19,616 were confidently mapped to eggNOG ortholog groups. Phylogenetic reconstruction placed the divergence of P. gangetica from the common bottlenose dolphin (Tursiops truncatus) at approximately 30.9 million years ago. Furthermore, we identified 66 positively selected genes in the P. gangetica genome possibly linked to key traits, including sensory adaptations such as vision loss and enhanced hearing, cardiovascular and metabolic regulation, limb modification, and cognitive function. Demographic inference revealed historical fluctuations in effective population size, with a marked decline during the Last Glacial Maximum (∼20 to 30 kya), likely driven by climatic shifts and habitat changes. This assembly represents a substantial improvement over previously available genomes within the Platanista genus and provides an essential resource for advancing our understanding of freshwater cetacean evolution and forms a critical foundation for population genomic studies to inform conservation and management strategies.
The Gangetic dolphin, an apex freshwater predator, represents a unique model of aquatic adaptation under constrained riverine conditions. Environmental and geomorphological changes have influenced the evolutionary trajectory of this species, while recently intensified anthropogenic pressure now poses a serious threat to its survival. Despite its evolutionary uniqueness and specialized adaptations, our understanding of the genetic basis underlying its adaptation and evolution remains limited primarily due to a lack of genomic resources. In this study, we present de novo genome assembly of the Gangetic dolphin and investigate the genomic foundation of its ecological adaptation, morphological transformation, and sensory evolution. The genomic resources generated herein provide a critical foundation for studying the evolution of freshwater cetaceans and offer valuable tools for population genomics and conservation planning.

## Introduction
Cetaceans are a group of aquatic mammals, members of the order Cetacea, that transitioned from terrestrial ancestors into obligate aquatic mammals approximately 50 million years ago during the Eocene epoch (Marx et al. 2016; McGowen et al. 2020). The land-to-water evolutionary transition involved significant morphological, physiological, and genetic changes, facilitating the divergence into two distinct lineages—Odontoceti (toothed whales) and Mysticeti (baleen whales)—around 34 million years ago (Thewissen et al. 2009; Zhou et al. 2018; McGowen et al. 2020; Cooper et al. 2023). Notably, the predominant trait evolution in Odontoceti was echolocation, while Mysticeti underwent tooth loss (Gatesy et al. 2013). Additional evolutionary modifications include reduced olfactory and gustatory functions, specialized vision for low-light conditions, enhanced cognitive capabilities, increased body size, sophisticated social behaviors, and specialized respiratory and circulatory adaptations for deep and prolonged diving (Gatesy et al. 2013; McGowen et al. 2014; Berta et al. 2015).
Extant cetaceans occupy diverse ecological niches, distributed broadly across marine, estuarine, and freshwater habitats (Bianucci and Landini 2007; Jefferson et al. 2015). Nearly every species, with minimal or no overlap, occurs in distinct ecogeographic regions and adapted well to its respective climatic and geomorphological conditions (Thewissen et al. 2009). The Gangetic dolphin (Platanista gangetica) is listed as “Endangered” on the IUCN Red List and represents one of the most primitive and evolutionary distinctive cetaceans (Kelkar et al. 2022). The species diverged approximately 20 to 30 million years ago in the Miocene, following the Indo-Asian tectonic collision (Cassens et al. 2000; Yin 2000; Hamilton et al. 2001; Braulik et al. 2021). They are the descendant of long-snouted coastal dolphin forms that evolved during marine transgression episodes (Cassens et al. 2000; Hamilton et al. 2001; Lambert and De Muizon 2013). Gangetic dolphins are adapted specifically to the sediment-rich, turbid freshwater systems of the Indian subcontinent and exhibit distinctive evolutionary traits, notably the loss of functional eyes (Herald et al. 1969; Pilleri 1974; Purves and Pilleri 1978; Cassens et al. 2000; Chinnasamy and Sood 2020). The evolutionary uniqueness and specialized adaptations of the Gangetic dolphin provide an exceptional model to investigate regressive evolution. Genomic studies offer a powerful framework to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying these adaptations that drive evolutionary transitions. Genomic resources are thus essential to advancing our understanding of the species’ evolutionary trajectory and are crucial for developing effective conservation strategies.

## Results and Discussion

## Genome Assembly
We generated 1.5 billion 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads and retained 1.2 billion reads after removing the low-quality bases. The k-mer-based estimated genome size was 2.35 Gb, and heterozygosity was 0.30% (Fig. S1). The final assembly was 2.53 Gb, consisting of 13504 scaffolds with N50 of 116.1 Mb, and the largest contig size was 193.73 Mb (Table 1). The genome was submitted to GenBank under the Accession JBLRPT000000000 (BioProject Accession PRJNA1206299). The assembled genome exhibits significantly higher contiguity compared to previously available assemblies within the Platanista genus. The previously available Gangetic dolphin (P. gangetica; Accession No. GCA_017311385.1) genome had a contig N50 of only 3.1 kb, the Indus river dolphin (P. minor; Accession No. GCA_004363435.1) had a scaffold N50 of 23.9 kb, and the Yantze river dolphin (Lipotes vexillifer; Accession No. GCF_000442215.2) assembly had a scaffold N50 of 2.4 Mb (Table 1). In contrast, the contiguity of our assembly is comparable to the Amazon river dolphin (Inia geoffrensis; Accession No. GCA_036417475.1) genome assembly (Table 1). The final assembly demonstrated high read alignment rates when mapped with publicly available P. gangetica reads (Accession No. SRR13005646), with 133,623,107 reads (98.2%) successfully mapped.
Comparative genome assembly statistics and benchmarking universal single-copy orthologs (BUSCO) genome completeness of four river dolphin genomes and gene annotation BUSCO score for the Gangetic dolphin.
The BUSCO completeness scores for the assembled genome were 97.73%, indicating a highly complete assembly (Table 1). In contrast, publicly available P. gangetica and P. minor genomes showed markedly lower genome completeness of 26.05% and 58.25%, respectively. The genome completeness of P. gangetica genome reported in this study is comparable to other high-quality river dolphin genomes, including the Yangtze River dolphin (L. vexillifer; 98.23%) and the Amazon River dolphin (I. geoffrensis; 98.84%) and represents a substantial improvement over previous Platanista assemblies.

## Genome Annotation
The repeat annotation of the P. gangetica genome reveals that repetitive sequence constitutes approximately 43.64% (1.10 Gb) of the genome (Table S1). Retroelements represent the largest class of repeats, making up 37.16% (943.6 Mb). Within retroelements, the long interspersed nuclear elements are predominant, accounting for 26.49% of the genome, while short interspersed nuclear elements comprise 5.24% of the genome. The long terminal repeat elements account for 5.40% of the genome, predominantly represented by the Retroviral subfamily and unclassified repeats at 1.96% (49.7 Mb) (Table S1). The repetitive sequence composition in the P. gangetica genome is comparable to L. vexillifer (43.80%), while it is lower than I. geoffrensis (50.85%) (Table S1).
We predicted 27,983 protein-coding genes from the repeat-masked genome, and 19,616 were confidently mapped to eggNOG ortholog groups (Table S2). Of the 19,616 sequences, 14,543 sequences (74.14%) were assigned at least one Gene Ontology (GO) term with an average of 122 GO terms per annotated sequence (Table S2), while 5073 sequences (25.86%) were classified under the unknown function category. The majority of annotated genes were associated with cellular processes and signaling (n = 7,449; 37.97%), with the largest subgroup being signal transduction mechanisms (n = 4,291). The information storage and processing category accounted for 17.43% (n = 3420) of annotated genes, and metabolism-related functions represented 2423 sequences (12.35%), with the largest categories being inorganic ion transport (n = 468) (Table S2). The BUSCO analysis of the predicted proteins, using the mammalia_odb10 lineage dataset (n = 9,226), recovered 92.90% complete BUSCOs (8,543 genes), with 4.30% (n = 393) of the genes missing. A similar number of protein-coding genes were obtained from the genomes of closely related species, such as the Indus river dolphin (P. minor; 25,291), Indo-Pacific humpback dolphin (Sousa chinensis; 24,640), Yangtze river dolphin (L. vexillifer; 22,168), harbor porpoise (Phocoena Phocoena; 22,154), common dolphin (Delphinus delphis; 22,148), hourglass dolphin (Cephalorhynchus cruciger; 21,461), and rough-toothed dolphin (Steno bredanensis; 19,451) (Table S3).

## Divergence and Demography
Phylogenetic analysis showed the Gangetic dolphin diverged from the common bottlenose dolphin ∼30.89 million years ago (Mya) and from the clade leading to humans, mice, dogs, and cats ∼231 Mya. These divergence estimates align with previous cetacean phylogenies and support the early Oligocene radiation of freshwater dolphins (Chen et al. 2011 ; Zhou et al. 2011) (Fig. 1a).
a) Divergence time was estimated among nine vertebrate species using 920 single-copy orthologs and b) demographic history of the Gangetic dolphin inferred using the pairwise sequentially Markovian coalescent (PSMC).
From 117 positively selected genes, 66 high-confidence candidates were retained post filtering (Table S4). These genes were associated with key adaptive traits, including visual regression (e.g. TIMP3, RBP3), echolocation and auditory adaptation (ERAL1), limb reduction (GSX2, RDH10), cardiovascular efficiency (MRPL44), metabolic shifts (CPE, GUCA2B), and cognitive enhancements (CHAT). Many of these genetic signatures reflect adaptations to the turbid, low-visibility environments of freshwater ecosystems (Thewissen et al. 2009; McGowen et al. 2014).
Demographic reconstruction revealed an oscillating population trajectory with ancient declines around 200 to 300 kya (Fig. 1b) and the most recent population contraction during the last glacial maximum (20 to 30 kya) (Clark et al. 2009). These fluctuations correspond to climatic shifts that likely altered river systems, food availability, and habitat suitability (Hoegh-Guldberg and Bruno 2010; Zhou et al. 2013). Other possible explanations are changes in population structure, admixture, or migration (Mazet et al. 2016; Chikhi et al. 2018; Beichman et al. 2019). Our findings mirror patterns observed in coastal dolphin species and suggest Gangetic dolphins have experienced prolonged evolutionary and demographic pressures (Vijay et al. 2018).

## Conclusion
The genomic resources presented here provide a foundation for future evolutionary studies and conservation strategies, particularly in light of ongoing environmental changes and habitat fragmentation in the Ganga River system. The genomic resources developed are crucial for studying the evolutionary progression of freshwater cetaceans and their conservation. Enhanced conservation strategies must account for these genetic and demographic factors to ensure the long-term survival of the Gangetic dolphin. Overall, the genomic insights underline the Gangetic dolphin's unique evolutionary path and extensive specialization to freshwater life.

## Materials and Methods

## DNA Extraction and Genome Sequencing
The sample was collected from a dead individual from the Chambal River, and necessary permission was obtained from the Chief Wildlife Warden, Madhya Pradesh Forest Department (Letter No. 11732). High molecular weight DNA was extracted from a snap-frozen tissue sample obtained from a dead individual using MagAttract HMW DNA Kit (Qiagen Inc, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. The 10 × Genomics Chromium library was constructed using the manufacturer's protocol, and sequencing was done on the Illumina HiSeq X10 platform (Illumina Inc., USA). The raw data obtained were quality-checked using FastQC v0.11.9 (Andrews 2010), and adapters were trimmed using Trim Galore v0.6.5 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore). The duplicates read were removed using BB tools v38.39 (https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap). The base with a quality score of ≥ 30 was retained for the de novo assembly.

## Genome Assembly
The de novo assembly was generated using 10× Supernova assembler v2.1.1 (Weisenfeld et al. 2017). The adaptor and vector sequences contamination in the assembled genome were identified and removed using NCBI Foreign Contamination Screen (FCS-adaptor v0.0.5) (Astashyn et al. 2024), and assembly correction and scaffolding were performed with RagTag v2.1.0 (Alonge et al. 2022). Genome characteristics such as genome size, heterozygosity, and repeat content were determined using k-mer k-mer-based approach using Jellyfish v2.3.0 (Marçais and Kingsford 2011) and GenomeScope v2.0 (Ranallo-Benavidez et al. 2020).
In addition to our assembled genome, we downloaded publicly available genome assemblies of four freshwater dolphin species from NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) for comparative analysis (Table 1). Assembly metrics for all genomes were generated using QUAST v5.0.2 (Gurevich et al. 2013), and the genome completeness was assessed using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) with the mammalian_odb10 dataset in BUSCO v5.8.2 (Simão et al. 2015). Furthermore, to assess the quality and completeness of the assembled genome, we downloaded publicly available Illumina paired-end reads of P. gangetica (Accession No. SRR13005646) and mapped the reads to the final assembly using Burrows–Wheeler Aligner v.0.7.17-r1188 following default parameters (Li and Durbin 2010), and the read mapping statistics were generated using SAMtools v1.13 (Danecek et al. 2021).

## Genome Annotation
We generated a de novo repeat library using RepeatModeler v2.0.7 (Flynn et al. 2020), with default settings. The de novo repeat libraries were then concatenated with the Dfam-RepeatMasker library (https://www.dfam.org/releases/current/families) and annotated using RepeatMasker v4.1.9 (http://repeatmasker.org). In addition to the de novo assembled P. gengetica genome, we annotated two publicly available genomes of L. vexillifer (Accession No. GCF_000442215.2) and I. geoffrensis (Accession No. GCA_036417475.1). All genomes were unmasked before repeat annotation. The genome assembly of P. minor (Accession No. GCA_004363435.1) was not included in the repeat annotation due to low contiguity and completeness.
Gene annotation was performed on the softmasked genome using the BRAKER pipeline v3.0.3 (Gabriel et al. 2023). For evidence-based prediction, we used a concatenated database containing the vertebrate OrthoDB v11 (Kuznetsov et al. 2023) and the protein sequence of L. vexillifer and I. geoffrensis for gene annotation. To ensure nonredundancy, the database was curated to retain unique protein sequences. The resulting GFF file was filtered to retain only the longest isoform per gene using agat_sp_keep_longest_isoform.pl implemented in AGAT v0.7.0 (Dainat 2022). The corresponding protein sequences were then extracted from the filtered GFF file using GffRead v0.12.7 (Pertea and Pertea 2020). The functional annotation of the predicted genes was performed using eggNOG mapper Web (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/), and the completeness was assessed using BUSCO v5.8.2 (Simão et al. 2015) in protein mode with the mammalia odb10 lineage dataset.

## Phylogeny and Divergence
We used Orthofinder v2.5.2 (Emms and Kelly 2019) to identify single-copy orthologs among genomes of nine species—Danio rerio, Pelodiscus sinensis, Cervus hanglu, T. truncatus, P. gangetica, Homo sapiens, Mus musculus, Canis lupus, and Felis catus. The longest transcript was selected for genes with multiple transcripts, and corresponding nucleotide sequences of the identified single-copy ortholog genes were then subjected to codon-aware alignment using PRANK v.170427 (https://ariloytynoja.github.io/prank-msa/#using-prank). These alignments were then transformed into a concatenated supermatrix. After removing ambiguous sites, the aligned supermatrix was used to generate a Maximum Likelihood species tree using IQ-tree v2.0 (Minh et al. 2020). The tree constructed was then used to estimate the species divergence time. The Bayesian estimation approach was implemented in MCMCtree in (Phylogenetic Analysis by Maximum Likelihood) PALM v4.10.3 (Yang 2007) under the independent clock model. Two independent simulations were performed with 100,000 iterations as burn-in, a sampling frequency of 500, and a total of 500,000 MCMC iterations. We used soft constraints with three calibration points between the following species pairs (i) C. elaphus and Tursoips truncus 58 (95% credibility interval = 53 to 59.4 million years ago (mya), (ii) C. lupus familiaris, and F. catus 55.0 (52.9 to 57.3 mya) and (iii) M. musculus and H. sapiens 87.0 (81.3 to 91.0 mya) to obtain the estimates of divergence (https://timetree.org).

## Positively Selected Genes
We used the codeML branch site (two-ratio) test for each gene, keeping the Gangetic dolphin lineage in the foreground in PAML v4.0 (Yang 2007). The significance was calculated using the Log-likelihood Ratio test to identify positively selected genes. Multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995) method was employed to determine the false discovery rate for the P-values obtained using codeML. We used Bayesian Empirical Bayes (Yang et al. 2005) to identify positive selection sites at ≥95% and ≥99% confidence intervals and criteria of P ≤ 0.01 to determine significantly positively selected genes among the species. The genes qualifying for the inspections were subjected to GO enrichment with PANTHER (Mi et al. 2021). The functional and disease associations of the positively selected genes were investigated by searching the literature and databases such as Entrez Gene (Maglott et al. 2010), Uniprot (UniProt Consortium 2019), International Mouse Phenotyping Consortium (IMPC) (Muñoz-Fuentes et al. 2018), and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) (Hamosh et al. 2005), MalaCards—the human disease database (Espe 2018).

## Demographic History
We investigated the historical trend in effective population size (Ne) using a pairwise sequentially Markovian coalescent (PSMC) (Li and Durbin 2011). The PSMC utilizes a single genome to estimate demographic history, spanning thousands of generations (Liu and Hansen 2017). We applied a mutation rate of 1.5 × 10−8 per site per generation (Vijay et al. 2018) and a generation time of 17 years (Kelkar et al. 2022) for demographic reconstruction. We parameterized the initial PSMC runs with 64 atomic time intervals and 28 free intervals with the -p parameter set to “4 + 25 × 2 + 4 + 6” following a previously published study on cetaceans (Moura et al. 2014). Furthermore, we performed 100 bootstraps to obtain confidence intervals.
