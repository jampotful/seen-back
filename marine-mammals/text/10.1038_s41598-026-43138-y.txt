# Backseat driver architecture to passively follow sperm whales by their voices with an autonomous underwater glider

## Abstract
Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) of marine mammals is essential for biodiversity monitoring and communication studies. Tracking animals in situ further enables behavioral investigation. Monitoring from boats, tags, or moorings is limited by the durability of tracking, whereas underwater gliders offer potential for long-term PAM-based tracking. In this paper, we present the design details of backseat driver operations on such a glider. The backseat driver employs acoustic detection, source separation, and angle of arrival estimation of sperm whale (Physeter macrocephalus) echolocation clicks from a four-element hydrophone array mounted on top of a customized Project CETI-SeaExplorer glider. The measurements are used to change the bearing angle of the underwater glider in real time, allowing it to react to the presence of sperm whales and follow them. The glider is a passive system with no radiated noise except when changing buoyancy (a few seconds roughly every hour), and its distance from the whales is above 100 m to have minimal impact on whale behavior. We discuss the operation and connectivity of the backseat driver to the SeaExplorer glider’s main computer and how estimation results are shared with the user upon surfacing. While long-term whale monitoring is left for future work, our results from a controlled sea experiment demonstrate the backseat driver operation and show performance in terms of response latency to commands, and results from a field trial off Dominica show the backseat driver source separation capabilities. The code and data for this experiment are open source.

## Introduction
The ability to study the movements of sperm whales (Physeter macrocephalus) and better understand their behaviors and cultural dynamics is essential for developing effective conservation tools. Monitoring is conducted to understand the context of whale vocalizations1, foraging activity2, and diving patterns3. Current techniques rely on passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) through remote sensing from buoys or vessels4, or by attaching small acoustic tags to whales. However, existing technologies provide only short-term tracking either until the suction-based tag detaches and is retrieved, or while the whales remain within range of fixed acoustic monitoring systems. Consequently, long-term tracking across large distances remains difficult. Here, we propose using underwater gliders equipped with specialized acoustic packages as a means to track and monitor individual sperm whales over extended periods (months).
Gliders are autonomous underwater vehicles designed for long-term monitoring operations that last several weeks to months. Their simple design relies on shifting buoyancy to achieve horizontal movement without active thrusters, enabling slow but energy-efficient operation. Buoyancy changes are made by moving oil between internal and external bladders, while accounting for onboard sensors such as a depth meter, altitude meter, inertial sensor, and gyrocompass. Steering is achieved by moving the glider’s battery along a rail to change the vehicle’s center of mass, thereby adjusting its angle of ascent or descent. This results in a gliding motion that can be performed even against water currents. As shown in Fig. 2, the glider can change its pitch, heading, and roll by adjusting the battery’s position. The battery can move longitudinally along the glider’s body, shifting the center of gravity and changing the platform’s pitch. It can also rotate about the glider’s central axis, inducing changes in roll. These two actuation tasks are managed by embedded control algorithms, allowing the glider to manage its heading and navigate toward a target waypoint.
The SeaExplorer glider receives missions as individual actions, directions, and dive profiles, which can be updated via satellite communication each time the glider surfaces. This occurs every 2–4 hours, the time required for the SeaExplorer glider to reach its dive depth and return to the surface. Once at the surface, the system communicates and recalibrates its sensors, such as clocks and pressure sensors. Surveys on the design and operation of gliders can be found in5,6. The most frequent use of gliders is for oceanographic measurements to monitor sea temperature, salinity, and water currents7. However, gliders have recently been proposed for PAM monitoring.
Operating without a thruster, the glider is an extremely quiet mobile platform ideal for PAM. Existing configurations include placing hydrophones outside8 or within a wet section9 of the glider, with an internal recorder sampling noise for offline analysis. Data analysis has been demonstrated to detect marine animals10, characterize shipping activities11, monitor wind and waves12, and create noise maps13. However, glider functionality also enables the development of onboard PAM analysis, opening the potential for new applications.
Onboard analysis of acoustic data enables decision making and control of the glider while submerged. Possible applications include using underwater acoustic communication for glider operation synchronization, detecting nearby vessels for safer ascent, or identifying an acoustic event to change the glider’s mission in real time. These applications are commonly referred to as backseat driver operations, in which the vehicle is self-controlled by a backend processor. Backseat driver operations have been demonstrated in autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) to obtain a full scan of the seabed upon detecting a potential target14, or to change course once an event has been detected15. In the context of marine animal monitoring, gliders have been programmed to ascend once vocalizations have been detected. However, a full backseat driver for a glider, including angle of arrival estimation, has not yet been demonstrated.
Applying a PAM-controlled backseat driver to a glider enables tracking of sperm whales. Sperm whales can travel at 3–5 knots, with bursts up to 12 knots16, but typically remain in the same area for days, slowly exploring a small area radius16. During such exploration periods, gliders can match the whales’ speed, moving at 0.5 knot despite local currents6. Upon detecting sperm whale vocalizations, the glider can change course to approach the whales and remain in their vicinity for extended periods.
Remaining within proximity to whales to monitor their vocalizations and diving activity has the potential to advance sperm whale conservation biology and monitoring. When applied to bioacoustics, this approach can also increase legal protections for whale populations17. Since autonomous underwater gliders do not have active propulsion (such as AUVs with propellers), they are acoustically quiet and can record vocalizations from several kilometers away18. Following whales may affect their behavior, which is antithetical to the application of this technology. While it is difficult to evaluate this effect before recording such activity, we evaluate that the risk to be minimal. First, the glider is an extremely quiet platform that operates most of the time with zero radiated noise. Second, the monitoring operation uses passive acoustics and emits no signals. Third, by localizing whales from their emissions (e.g., using bearing-only target motion analysis19), the glider can maintain a minimum distance from the tracked whale (for example, farther than 1 km) to minimize impact. Finally, the glider is a much slower platform than sperm whales. Thus, a sperm whales can easily avoid a following glider if it perceives the glider as a source of disturbance.Fig. 1Concept of Passive acoustic monitoring (PAM)-based backseat driver via the Project CETI glider.

We propose and demonstrate at sea the integration of a backseat driver on a SeaExplorer glider. Our focus is enabling the glider to autonomously change its mission based on acoustic events. Our application is long-term PAM monitoring that can follow sperm whales to build a long-term database of echolocation clicks and coda signals from multiple whales for behavioral studies. In particular, following a whale enables the evaluation of its dynamics over time and space. As the signal strength increases when approaching a whale, it also becomes easier to identify the whale’s vocalizations, especially for detecting the whale’s coda signals and distinguishing them from those of other whales20. This may allow researchers to link the evaluated 3D location of the tracked whale and its nearby whales with their vocalizations for interaction studies. For example, it may be possible to record changes in whale dynamics in response to a coda sequence emitted by a nearby whale. A special case is the mother-calf pair, where coda signals are known to be emitted frequently.
The backseat is triggered by a hydrophone array. Processing is managed by a dedicated scientific controller, which performs the user’s specific scientific tasks. The scientific controller is connected to the SeaExplorer glider mission controller within a ROS framework. When the SeaExplorer glider controller receives a new command from the backseat operation, all built-in safety rules remain in effect, ensuring safe navigation of the glider. The onboard PAM processing includes real-time detection of sperm whale echolocation clicks, estimation of the angle of arrival for each click, separation of click sequences into individual whale sources, and determination of the bearing and elevation angles to a whale of interest, for example, by selecting the highest signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). The solution is tested for stability and reliability, and new bearings and depths are transmitted to the SeaExplorer glider for an onboard mission change and autonomous operation of the glider. The concept is illustrated in Fig. 1. While active sonar systems emitting signals must comply with exposure level standards21 to ensure the safety of marine mammals, our operation is passive. The SeaExplorer glider is a quiet platform with negligible radiated noise emissions, except when switching between ascent and descent modes for a few seconds roughly every hour. Therefore, the glider’s effect on noise-sensitive biofauna such as marine mammals, corals, or fish is minimized.

## Current approaches
Tracking marine animals with an autonomous platform offers significant potential for behavioral studies because the platform can stay within the animal’s vocalization detection range for extended periods. This requires both onboard tracking and the ability to change the vehicle’s mission on the fly.

## Survey on autonomous tracking of marine animals
A review of fish tracking strategies using autonomous platforms is provided in23, while the review in24 discusses the suitability of different autonomous vehicles for tasks such as biodiversity estimation and animal monitoring. Applications of monitoring marine fauna through acoustic tracking are discussed in25,26. Based on periodic emissions from an acoustic tag27, presents a fish tracking solution for an AUV. The bearing and range are estimated onboard and shared with other AUVs to reach a global consensus on the fish location and to determine a leader-follower strategy to approach the tagged fish. Tracking is based on a particle filter, and the fish motion model is derived from inertial measurements from the fish. The motion model can also be evaluated from visual observations in a semi-supervised manner28.
Vision tracking is used in29, where IMU and cameras are integrated to improve trajectory tracking. Another solution for tracking a tagged marine animal by an AUV is presented in30. The results show that maneuverability enables better localization than static observations. In31, an autonomous floater adjusts its depth to match that reported by an acoustic tag attached to a jellyfish. In32, an AUV was used to track an acoustically tagged shark with a two-hydrophone array. To resolve side ambiguities, a state estimate is obtained over time, considering possible maneuvers of the AUV or target. The method was demonstrated with an AUV tracking a tagged leopard shark for several hours. For improved localization of the tag’s emissions, a circular array is used in33. A demonstration of tracking a sea turtle showed the ability to approach the turtle closely enough for video recordings.
When active tags are not available34, demonstrated tracking sea turtles using a multibeam sonar. The turtle is identified by a CNN-based classifier, and the distance from the animal is maintained using sonar range readings. An extension for 3D multibeam sonar is presented in35. Tracking is performed by identifying the target and monitoring its dynamic state. The technique is demonstrated with a hovering AUV tracking a sea turtle. For sperm whale dive monitoring36, used an AUV capable of descending quickly to 1000 m. However, unlike the quiet glider platform, the results show that the AUV’s self-noise prevents localization of the whale based on its clicks. One option is to use aerial drones for monitoring whale activities37, but the monitoring duration is highly limited. A better fit may be to use underwater gliders as a monitoring platform. The use of gliders to detect sperm whale clicks has been demonstrated in38. However, the analysis was performed offline and the click detections were sporadic, leaving the possibility for mission change as future work.

## Survey on backseat driver
The concept of a mission change triggered by detected events is often referred to as a backseat driver, where a task running in the background of the main application generates an interrupt to change the vehicle’s current mission or directly assigns a new mission using a preset protocol. Such a backseat driver was implemented on an AUV in15 to improve the scanning of marine infrastructure based on concurrent sensor readings. In39, the backseat driver is designed to integrate new inputs into the vehicle’s dynamic control. Robustness in vehicle operation is achieved through a software architecture that enables mission changes by adjusting the vehicle’s navigation system. Another form of backseat driver uses underwater acoustic communication to send mission change commands across a network of underwater sensors40. This architecture allows changes to protocol parameters and operations while imposing limitations to maintain the safety of the underwater sensors. A similar method of control by a shore station over a surface vehicle to keep the target in sight is presented in41. A survey of such surface-submerged operations is provided in42.
The software architecture in43 proposes a two-layer process, where the direct layer manages AUV control and the backseat driver analyzes data from the vehicle’s sensors to adaptively modify the mission. A demonstration was conducted for oil plume detection. A different control approach is presented in44 for multi-vehicle coordination. In this case, the backseat driver was implemented as middleware with a preset protocol for mission changes. In45, the backseat was used with a different controller than the front-seat one, and mission change operations were treated as user commands by connecting the backseat controller to the AUV interface network. Mission changes for an AUV based on synthetic aperture sonar were demonstrated to capture sonar images of a possible wreck from different directions. A backseat driver can be easily configured using existing control architectures. In46, the MOOS-IvP marine robotics software was used to monitor the energy of a surface vehicle in real time and adjust the vehicle’s operation accordingly. A Robot Operating System (ROS)-based backseat was implemented on a glider in47. The backseat was set to change the glider’s ascent and descent patterns, but the operation was demonstrated only in simulation mode to avoid risking the glider’s safety. In this paper, we take a step forward and adopt ROS as a backseat driver architecture to change a glider’s complete mission based on acoustic detection and localization of sperm whales by vocalizations.

The structure of the CETI-SeaExplorer glider is shown in Fig. 2. The CETI-SeaExplorer glider is 2.7 m long, including its 0.7 m foldable mast, and has a diameter of 250 mm. It can dive between 50 m and 1000 m and perform operations such as ’V-shape’ (traversing between maximum and minimum depths), ’W-shape’ (traversing between multiple defined depths), or ’Drifting’ (maintaining a constant depth while drifting with the water current). The glider can also maintain its position by spiraling around the target location. The rates of ascent and descent are set by the user to optimize motion with respect to speed, and onboard automatic compensation of sideslip angle is applied to correct the glider’s course.Fig. 3Navigation results and acoustic signals captured during a sea trial in West of Dominica (Lat: 15.4272 Lon: −61.5581 (WGS84).

Navigation relies on dead reckoning between surfacings, during which GPS signals correct the glider’s position. Correction for water current (sideslip angle) is achieved by learning from previous depth-averaged current profiles, enabling a counter-reaction for the next dive. In one of our sea trials, the glider demonstrated remarkable navigation accuracy, with an error of only 80 m over a 20 km trajectory. In addition to GPS reception, surfacing is used to communicate with the user via Iridium, and the time spent on the surface — which poses a risk to the glider due to possible collisions with vessels — can be adjusted by the user. The acoustic payload for the PAM array is arranged in a tetrahedral configuration with a 15 cm distance between each of the four receiving elements. This configuration was chosen as a compromise between aperture and ambiguity. The 3D beam pattern and a slice along the elevation and azimuth angles are shown in Fig. 4. The array is located at the front of the glider and is aligned prior to deployment to enable orientation determination using concurrent gyrocompass measurements. An example of sperm whale echolocation clicks recorded by the array during a sea trial west of Dominica is shown in Fig. 3b. The glider also carries a conductivity-temperature-depth (CTD) sensor, which allows calculation of the sound speed profile in real time to support acoustic localization.
The glider can operate in either simulation or operation mode. In simulation mode, controlled experiments can be conducted with the glider held stationary, for example, from a boat, while bypassing all safety procedures. This enables testing of source localization and detection techniques, as in the experiments reported in50 and51, respectively.

## Backseat architecture
To enable smooth integration of backseat commands, the backseat driver is implemented as a process within the SeaExplorer glider’s science section ROS framework. Connected to all of the SeaExplorer glider’s sensors via the ROS network, this process can request data logs from each sensor and perform onboard processing of the raw data. Processing occurs in parallel with the SeaExplorer glider’s main operations on the scientific processor, ensuring that computation does not interfere with the glider’s smooth functioning. However, the main computer monitors the high power consumption from this processing as part of the power budget allocated to each sensor. The scientific processor is a Jetson Nano board running a Debian-like system with both CPU and GPU, and 4 GB DDR4 memory. Its main interfaces include four USB channels for connecting dedicated sensors (such as an acoustic projector), an Ethernet port, UART, I2C, I2S, SPI, and SATA. Processing supports Python programming through the ROS libraries.

The Jetson Nano controller, which handles backseat processing, is connected to the main vehicle controller via a serial port, but can also be accessed separately by the user through Ethernet for software development and testing. The other communication ports allow the Jetson Nano controller to connect directly to user sensors that are not originally part of the SeaExplorer glider sensor suite. Communication between the backseat controller and the vehicle controller uses two-way ASCII commands, while information transfer from sensors to the user via satellite communication is handled as a CSV file converted into a binary file.

The possible commands for the backseat driver are divided between mission control and operation control. Mission control commands include: 1) resetting the depth profile, top inflection depth, and drifting depth of the glider. Safety operations will ignore the command if it is not within the glider’s diving capabilities or if the altitude is insufficient for a deep dive; 2) setting the altitude-keep operation and its value on or off to determine whether the glider is constrained by a set altitude value from the seabed; 3) setting the heading of the glider to discard the existing mission and follow a target heading until the next ascent.
Backseat driver operation control commands include: 1) setting the ascend and descend bladder volumes to control vertical speed. Safety is maintained by discarding values that exceed limits for rapid descent or ascent. 2) Setting the descent and ascent linear offset, which affects the vehicle’s pitch angle. Values are permitted only within safety limits. 3) Setting the surfacing rate, which is the number of dives the glider will perform before its next surfacing. This command can enable immediate ascent of the glider if an event needs to be reported to the user. The simple ROS environment allows adaptation of these commands and easy addition of future commands, such as canceling an ascent attempt in case of high shipping noise.

## Acoustic processing
Fig. 6A block diagram for the acoustic operations for whale tracking.
A block diagram for the acoustic operations for whale tracking.
The processing chain for the PAM is illustrated in Fig. 6. The process begins by identifying echolocation clicks. While sperm whales p roduce other sounds such as codas and creaks, echolocation clicks are their most frequent signals1. These clicks occur in long sequences, which can be used to differentiate between multiple whales emitting simultaneously, which is a common scenario. The individual clicks are processed to estimate their angle of arrival on both the horizontal and elevation planes. The next step is sequence identification, where individual clicks are merged into sequences based on an ordered inter-click interval (ICI) and the estimated angles of arrival. The sequences are then separated into individual sources, and each sequence undergoes stability tests to measure its reliability. The stable sequences are evaluated to select a target source and estimate its bearing with respect to the glider. This triggers a backseat driver command for the glider to change its heading to follow the whale of interest. To avoid frequent changes in heading, the acoustic operation then pauses for a fixed period before restarting the click detection process. This waiting period is adjustable and decreases as the SNR increases, allowing fine-tuned tracking when the glider is close to the whales.
The acoustic process enables the user to monitor findings through the backseat driver’s information send/receive module. Information sent to the user includes the results of stability tests, estimated bearing, and SNR. The user may adjust the detection threshold if frequent non-stable sequencing results occur. ## Acoustic detection
The process of click detection is designed to distinguish echolocation clicks from other noise transients, such as snapping shrimp noises or waves. Although many click detectors exist (see52), backseat operation onboard a glider requires low complexity and minimal memory usage, with robustness across different marine environments achieved by using a small number of adjustable parameters. While sperm whale echolocation clicks are directional, the signals are emitted at high intensity, providing enough energy in the side lobes to allow detection. Results from approaches surveyed in52 show that these signals can be detected from distances of a few kilometers. This enables a glider following the whales from a distance to maintain continuous detection.
Our solution for click detection, described in53, uses the multipulse structure (MPS) of the clicks as the primary metric for distinguishing them from noise transients. Given a buffer of raw acoustic data, the method identifies possible clicks as periodic transient signals and applies a stability test to the clicks’ MPS. This MPS depends only on the whale’s orientation change relative to the receiver, not on the distance between the whale and the receiver, which is assumed to remain fixed for a short period. The process begins with the identification of a region of interest (ROI) around each possible click, followed by the calculation of the MPS for each identified ROI. The resulting MPS point cloud is then clustered into groups of consistent values that meet criteria such as acceptable ICI, click duration, and resonant frequency.
The MPS is measured by identifying the two most dominant identified pulses within the ROI, where pulses are detected as transient energy signals using the Teager-Kaiser operator (TKEO)54. Surface and seabed reflections are handled by clustering the clicks in the detected sequence by their time-of-arrival to distinguish between direct and multipath. The clicks associated with the direct path are then selected for further processing. Clustering stability is tested by solving a constrained optimization problem to guarantee acceptable ICI within predefined boundaries, a maximum number of clusters based on the expected number of simultaneously emitting whales, and a bound on the click duration. The problem is formalized as a binary programming due to the uniqueness of the clustering with factorial complexity, but it can be relaxed by an iterative process to perform clustering sequentially. Our implementation of the relaxed version on the Jetson Nano board shows a processing time of 1 sec for a batch with a 4 sec window.

## Angle-of-arrival estimation
The process of angle-of-arrival estimation is detailed in50. The main challenge is achieving high-resolution separation with the relatively small array of four elements. To address this, we use a correlation-based beamformer rather than a phased beamformer. Because the echolocation clicks are wideband transients, a correlation beamformer that cross-correlates the signals received by the hydrophones enables much higher spatial separation than a phase-based approach.
Angle estimation is performed for each ROI around every detected click. Angles are estimated using direction finding by calculating time delays between pairs of hydrophones. The angle is calculated geometrically, taking into account the sound speed at the glider’s current depth. Stability tests across ROIs allow for the removal of outliers. The process is refined by performing beamforming to synthetically steer the array by the estimated angle, enabling fine-tuned angle estimation at a higher SNR.
The process provides both bearing and elevation angles. However, when the depth difference between the glider and the whale is large, elevation estimation becomes inaccurate due to signal refraction, which cannot be resolved from a single location. To address this, we rely on the slow changes in depth and location of both the glider and the whale to obtain multiple elevation angle measurements at different locations, creating additional observation points to resolve these ambiguities. This is achieved by running ray tracing calculations for measurements obtained at different times, while carefully verifying that the same whale is being tracked by monitoring the stability of the bearing information, the SNR, and the characteristics of the identified clicks. That said, we clarify that the glider’s heading calculation depends only on the bearing angle estimation.

## Source separation
To study the behavior of sperm whales, continuous recordings are needed to capture changes in vocalization and link them to interactions, prey, or environmental events. The data must allow recognition of individual whales vocalizations so that behavioral changes can be associated with specific whales. This requires the ability to separate echolocation clicks by source. A glider capable of detecting and separating sources over long periods by following individual whales can provide the database needed for such a behavioral study. Equipped with backseat capabilities, the gliders perform this task while submerged. Additionally, the backseat driver enables integration of glider monitoring with other methods, such as tagging, by alerting the user to the whales’ locations.
The source separation module is designed to lock onto a single whale of interest for tracking. The goal is to cluster the identified click sequences into sources, rather than to identify the actual vocalizing whale. The results of this clustering are then processed through a decision procedure to determine which whale to follow, for example, using maximum SNR criteria. The clustering relies on similarities within the sequence in terms of the temporal and spectral features of the clicks, as well as the spatial stability of the first reflection from the water surface. The assumption is that the first reflection from a source whale will remain stable within a sequence time period of tens of seconds. The similarity test is performed over short periods, while the stability test is performed on long click trains.
Separation by feature similarity in short time periods is performed using a maximum a posteriori (MAP) formalization. The short time frames are then merged sequentially by another MAP process, again testing the similarities between calculated click features. The final clustering of the resulting sequence is based on the stability of the slant delay – the time difference between the direct arrival and the first reflection – while accounting for periods of no emissions or missed detection of clicks. Full details of the approach, along with the implementation code, are provided in51.

## Choice of reaction to a detection event
As shown in Fig. 1, once a whale is identified, the backseat driver can either surface to report findings to the user or follow one of the detected whales. This decision is controlled by a flag that the user can change remotely via the Iridium connection to the backseat driver. If surfacing, the glider reports the elevation and azimuth angles at which the whale was detected, as well as its own position and depth at the time of detection. This information can be used to locate the whale at the surface, for example, to support a tagging operation.
In the case of whale following, the backseat driver changes the glider’s heading direction. When multiple whale sources are detected, the heading change is preceded by selecting which whale source to follow. We used two criteria for this selection: 1) the average SNR of the clicks detected from each whale, selecting the whale vocalizing with the highest SNR, and 2) the whale with the highest number of associated clicks. Both criteria aim to select the whale closest to the glider, increasing the likelihood that the glider can catch up.

## Results
We now present proof-of-concept results from two experiments we conducted with the Project CETI-SeaExplorer glider. The experiments demonstrate our ability to perform closed-loop autonomy of the glider and to detect and estimate the angle of arrival of echolocation clicks from real whales using the glider, while demonstration of long-term whale following is left for future work. The first experiment is a controlled study conducted in southern France, in which sperm whale clicks were played back by a projector deployed from a boat. This experiment allows us to measure the response time of the backseat driver and the accuracy in determining the new heading. Since the playback sequence was limited in its click variation, we conducted a second experiment to explore the glider’s ability to detect a large number of real sperm whale clicks over a five-day survey. This second experiment was conducted off the coast of Dominica and presents results from sperm whale vocalizations.

The at-sea demonstration of the Backseat Driver on a Project CETI-SeaExplorer glider took place in southern France in July 2025; see the map in Fig. 7b. The goal of the experiment was to test the Backseat Driver module, including 1) heading change in response to a PAM event, 2) information transfer, and to measure operational latency. The experiment involved the Project CETI-SeaExplorer glider (see a picture from the experiment in Fig. 7a), which was deployed from a boat. The glider mission was pre-set to perform V-shaped dives to varying depths: 50 m, 100 m, and 150 m. Another dive to 200 m was also performed without acoustic emissions to test for false alarms. The water depth in the experiment area was approximately 300 m. The mission schedule is shown in Fig. 8a. Between each dive, the glider was programmed to report its detection results, and its heading timeline was downloaded. The dive was then repeated with a changed target location. The glider’s water current auto-correction module was disabled during the test to allow unbiased estimation of the heading change delay.Fig. 8Panel a: A plot of the diving profile during the experiment. Panel b: a time and spectral plot of the acoustic signals received by the glider during the experiment.

The deploying boat served as the vocalizing target. A 7–17 kHz commercial Evologics LF modem was deployed to a depth of 20 m from the boat. The modem was programmed to continuously emit a recorded sequence of 20 sperm whale echolocation clicks. The clicks were emitted at a level of 174 dB//1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document}Pa@1 m, with a frequency range of 2 kHz to 20 kHz. The average click duration was 1 msec, including multipulse, and the inter-click interval was approximately 1 s. To test the source separation algorithm, the sequence included clicks from three whales. The sequence ended with a linear chirp signal to mark breaks between sequences for offline processing. A plot of the acoustic signal received by the four hydrophones is shown in Fig. 8b. The signal is shown with high SNR, which further supports the use of gliders for marine mammal vocalization tracking.
The emissions were scheduled to start and stop while the glider was diving. Between each dive, the boat moved to a new location to test different bearing angles between the glider and the boat. The ground truth location of the boat was determined by a GPS receiver (accuracy of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 5$$\end{document} m), while the glider’s location was set based on its navigation system. The latter is expected to be accurate due to the short interval of the dives between each ascent and GPS fix. To increase the number of heading changes, during each dive and after the first click detection and heading change, the backseat driver algorithm was programmed to send additional heading changes to the glider’s main controller. These additional heading angles were uniformly selected at random. In this way, the first backseat driver heading change command is used to compare the calculated and actual bearing angle between the glider and the boat, while the other heading commands are used to examine the delay in the glider’s response. Dive 1 (50 m), Dive 2 (100 m), and Dive 3 (150 m) exhibited two, five, and four such artificial heading directions, respectively.
The entire block diagram in Fig. 6 was implemented on the glider’s scientific computer for real-time operation. The algorithm concatenated short 1 s files into 30 s buffers, from which clicks were detected, a target was determined, and a new heading was set based on the angle of arrival.

The glider’s heading position in three diving scenarios is shown in Fig. 9. The results are presented alongside the diving profile to provide context for the glider’s activity. Commands from the backseat driver are issued for each 30 sec detection time window. In all figures, three distinct heading command values are observed. The timing of these heading command shifts corresponds to changes in the location of the transmitting boat. During dive 4 (200 m with no acoustic emissions), no click was detected and no heading change was requested from the backseat driver. Figs. 9a and 9b show stable heading commands between each of the three location shifts of the transmitting boat. Fig. 9c shows some instability in the heading command at the last transmitted location, with changes of roughly 15 degrees.
Regarding the glider’s response to heading commands from the backseat driver, we observe convergence within a few minutes. Arguably, this is an acceptable delay for our application, considering that sperm whales tend to swim smoothly with no rapid turns55,56 at a moderate speed of 2–5 m/s57. The observed delay results from the relatively long time the glider needs to change heading by adjusting the position of its battery. In particular, without a thruster, a heading change relies on a control algorithm that gradually changes the battery’s angular position while monitoring the effect on the heading. Some overshoots occur during this process, for example, during the first heading shift command in Fig. 9a. Once the glider approaches the surface, its ability to change heading becomes limited, and the heading is mostly determined by surface waves. We observe this effect toward the end of the dive in each of the three profiles, where the glider struggles to achieve the required heading. In addition, some heading changes were not made to the required value. For example, for the third heading change requirement in Fig. 9b, the glider converged to a heading that differed by about 20 degrees from the required one. This response may be due to water currents that required a longer time for convergence. Spikes in the glider’s heading are observed when the depth profile changes from descent to ascent; during these changes, the heading values are discarded from the glider’s heading control. At this time, the glider changes its pitch angle and ballast position, which in turn affects the heading.Fig. 10A plot of the angular position of the glider’s battery during the second backseat driver heading change in the profile from Fig. 9a.

The results of the heading change accuracy are shown in Table 2. The table presents the absolute error between each heading change command of the backseat glider and the true angle between the transmitting boat and the glider’s location, based on their ground truth positions. Because the heading change is given relative to the current heading of the glider, the error is calculated using the logged heading of the glider just before the heading change command. The results show a larger error for Dive 1 and smaller errors for Dives 2 and 3. We attribute this difference to the fact that, in Dives 2 and 3, the emissions started later in the dive than in Dive 1, when the glider was closer to the sea surface. Closer to the surface, it is more difficult to separate multipath, which may have affected the angle estimation.
Table 2 also provides the average response time of the glider, including both the start of change and convergence time. The results for each dive are averaged for both the first real heading change and the subsequent artificial ones. The first is based on the angular position plot of the glider’s battery, as shown in Fig. 10, and the latter on the measured heading of the glider in Fig. 9. For the latter, we neglected the convergence time for heading change requests made when the glider was close to or at the surface, where it cannot fully control its heading due to waves. Considering that the logged changes in the glider’s main computer have a resolution of 20 sec (while that of the backseat driver is more dynamic), the results for the response time for all dives show an immediate response to the backseat driver heading change command. The results for the convergence time show a response of a few minutes, ranging from 2.6 min to 4.6 min. This relatively long time for heading change is due to the conservative heading control mechanism within the glider, which prefers a slow angular shift of the battery and expects slight side-slip angles from the water current. Still, assuming the whales’ motion is smooth, the number of heading changes during a tracking session is expected to be small.

Next, we describe the glider’s capabilities to detect echolocation clicks in a real environment. We show its ability to distinguish between sources, which plays a crucial role in behavioral analysis by enabling the simulated monitoring of several whales over long periods. The data can be used to explore mutual relationships and effects within the pod, and even to detect contextual meanings. Results from a glider deployment and experiment in July 2024 are described in Fig. 3, and are shown in Fig. 11. In this experiment, the glider did not follow the detected whales, and the demonstration focuses on the real-time capability of the glider to detect, separate, and estimate the direction of received echolocation clicks. The example in Fig. 11a shows a 3-second time buffer recorded by the glider, in which 37 echolocation clicks were identified. The different colors represent the separation of clicks into whale sources. The acoustic algorithm detected these clicks and identified seven different whale sources simultaneously emitting echolocation clicks.
Fig. 11b shows the elevation and azimuth angles for each identified click, using the same colors as in Fig. 11a. There is a clear separation of the whales in the elevation-azimuth plane. The clicks are densely clustered, which likely reflects the accuracy of the measurements, although no ground truth was available in this experiment. Here, based on both SNR and the number of detection criteria, the ”red” whale source was chosen to be followed.

## Discussion
Our results demonstrated the capability to self-control the glider through acoustic event detection. Although long-term whales tracking has not yet been achieved and remains future work, the demonstrated operation of the backseat driver shows great potential. The glider responds quickly and reliably to heading change commands and has proven to be a quiet platform for PAM monitoring. The simple ROS framework allows easy adaptation of the backseat driver, making it plug-and-play. The glider can cover large distances during its few weeks of operation, providing opportunities to track whales in situ over extended periods. The transfer of detection information upon surfacing keeps the user informed, with the option to change critical parameters and adjust operations based on real-time results.
However, the glider also has limitations for tracking marine animals. The biggest challenge is maintaining contact with the animal as it moves. The glider is a relatively slow platform (compared to vehicles with active propulsion) and holds the capability to quietly remain at a distance and passively monitor cetaceans, such as sperm whales. To address this, in future work we plan to implement our localization algorithm from19, which is based solely on bearing information. This will provide range estimation to the tracked whale, allowing the glider to maintain a minimum distance and reduce impact. Still, as a 2.7 m long, 250 mm diameter device, a sperm whale will likely detect the glider with its sophisticated biosonar even from this distance. Arguably, sperm whales will also be able to distinguish this object from a soft-bodied cephalopod prey. Another challenge in PAM-based backseat operation is the need to account for the glider’s operational state when computing, for example, the angle of arrival. When the glider operates its pump or changes the position of its battery, measurements such as orientation angles are affected due to changes in the magnetic field. Additional challenges arise when following distant whales whose vocalizations are detected only sporadically. This can lead to frequent changes in the glider’s heading. The same outcome can occur if the followed whale quickly changes its heading, emits overlapping clicks, or produces clicks in short bursts. Finally, while the glider is generally a quiet platform, it generates high radiated noise when moving its battery to ascend or descend. Although these operations are infrequent (a few seconds once in roughly one hour of deep dive), they are not time-synchronized with acoustic detection and can cause false detections.
Besides the PAM-based backseat driver for tracking marine animals, we see additional possibilities for application. The first is straightforward and was already demonstrated in58, where the glider ascends and reports the presence of nearby mammals. Implementing this over ROS increases flexibility, as different detection algorithms can be tested while leveraging the user’s information transfer capability to adapt the detector in real time. Another application is the ability to change the glider’s mission via underwater acoustic communication while the glider is submerged. This requires implementing an acoustic receiver protocol on the scientific computer and adapting the backseat driver to allow changes to waypoint positions, up to setting an entire mission. However, this operation carries risks to the safety of the glider. A third application is to adjust the glider’s depth in response to detected acoustic events. The stratified acoustic channel, with its strong depth-related sound speed profile, makes certain depth layers more suitable for detection. A further refinement is coordinating multiple gliders via the surface and report module of the backseat driver to survey different bathymetric layers.
The data collected by the glider’s backseat driver enables behavioral studies of sperm whales. The glider provides a long-term record of clicks and coda signals from multiple whales while distinguishing the sources of these signals. This results in a long-term dataset of whales clicks and coda, each associated with its source. Such data may allow exploration of temporal correlations between whale movements, diving characteristics, and potentially contextual inferences, all relevant to behavioral research. While similar information can be obtained from on-whale acoustic tags59,60 —a glider allows for much longer observation periods, spanning weeks, which can capture behavioral changes over extended time-frames. Therefore, the application of autonomous underwater gliders combined with this backseat driver architecture holds the possibility to greatly advance understanding of sperm whale communication, biology and population dynamics.
