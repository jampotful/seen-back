Predicting mesoscale movement of sperm whale units in the Caribbean based on social dynamics.

# Predicting mesoscale movement of sperm whale units in the Caribbean based on social dynamics

## Abstract
AbstractSperm whales (Physeter macrocephalus) navigate complex oceanic environments and social structures. In the waters off Dominica, female and juvenile whales form long-lasting social units and vocal clans, distinguished by unique click dialects known as codas. While prey availability is often seen as a driver of whale movements, we highlight the role of sociality in shaping spatial behavior. Using 20 years of photo-identification data, we examined the sequential presence of social units for predictable patterns linked to social structure. Applying long short-term memory (LSTM) neural networks to sequences of one to five days across 16 states including 14 units, mature males and unknown units, we achieved prediction accuracies over 60%, far exceeding random chance (0.00001526). We then compared unit-to-unit transition probabilities to their social association matrix using a Hemelrijk test, revealing strong alignment between movement and social bonds for some of the units. To support long-term monitoring, we developed an acoustic classification method based on inter-pulse intervals (IPIs) in echolocation clicks, serving as acoustic fingerprints linked to body size. Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) classified units with 78.26% accuracy. Our findings provide quantitative evidence that sperm whale movements are socially coordinated and predictable, offering new insights into the spatial and social dynamics of sperm whale societies and highlighting the role of social affiliation in shaping large-scale movement patterns.Keywords: Social analysis, Movement, Sperm whales, Inter Pulse Intervals, Echolocation clicksSubject terms: Ecology, Ocean sciences, Marine biology

## Introduction
The animal kingdom is in constant motion. Thousands of species shift their location across time and space: daily, seasonally, or yearly. Some migrate horizontally with changing seasons, seeking water sources or food patches in vast savannas1; some make diel vertical migrations through the surface and deep layers of the ocean to feed in nutrient-rich surface waters as well as to avoid visual predators2. Some species cross entire continents and oceans for the opportunity to mate, whereas others wander in unpredictable nomadic patterns3–5. Various factors influence animal movement. Abiotic factors, such as physical features like mountains or river-filled canyons create landscape heterogeneity that can limit movement and divide populations. In addition, anthropogenic features can disrupt habitats and interfere with migratory routes6,7. Biotic factors play an equally crucial role, particularly the presence and movement of predators8, prey9, species density10, and the primal drive to reproduce11. These forces and others compel animals to migrate in order to adapt, survive, and thrive. But when food and other resources are abundant and threats are minimal, what else may drive them to move?
Complex social species, such as many mammals, introduce additional layers to their movement dynamics. Inter-specific and intra-specific interactions like competition and communal care, as well as individual traits and preferences have been shown to affect individual movement12. Social hierarchies and group dynamics further shape movement, with individuals navigating their environment in relation to their social position or dominance12–14. At an even broader level of social organization, interactions between groups play a crucial role, as multiple social units sharing the same space may choose to converge, avoid one another, or even coordinate their movements. Thus, animal movement is not only dictated by the physical environment, but is also deeply influenced by the complex social structures within their communities.
This is particularly relevant for cetaceans, which range across much larger spatial15 and temporal16 scales than most terrestrial mammals. The open ocean, with its three-dimensional space and lack of physical boundaries, allows for diverse movement patterns among marine mammals17. For example, Orcinus orca (Orca) groups migrate seasonally in pursuit of herring18, while the movement of Balaena mysticetus (bowhead whale) is influenced by fluctuations in sea surface temperature19. Although numerous studies highlight the role of environmental factors in shaping cetacean movement, little is known about the impact of social affiliation, as observed in several terrestrial mammals, such as the movement of baboon species which can often be predicted based on the proximity of strongly affiliated nearest neighbors20. Similarly, the position of an individual within a social network can determine its role in the collective movement of the group13.
The sperm whale (Physeter macrocephalus, Linnaeus 175821) is a highly mobile and cosmopolitan species22. Sperm whale society is characterized by sexual segregation, multilevel associations, and cooperation. Adult females and immature individuals form stable groups that remain in tropical or subtropical waters year-round22. In contrast, males migrate to higher latitudes upon maturation, where they live alone or in loose aggregations with other males, returning to lower latitudes periodically to mate23,24. Females and juveniles form complex social structures, exhibiting long-lasting social bonds25, cooperative behavior26, mutual care for offsprings27,28, and collective defense against predators29. These long-lasting relationships typically occur within a social unit, consisting of mature females and their dependent offspring, who are often (but not always) related30,31 and move together as a group26. This form of social structure is not directly observed in the field but inferred from long-term associations. Unit members will usually stay with their unit for their entire lives and will move together in space, therefore, encountering one unit member will mostly suggest that the others are in the area25,32. In contrast, a sperm whale “group” is defined as all individuals encountered on the same day that were coordinating their movement and behavior22,25, but does not necessarily imply social affiliation between the present individuals. In the Pacific, two or more units are frequently observed associating for short periods (hours to days), forming temporary groups22. However, in the Caribbean, units are typically observed alone, but maintain preferred associations with other units, forming “bond groups”25,33. Units that are associated with each other belong to the same vocal clan, a higher-order social structure distinguished by their unique dialect of social vocalizations known as codas34,35. These vocal clans, which can coexist in the same regions (sympatry), can include hundreds to thousands of individuals35–37.
When foraging, sperm whales do not produce codas but instead use broadband echolocation clicks to navigate and locate prey in the dark deep ocean. These clicks are characterized by a multi pulse structure, with an intense first pulse followed by several pulses of decreasing amplitude38. This multi-pulsed click pattern results from the reverberation of the initial pulse within the spermaceti organ of the whale, located in its nose38,39. The inter-pulse interval (IPI), the time between the first intense pulse and the subsequent one, typically ranges from 2 to 9 milliseconds40,41 and is correlated with the size of the spermaceti organ, as it depends on the time it takes for the pulse to reverberate within it40 and, by extension, with body size. Therefore, in some circumstances, such as among small social units in the Eastern Caribbean, IPI has been used to distinguish between individual sperm whales of different body sizes within a unit34 and even to characterize entire units by their unique IPI distribution42,43.
Over short time periods, sperm whale movements are generally believed to be driven by food availability22,44. When feeding conditions are favorable, individuals tend to remain in one area for longer periods, whereas poor feeding conditions prompt them to move elsewhere22. However, in the eastern Caribbean, the distribution of sperm whales appears to be also strongly influenced by social factors, suggesting a complex pattern of overlapping home ranges25,45,46. Specifically, two vocal clans, EC1 and EC2, exhibit distinct small-scale distributions, each favoring particular islands in the Lesser Antilles. Since the habitat characteristics of these islands are relatively similar, site fidelity appears to be a key driver of the distribution of sperm whales in the region, shaped primarily by social structures at the vocal clan level45,46. This suggests that sperm whale units or individuals may prefer certain islands because they are familiar environments where they can associate with their families and other members of their vocal clan25.
The social preferences of sperm whales towards their vocal clan have been well documented in numerous studies35,37,45,47, as have their preferences within and between units33. Along the coast of Dominica, sperm whales are present year-round, with social units, primarily from EC1, varying in composition on a daily basis25. This region is home to one of the most extensively studied sperm whale communities, observed since the 1980s as part of opportunistic sightings, occasional research projects, whale watching citizen data collection and since 2005, as part of an established long-term research projects, The Dominica Sperm Whale Project and Project CETI. These long term studies show that in Dominica, when a sperm whale is present, its unit members are typically nearby, whereas other units are generally absent, suggesting that spatial movement is strongly influenced by social dynamics.
However, the spatial and temporal scales at which inter-unit associations influence sperm whale movements across the region remain an open question. Do units coordinate their movements along the islands? Do they detect the presence of other whales and leave, only to return the next day? Or do they actively avoid areas where they can hear the echolocation clicks or codas of other units? To what extent does the sequence of unit presence off the island reflect social interactions occurring at a broader spatial scale than at our study site? If social interactions between units operate at larger spatial scales, we would expect the ordering of units detected off the coast of Dominica to reflect their social preferences.
In this study, we tackle these questions using a two-decade-long photo-identification database from Dominica, collected between 2004 and 2024. By analyzing sequences of sperm whale social units present off the island, we train a predictive model to anticipate the next unit, revealing that their residency patterns are not random but shaped by high-level sociality. To push the boundaries of our understanding beyond fieldwork periods, we introduce an innovative method for passive acoustic identification of well-known sperm whale units, allowing for continuous monitoring of their residency patterns and offers new insights into the complex social dynamics driving their movements.

## Methods
The methodology of this study follows a structured sequence of steps to investigate the predictability and patterns of sperm whale social unit presence offshore Dominica. First, social units were identified using photo identification data. Second, the sequencing of well-known units was analyzed using long short-term memory (LSTM) neural networks, with the primary objective of uncovering underlying patterns rather than achieving high prediction accuracy. Third, the correct predictions of the model were examined to compute transition probabilities between social units. Fourth, the transition probabilities were investigated to identify common patterns in unit presence, including temporal gaps and varying residency durations. Fifth, sequences following the identified patterns were removed from the data, and the LSTM was re-run to uncover more hidden patterns. Finally, a passive acoustic method was investigated to monitor unit presence using inter-pulse interval (IPI) distributions, providing an alternative when photo ID data is unavailable. A detailed description of the methods is provided in the following paragraphs.

## Study area
The island of Dominica (15.30N, 61.40W), located among the eastern islands of the Lesser Antilles in the Caribbean Sea, lies within the home range of a community of sperm whale units that inhabit the area year-round. This community is part of a population of approximately 414 individuals (95% CI = 338–490)48 that resides in the eastern Caribbean Sea and likely moves frequently between Dominica and nearby islands. The population consists of 51 known social units (95% CI = 41–62), each averaging 7–9 individuals25,48. The long-term site fidelity of this community has allowed continuous behavioral research through the Dominica Sperm Whale Project (DSWP), which has studied these whales since 200525 in a 2,000 km² study area off the west coast of Dominica. As a result, individuals within these units are well documented both photographically and acoustically through photo identification and digital recordings.

## Field methods
Data were collected between 2004 and 2020 by the Dominica Sperm Whale Project (DSWP) in the study area25, with additional data gathered between 2020 and 2024 by Project CETI, following a similar protocol described in Gero et al; (2014)25. Opportunistic photo identification data collected by collaborative organizations between 1991 and 2009 was also used, as described in25. Fieldwork was carried out two to three times per year, typically in January to February and May to July. During daylight hours, clusters of individuals visible at the surface were approached to collect behavioral data, recordings, and photographs for individual identification based on markings on the trailing edge of their flukes49. An individual was considered part of a cluster if it was within approximately three adult body lengths ( 40 m) of another whale and if the whales displayed coordinated behavior, following the “chain rule” described in Whitehead (2003)22. When possible, solitary, photo-identified individuals were recorded after descending for a foraging dive. This was done to obtain clean on-axis echolocation clicks during their descent, which provides high-quality inter-pulse interval (IPI) measurements50. Additionally, systematic recordings were made every hour during an encounter to passively sample the IPIs of the whales present in the area. An encounter was defined as the period from the first confirmed acoustic detection or sighting of sperm whales -whichever occurred first- until two hours had passed since the last detection or until the decision was made to leave the animals22. Photographs were taken using Cannon DSLR cameras with fixed 300mm lens. Recordings were obtained by several types of hydrophones: C75 - one channel, 96Khz, 16bit, wav files, recorded using MixPre-6 (Sound Devices, LLC.). Soundtrap (Ocean Instruments, NZ) - one channel, 96Khz, 16bit, wav files. Towed array - A custom-built towed hydrophone (Benthos AQ-4 elements, frequency response 0.1–30 kHz) with a filter box incorporating high-pass filters up to 1 kHz, resulting in a recording chain with a flat frequency response across 2–20 kHz. Audio data from the towed array were collected through a computer-based recording system with a sampling rate of 48 or 96 kHz and 16-bit resolution, stored as WAV files.

## Photo ID and unit membership
Fluke photographs were used to identify individuals and define social units. Only high-quality photos with a quality score were included in the analyses49,51. The best available photo for each identifiable individual within an encounter was assigned a temporary identification code and then matched across encounters and against a digital catalog using either a computer-based matching program52 or the online platform Flukebook53. Since calves do not regularly fluke, they were individually identified based on the shape of their dorsal fin and distinct markings on their dorsal fin and body28.
Identified sperm whales were assigned to social units, defined by long-term, stable associations that persist across years. Twenty years of association data were analyzed to examine the standardized lagged association rate, as described in54, to determine the appropriate time frame for defining social units. A unit was defined by either one of the two different criteria based on previous work25,54,55: 1. A set of individuals for which each pair was observed associating in a cluster during at least three different years. 2. A set of individuals seen on the same day in at least two different years. The first criterion was used to better delineate frequently observed units, who may have associated in different years across the last two decades; while the second was applied to enable the delineation of less commonly sighted animals and define their units based on existing, less stringent definitions25. Once the initial list of units was established, an additional third criterion was applied to include individuals that had been observed in a cluster with any already-established unit members in two separate years. This approach accounts for less gregarious individuals, who may be more solitary but still travel with their unit over time. Unit membership is transitive, meaning that if A and B belong to the same unit and B and C are also unit members, then A and C are considered to be part of the same unit22.
Since units are defined based on long-term social data rather than direct observations, some of the rarer units are likely incomplete, as only a few of their members met the criteria for unit assignment. As a result, the average unit size does not accurately reflect the actual number of whales present in Dominica’s waters on a daily basis. To address this, observed group size was estimated by dividing each day’s identifications into two subsets, either by splitting at midday or randomly halving the day’s identifications, and applying the Petersen mark-recapture estimator to estimate the total number of individuals present56. From these estimates, mean typical group size, which represents the group size as experienced by a randomly chosen individual rather than from an external observer’s perspective57, was calculated following Coakes, 200456 and Gero, 201425.

## Unit sequencing analysis
A unit was considered present in the study area if at least one of its permanent members was observed and photographed during an encounter on a given day. Presence off the island was represented by a binary vector, where 1 indicated the unit was present and 0 indicated it was not. Additionally, the parameter “UNK” denoted the presence of a rare or little-known unit, or individuals that were not assigned to any unit, and “Male” indicates the presence of a mature male. If no unit was present, the vector contained only 0s. To capture the overall movement patterns of units off the island, a movement model was fitted using SOCPROG58, as described in Whitehead (2001)59. Movement analysis was conducted based on a single location, treating units as individuals, setting a day as the sampling period, and defining associations as sightings on the same day.

## LSTM neural networks
Long Short-Term Memory neural networks 60,61 were used to predict the presence of whale units the following day. Prediction function. Once trained, two approaches were used to make predictions, one without assumptions and the other with:
No assumptions - threshold: A simple entry-wise threshold operation was applied to the output values. Since the final layer of the network uses a sigmoid activation function, each output entry corresponds to a probability between 0 and 1, representing the likelihood of each whale unit appearing. A threshold of 0.96 was applied to the output values to obtain binary predictions. This relatively high threshold results from the weighting technique used during training, which increased the output probabilities toward 1. With this threshold, any output value greater than 0.96 is considered to be 1 (presence), while values below 0.96 are considered 0 (absence).
Expert knowledge assumption - top k: This approach assumes expert knowledge, simply, providing an estimate of how many whale units are expected to appear the following day (denoted k). Based on this estimate, the output probabilities were classified by setting the top k whale units with the highest probabilities to 1 (presence), while the rest were set to 0 (absence). This approach incorporates the assumption that the expert knows the expected number of whale appearances on the following day. Notably, this approach focuses more on the ordering of the probabilities of unit appearances less on predicting the number of appeared units.

## Transition analysis
The LSTM output reflects the patterns the model identified and utilized for predictions. Correct predictions from the LSTM models were then used to calculate transition probabilities between unit pairs, revealing sequential patterns in the data. A correct prediction was defined as a case where the model’s predicted sequence exactly matched the target sequence, correctly predicting all 0s and 1s across all positions in the vector. Within these correctly predicted sequences, transitions between unit pairs were counted and converted into probabilities by dividing the number of observed transitions by the total number of sightings of the originating unit. When multiple units were present on the same day, transition probabilities were calculated separately for each combination. This process resulted in a transition probability matrix encompassing all units in the dataset. These transitions only capture part of the patterns detected by the LSTM, while it may also identified higher-order dependencies that could involve subtle multiunit interactions, time-lag effects, or context-specific patterns that are difficult to represent with a single transition matrix.

## Social comparison
A high transition probability indicates that one unit is likely to be followed by another, which may be influenced by factors such as social affiliation. To explore this, a unit association matrix was created and compared to the transition probability matrix. An association was defined as a pair of units observed within two hours of each other, and a half-weight index was applied. The resulting matrix was then analyzed using a Hemelrijk test65, a variation of the Mantel test commonly used in ecological and behavioral studies to assess correlations between spatial or social relationship matrices. The test was undertaken using SOCPROG 2.9 in Matlab R2022b66.

## IPI data and distributions
IPI measurements were obtained from two types of recordings: Systematic recordings – Conducted for five minutes every hour during an encounter with whales, these recordings sampled the IPIs of the present unit using passive acoustic methods. First-click recordings – These were obtained by lowering a hydrophone into the whale’s fluke print (the location of the dive) and recording the first echolocation clicks of the foraging dive while the whale was still descending. At this stage, the whale most likely remained on-axis with the hydrophone, allowing for the capture of clear echolocation clicks from the targeted individual40,67,68. To ensure the recorded clicks belonged to the intended whale, these recordings were made only when the animal was in a singleton cluster.
The CETI click detector69,70 was used to detect clicks within each recording. All first-click recordings were filtered based on specific amplitude and multipath ranges characteristic of focal whale clicks. If a recording was too noisy for the automated detector to perform successfully, the first clicks were manually detected using Coda Sorter-a custom software developed by K. Beedholm at the Marine Bioacoustics Lab, Aarhus University, Denmark, implemented in LabView and run in MATLAB (version R2020a). The IPI was estimated based on phase, as described in71. All IPI measures from first-click recordings were then used to estimate the focal individual’s modal IPI, calculated as the mode of all IPI measures. These values were combined with the modal IPI of other unit members to construct a set of ground-truth IPI measurements for the specific unit. Systematic recordings were used only when no other social unit was within the hydrophone’s detection range. These recordings were then processed using the CETI detector without filtering to analyze all IPI estimations. The resulting IPIs were used to generate daily sets of IPI measurements, considering only days with at least three recordings to maximize the inclusion of individuals from the unit present in the study area.
The IPI measurements from the two recording types were compared using kernel density estimation (KDE)72–77 for four whale units which had enough recordings to represent all unit members. Each daily set of IPI measurements was compared to all four sets of unit ground-truth IPI measurements and was assigned to a specific unit based on the KDE with the highest posterior probability. Detailed description of the analysis steps can be found in the supplementary information.

## Results

## Social units
All available photo ID data from Dominica and neighboring islands were used to generate a comprehensive dataset of photo ID to establish unit composition. Data collection began in 1984 with sparse field seasons in the eastern Caribbean Sea until 2005, when systematic research efforts were initiated in Dominica and have continued uninterrupted until 2024 (and still going). A summary table of all available data and their corresponding analyses is presented in Table S2. Over these 40 years, data were available for 32 years, resulting in 1,037 days spent with whales. The mean number of identifications per day was 5.2 (range: 1–75, median: 4, SD: 6.09). During this time, 756 individual sperm whales (including adult males, adult females, juveniles, and dependent calves) were photographically identified. However, only 175 individuals met the criteria for unit assignment (excluding calves), forming 32 distinct units. Their composition and membership details are provided in Table S3. Seventy-one percent of the days spent with whales included only one social unit present in the study area.
Of the 32 identified units, 23 were sighted off the coast of Dominica between 2004 and 2024, with varying sighting frequencies (Figs. 1 and 2). Dependent calves, defined as small, nursing calves that do not perform fluke-up foraging dives when alone25, were observed in 20 of these units across multiple years but were excluded from the analysis, as their associations were dependent on their mothers. Additionally, 51 different mature males were observed over 61 days, but they did not meet the unit criteria, as they were only present in the area for short periods. These mature males typically interacted with multiple females, often from different social units25,36. The average unit size was 6.2 individuals (SD = 2.9, range: 2–12). However, as described in the methods, this estimate does not necessarily reflect actual unit sizes, since rarer units had fewer members that met the assignment criteria. The typical mean group size, calculated using two different methods, ranged between 6 and 8 individuals, supporting observations that most groups encountered at sea consist of a single social unit.

## Unit sequencing
To ensure a continuous dataset for sequential analysis, only data collected in Dominica between March 2004 and March 2024 were included. This period featured more detailed data collection, including effort measures, encounters, and cluster observations. During these two decades, a total of 907 days of effort were recorded, of which 673 days were spent with whales. These whales were part of 921 encounters. Occasionally with more than one distinct encounter on the same day, though not all encounters resulted in successful whale identification. On average, 4.41 individuals were identified per day (±2.75, range: 1–21, median: 4), while the mean cluster size was 1.44 (±0.8, range: 1–12, median: 1). This movement model accounts for emigration, reimmigration, and mortality rates, describing movement patterns in which most units remain in the area for only a few days, while a few are re-sighted over extended periods (ranging from six months to three years). The lagged identification rate declines to nearly zero after approximately three years, indicating that very few units are consistently re-sighted over longer timeframes. This pattern is further supported by residency periods (the number of days a unit remains in the study area), which have an average duration of two days and are distributed as shown in Fig. 2. Importantly, these values likely underestimate the duration of longer residencies off the western coast of Dominica, as the research boats would occasionally have shore days for rest, maintenance or resupply; and thus, breaking continuous sequences of days of identifications. Moreover, there is no information about unit presence when the research team is not in the field or the weather is not suitable for boat work.
Unit sightings were organized into daily vectors representing unit presence and absence. These vectors were segmented into 232 sequences of consecutive days, with gaps in effort marking the separations. Ten sequences consisted of single-day efforts, with no data available from the preceding or following day, while the longest continuous sequence spanned 31 days. The sequences included 16 possible states, comprising 14 social units (the more frequently observed units) seen on more than 1% of all whale sighting days, males and unknown units. When no units or males were present, the vector contained only 0s. To accurately represent transitions between unit presence states, a complete vector should ideally have been constructed, encompassing all possible combinations of unit presence off the island. This would have allowed the model to be trained on all states, enabling the application of a traditional Markov Chain78 to predict transitions between all possible states. However, the vast majority of these combinations were not observed in the data, making it infeasible to use them for modeling. As a result, states were defined at the unit level, treating each unit separately when modeling and calculating transitions.
To prepare the data for modeling, sequences were split into varying lengths using two different methods: Fixed Window Method: The sequences were divided into non-overlapping blocks of a predefined size. Each subsequence started immediately after the previous one ended. For example, if the sequence length was set to three, a longer sequence like ABCDEF would be split into ABC and DEF, without overlap. Sliding Window Method: The sequences were divided into overlapping blocks of a predefined size, with each new subsequence starting at a defined step size. For example, with a sequence length of three and a step size of one, the sequence ABCDEF would be divided into ABC, BCD, CDE, and DEF, creating overlapping subsequences. Sequences shorter than the predefined length but containing at least two daily vectors were retained in their original size. The vector representing the day immediately following each sequence was designated as the target for prediction. Due to the limited number of sequences, no separate validation set was employed, and the model was evaluated directly on the test data.

## LSTM Neural networks
Two hundred LSTM models were trained on the sequential data using the two prediction approaches described in the methods section. The models were trained on sequences of varying lengths, ranging from one to five days, generated using either a fixed or sliding window. To ensure reproducibility, ten different random seeds (1992, 123, 1, 777, 2025, 42, 974, 344, 222, and 0) were used. Each model was trained with six hidden layers for 200 epochs, using a learning rate of 0.01. Detailed explanation about these hyper parameters can be found in Table S4. The complete results are presented in Table S5, while the models with the highest accuracy scores are summarized in Table 1, sorted in descending order of test accuracy. The model that achieved the highest test accuracy, Model 96, was trained on 173 sequences of five-day intervals. However, because it utilized a sliding window approach-where targets can appear in the training data-this model was considered less optimal for LSTM learning. As a result, the second highest-scoring model, Model 3, was selected as the best-performing model. Model 3 was trained on 358 single-day sequences and achieved 140 correct predictions on the training set and 49 correct predictions on the test set. The remaining top five models, Models 44, 39, and 23, were all trained using the top-k prediction method with fixed windows of five, four, and three days, respectively. The small difference in accuracy between these models and Model 3 suggests that the LSTM was capable of generating strong predictions based solely on single-day sequences. This finding indicates that incorporating information from past days did not significantly enhance predictive performance. Therefore, Model 3 was chosen for further analysis to examine the patterns learned by the LSTM that contributed to its high accuracy.

## Transition analysis
Samples that were correctly predicted by Model 3 of the LSTM models (correct prediction means that all 0s and 1s in the vector were correctly predicted) were used to generate a transition probability matrix, shown in Fig. 3(a). The probabilities revealed two dominant patterns: (1) the predicted state often matched the last vector in the sequence, and (2) the prediction frequently corresponded to all zeros (no unit or males). To determine whether these patterns were responsible for the model’s successful predictions, two simple baseline models were created without any training. The first model always predicted the last vector in the sequence as the output, while the second model predicted a vector of all zeros (no unit or males). The results, presented in Table 2, showed that both baseline models achieved approximately half the test accuracy of the original LSTM model. This suggests that while these simple patterns played a role in the LSTM’s predictions, the model was also able to learn more complex patterns that contributed to its higher accuracy.

## Social comparison
Transition probabilities were also calculated for all sequences in the dataset (in addition to the output of the LSTMs) based on consecutive dates, generating a comprehensive transition matrix, represented as a network in Fig. 4. This matrix was then compared to a half-weight association matrix using a Hemelrijk Rr-test, a variant of the Mantel test designed to assess rank-based correlations within rows. The results indicate a strong and significant correlation between the matrices (matrix correlation coefficient = 0.633, ). Within-row rankings assess whether the relative likelihood of one unit transitioning to another follows the same order as their strengths of social association. This means that if a unit frequently transitions to certain other units, those same units are also the ones with which it is most often associated. This has shown to be the case for some of the more frequently sighted units, as can be seen in the network presented in Fig. 4.

## IPI distribution
The final recording dataset used for the IPI analysis (after processing the recordings and removing clipped or low-quality data) is detailed in Table 3.
Recording database used for the analysis of IPI distribution.
Data from two time periods, 2015–2016 and 2022–2023, were available for analysis. Given the slow growth rate of sperm whales79, IPI values were assumed to remain relatively stable within each one-year period. To exclude false measurements that are potentially caused by echoes, only IPI values between 1.5 and 4.5 were considered, corresponding to individuals that measure 7-12 meters in length50,80,81, the typical range of whales that inhabit the waters off Dominica. First-click recordings were used to establish a reference IPI distribution for four units, where recordings were obtained for all unit members based on photo ID (Fig. 5). The majority of these recordings were collected in 2022–2023 to minimize changes in IPI due to growth over time. Only individuals that were mature adults in 2015–2016 had additional first-click recordings from this earlier period. Systematic recordings were used to generate daily IPI measurement sets for days when only a single identified unit was present. These sets were analyzed using methods described in the Method section and in the supplementary data.

## KDE analysis
The overall likelihood of each daily IPI distribution originating from each fitted KDE was then computed and updated using Bayes’ theorem, incorporating the following prior probabilities: Unit A - 0.343, Unit FU - 0.268, Unit J - 0.209, Unit R - 0.179. To prevent random assignment, IPI values with zero probability of originating from any of the four ground-truth distributions were removed. Similarly, if the overall likelihood of a daily IPI distribution was zero, it was classified as not belonging to any of the four units. The best-fitting parameters were obtained using lognormal kernel density estimation with a bandwidth of 0.01, achieving a 78.26% success rate.

## Discussion
This study demonstrates that sperm whale unit movements are not random but follow structured, socially influenced patterns. Our findings reveal that for at least part of the sperm whale community, the presence of one unit is often predictive of the next, indicating that social associations play a crucial role in shaping spatial distribution. By integrating LSTM-based sequence modeling, transition probability analysis, and IPI distribution monitoring, we provide a robust framework for studying these complex social dynamics at multiple scales.
The LSTM model (Table 1) achieved high predictive accuracy based on sequences as short as a single day, suggesting that sperm whale unit presence is largely determined by recent unit presence rather than long-term historical patterns. The best-performing model, trained on one-day sequences, successfully predicted subsequent unit appearances with an accuracy exceeding 60%, far surpassing random expectations (). This result indicates that unit movements are not independent; instead, they are dynamically influenced by the recent presence of other units, some more clearly than others. Interestingly, models trained on longer sequences did not significantly improve accuracy, reinforcing the idea that movement decisions are primarily driven by short-term social and environmental cues rather than extended historical trends. Transition probability analysis further clarified distinct residency patterns among units (Fig. 3): 1. The most frequently sighted units, A and F&U, tended to appear in alternating succession, often arriving shortly after another unit’s , as can be seen by the green edges between these units in the transition network 15-1 (Fig. 4). 2. When less common units departed, the next presence state was often “no unit”, suggesting a delay in new arrivals. 3. Once a unit was present, it typically remained in the area for several consecutive days before leaving.
These structured transitions highlight that sperm whale residency patterns are shaped not only by the decisions of individual units but also by their interactions with others. Furthermore, the strong correlation (, ) between the transition probability matrix (represented as a network in Fig. 4) and the social association matrix suggests that movement patterns are deeply intertwined with social relationships. Units that frequently follow each other in spatial presence are also those with the strongest social associations, pointing to three possible mechanisms: 1. Units may actively coordinate their large-scale movements with socially affiliated units, potentially through communication or shared travel strategies. 2. Units with similar movement patterns are more likely to encounter each other repeatedly, reinforcing social bonds over time. 3. Shared cultural foraging strategies or habitat preferences may lead to similar movement trajectories, driving both associations and transition probabilities. Disentangling these mechanisms remains a challenge due to the vast mobility of sperm whales, but the observed predictability in presence patterns suggests a significant role for social coordination.
The factors influencing their movement are likely a combination of all these aspects. Moreover, the predictive success of the LSTM is probably based on more complex patterns that are only partly captured by the transition probabilities such as higher-order dependencies that may involve subtle multi-unit interactions, time-lag effects, or context-specific patterns, which are more difficult to represent with a single transition matrix. Future work should aim to refine these predictive models by incorporating additional ecological variables, such as seasonality, prey availability, and habitat usage, to explore how sociality and the environment intersect in shaping the movements of mobile species and to understand the influence of cultural transmission on their movement behavior. To further investigate the role of social factors in movement design, data collection and analysis should focus on the communication patterns produced during changes in movement. This includes determining whether another social unit is within hearing range of the whales and, if so, how this presence influences their communication and movement choices.
The LSTM is a predictive model that learns patterns from a training dataset to generate accurate forecasts. In this study, we applied it to photo-identification data of individual whales, which has already yielded promising results with over 60% accuracy. This dataset reflects periods when the research team is actively on the water, providing identified observations of unit presence. While the LSTM captures valuable snapshots of unit appearances, its episodic nature highlights the opportunity to complement it with continuous data sources. To address this, we also explored the use of passive acoustic monitoring (PAM) as a complementary tool. Unlike photo-ID, PAM enables continuous, year-round data collection via stationary hydrophones, allowing for the detection of social units even in the absence of the research team. By analyzing inter-pulse interval (IPI) distributions, we achieved nearly 80% accuracy in correctly identifying the unit present using a simple drop-down hydrophone. Theoretically, this drop-down hydrophone can be replaced by a moored hydrophone, which collects the same data continuously throughout the year, allowing identification of the units that were present using the described method. This method provides a powerful and noninvasive approach for long-term monitoring, although it requires prior knowledge of unit-specific IPI measurements (as in Fig. 5) and will work better when social units are alone and not in a group formation. In well-studied populations, this technique offers a valuable means of filling temporal data gaps, enabling year-round tracking of social dynamics and residency patterns.
The findings of this study have important implications for understanding the social dynamics of highly mobile species. By integrating sequential modeling with acoustic and photo-ID data, we present a novel framework for investigating how both the physical and social environments influence large-scale movement patterns. These insights emphasize the critical role of social structures in shaping marine mammal distributions and highlight the need for further research at the intersection of sociality and spatial ecology in mobile species.
