Male humpback whales switch to singing in the presence of seismic air guns.
Dunlop R, Noad M. (2024). Communications biology. . doi:10.1038/s42003-024-06908-w. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Male humpback whales switch to singing in the presence of seismic air guns

## Abstract
AbstractOcean noise produced by seismic exploration has been implicated in causing changes in baleen whale hearing, physiology, feeding, breeding, and migratory behaviours. Here, we observed changes in the mating tactics of humpback whales (Megaptera novaeangliae) during a one-hour exposure to nearby seismic air guns. Males employ a conditional mating strategy where they switch between singing and non-singing tactics. Singing is presumably an advertisement signal, while non-singing behaviours include seeking out and joining with females as well as forming competitive groups. During periods of active air guns, the number of male singing whales increased, and singers were more likely to be observed joining females. Conversely, non-singing males were less likely to engage in joining interactions suggesting that active air guns caused a switch in male breeding tactics. Though we cannot translate these effects into changes in breeding success, this indicates that seismic exploration has the potential to alter breeding behaviours in baleen whales.Subject terms: Behavioural ecology, Conservation biology, Ecology, Animal behaviour

## Introduction
Marine mammal populations are subject to multiple threats including ocean warming due to climate change, entanglements from the fishing industry, and ocean pollution. While mortality rates can serve as a crucial metric for tracking these threats, non-lethal effects from sources such as anthropogenic noise can significantly impact an individual’s physiology, feeding, breeding and migratory behaviours, leading to potential population-level consequences1–6.
Seismic air guns, integral to global offshore oil and gas exploration, emit intense percussive sounds or ‘bangs’. These high-level signals commonly have source levels >230 dB re 1 µPa@1 m SPLrms and have been detected up to 4000 km from the source7,8. In terms of impacts of air guns on marine mammals, if they happen to be close to the source, they may experience hearing impairment9. Generally, the dominant frequencies of seismic air guns overlap with those of the communication signals of low-frequency specialists such as baleen whales (10 Hz–1 kHz). Given the large propagation distances of air gun noise, baleen whales face significant large-scale impacts. For example, at distances of kilometres up to tens of kilometres from the source, marine mammals have been shown to change their migratory behaviour10–13 and feeding behaviour14,15. These changes have been linked to direct energetic consequences, which have the potential to cause changes in their health and, if severe enough, may translate to population-level consequences6,16,17.
In contrast to migration and feeding, the link between seismic survey activity, changes in marine mammal breeding behaviour, and potential population-level consequences are less clear. To date, there are few studies that have found any direct link between air gun activity and changes in breeding behaviour in baleen whales. Dunlop et al.18 found that humpback whales, during their migration from breeding to feeding grounds, reduced the number of joining interactions during air gun activity. Given these joining interactions were primarily breeding interactions where males are joining with females19–21, it seems the air guns potentially caused a reduction in breeding behaviours. Some species of baleen whales sing, and this is thought to be a male breeding display22–25. If singing in baleen whales serves as a breeding signal, then changes in singing behaviour could also indicate changes in their breeding behaviour, which is the focus of this study. In opportunistic studies with distant seismic surveys, Clark and Gagnon26 reported that fin whales (Balaenoptera physalus) ceased singing, while Cerchio et al.27 found that the number of singing humpback whales decreased. This suggests seismic air gun surveys can cause large whales to cease displaying. Blue whales (Balaenoptera musculus), however, increased their calling when exposed to ‘sparkers’, another high-level impulsive source used for seismic exploration28. Bowhead whales (Balaena mysticetus) increased their singing at low received levels of air gun noise but reduced their singing at higher levels29. In other words, the vocal response of baleen whales to seismic air guns is not clear cut and there is likely to be the interplay of other factors such as proximity, received level, and duration of the signal, as well as the context of the signalling whale.
Flexible mating strategies can play a role in population resilience to a changing environment30,31. Many populations of humpback whale, for example, have experienced a rapid recovery in numbers following the moratorium on whaling32–35. This ability to recover their numbers from complete decimation may be partly due to their flexible mating strategy36. Male humpback whales employ a conditional mating strategy, which involves a balance between non-singing and singing breeding behaviours36–39. The non-singing strategy involves intra-sexual competition. Within humpback whale breeding aggregations there is likely to be a strong operational sex ratio skewed towards males40–42. This results in high levels of conflict and physical competition when competing for access to females37,38,43, and sometimes death44. Non-singing males will seek out, temporarily join, and then escort females, including mothers with suckling calves21,45,46 More males may then move in and join this group to form a ‘competitive group’ around a single female. This larger group is comprised of a ‘principal’ male escort with several ‘secondary’ male escorts jostling aggressively for the closest position to the female37,38,43,47. From here on, this tactic will be referred to as a ‘seeker’ tactic, where males seek out and attempt to join presumably receptive females that are alone or already being escorted by another male.
Male humpback whales also sing. Whether singing functions to attract females and/or is a form of passive male–male competition has been debated in the literature48. Singing whales usually start singing when they are alone but may soon be joined by another animal, usually a male21,49,50. This usually leads to a cessation of singing, implying that song is involved in mediating competitive interactions21,50. Lone singers, however, also join females whilst continuing to sing as ‘singing escorts’19,21,51. This suggests a role in female attraction. Like non-singing males and lone singers, singers escorting females can attract other males21,51. Here, the risk to the singing escort would be displacement from its position closest to the female21,51 and/or injury from fighting if more males move in to form a competitive group37,38,43,47. The cost of being joined is therefore higher for a singing escort than for a lone singer21. The benefit to the non-singing joining males is likely the chance that they will outcompete and displace the original escorting male and therefore gain access to the female.
Compared to many species of large marine mammals, the breeding strategy of humpback whales has been well studied. The prevalence of singing, and the payoff for each tactic (singing and seeking), have been shown to rely on the density of other males in the local area36. Although the number of singers in an area tends to increase with the number of males, in low male densities, the proportion of males that sing is higher, with singers more likely to join with females (i.e., a higher payoff). In higher male densities, males are more likely to switch to the seeker tactic and undertake competitive behaviours36, with this tactic having a higher payoff. Males can change tactics, from singer to seeker, depending on other animals in their vicinity, meaning there is likely to be a trade-off between the advantages of singing versus the advantages of seeking.
This breeding system provides an opportunity to assess the impact of seismic air gun activity on male breeding behaviour in a large whale species. To determine if air guns significantly changed male humpback whale mating tactics, observations of humpback whales undergoing breeding interactions were undertaken during a series of seismic air gun experiments. Two hypotheses were tested. The first is that males are less likely to sing in the presence of air guns27, and the second is that males engage in fewer joining interactions during periods of active air gun activity18. Two metrics are used to test the hypotheses: (1) the number of males that sing versus seek in the area and (2) the joining payoff for each tactic.

## Results
Data collection occurred during the return migration of humpback whales, from breeding grounds in the Great Barrier Reef, to feeding grounds in the Southern Ocean in 2010, 2011, 2014, and 2015. The field site was off the eastern coastline of Australia, and whales were carrying out typical breeding behaviours such as males joining and escorting females, singing, and forming competitive groups (Fig. 1).

Groups took ~3 h to migrate through the study area. The social environment was therefore highly correlated between successive hours, but by 3 h, was uncorrelated. For this reason, 3-h time frames were chosen for the analysis (8–11 a.m., 11 a.m. to 2 pm, 2–5 p.m.) and assumed to be independent samples. For each 3-h period, the number of likely males, females, and the number of sighted non-singer and singers were totalled as outlined below.
Non-calf ‘adult’ whales were presumptively designated as male or female based on group characteristics, presence of a calf and previous biopsy studies (see ‘Methods’ section). To summarise: whales that were singing and whales that joined with other adults were presumed to be male; lone adults with a calf were allocated female; lone non-singing whales and those in pairs of adults (without a calf) were given a 70% likelihood of being male and 30% female; groups with three adults were presumed to have one female and two males. Lone male humpback whales were designated either as a ‘singer’ or a ‘seeker’. Singer and seeker males can travel alone and are often sighted joining a female (usually with a calf in this study area) to become her escort or can join groups that already contain at least one male and a female. These groups can turn into competitive groups, where multiple males are physically competing for the position of principal escort of the female. Once all adults were allocated a likely sex based on their behaviour (and using the results of a previous biopsy study at this site36), the total number of singing males, (presumed) seeker males, and (presumed) females were counted for each 3-h period. For the full dataset, the number of seeker males per 3-h ranged between 0 and 35, the number of singers ranged between 0 and 3, and the number of females ranged between 1 and 24. A high number of presumed females correlated with a high number of female-calf groups in the area (usually towards the tail end of the migration when there were predominantly female-calf groups). A high number of presumed males correlated with a high number of female-calf-escort and multiple adult groups, which consisted of one female and multiple males. It was not possible to age the whales.
Three-hour time periods were then allocated ‘baseline’ or ‘air gun’. Baseline periods were entirely free of air guns. Baseline periods that occurred after air gun activity on the same day were not used. Air gun periods were allocated based on the time of the first air gun shot. Each of the air gun exposures lasted a maximum of 1 h. Each day usually consisted of two experiments, with the first in the morning, and the second in the afternoon. For most samples, the hour-long exposure fell entirely within a 3-h period. In the few occasions, the air guns started in one 3-h period, and continued into the next, the period which contained most of the exposure was used. Air guns were either towed eastwards across the migration stream, or northwards against the migration stream (Fig. 1).

## Conditional tactical choice in the absence and presence of air guns
A negative binomial GLM was used to test for a relationship between the number of singers and their social environment at the time of singing. It was assumed the number of likely males (1) and the number of likely females (2) determined the likelihood a male would choose to sing, as measured by the number of singers per 3 h. First, a baseline model was constructed using periods when there were no active air guns, or source vessel, in the area. Baseline data included times before an air gun experiment was carried out on a given day but excluded periods after the air gun activity had ceased for that day.
Given the increased chance of missing and/or confusing groups in high whale densities, the maximum number of males and females per 3 h included in the analysis was 20 males, which equated to less than (approximately) 10 groups being tracked at any one time and a maximum number of 41 whales spread over the 3-h period. In baseline conditions (n = 67 time periods), the number of (likely) males per 3 h ranged from 1 to 20 (average of 8 and median of 7) and the number of (likely) females per 3 h ranged from 1 to 21 (average 7 and median of 6). Singing is a male behaviour, therefore, as the number of likely males in the area increased, the number of singing males also significantly increased (GLM; N = 67; Z = 3.925, P < 0.0001). High male numbers occurred when there were groups containing multiple adults with and without a calf (i.e., one female and multiple likely males), and lone animals (presumed males) joining other groups in the area. However, there were significantly fewer singers as the total number of likely females increased (GLM; N = 67, Z = −2.095; P = 0.036; Fig. 2b). High female numbers occurred when there was a high number of female-calf pairs and female-calf- (non-singing) escort groups in the area. These results align with predictions of singing activity according to their social environment found in Dunlop and Frere36.

Next, using the entire dataset, the term ‘air gun presence’ was included (i.e., baseline or air guns) to compare male behaviour during baseline and air gun periods. Here, a generalised additive model (GAM) was used assuming a negative binomial distribution. Given the significant trends between numbers of singers, males, and females, observed in the baseline data, these relationships were included as smooth terms, with air gun activity (baseline or air guns) included as an additional covariate. It was hypothesised that significantly fewer males would choose to sing in the presence of air guns. During active air gun periods (n = 52 time periods), male and female numbers were similar to baseline conditions, with an average of 9 males (median of 9, maximum of 20) and 8 females (median of 7, maximum of 21). Overall, there was a significantly higher number of singing males (GAM; N = 119; Z = 3.136; P = 0.0017) during air gun activity compared to baseline conditions. However, unlike baseline conditions, there was no significant relationship between the number of singers, and the total number of males (GAM; N = 119; χ2 = 2.903, P = 0.2380) or females (GAM; N = 119; χ2 = 0.293, P = 0.5880) in the area during air gun periods (Fig. 2a, b). This is because, during air guns, there was almost always one to two singers whereas in baseline conditions, there was usually zero or one singing whale when male numbers were low, increasing to one to two singers when male numbers were higher.

## Tactical payoffs in the absence and presence of air guns
The payoff for each male tactic was a combination of the benefit of joining with a female and the cost incurred by more males joining the group leading to increased within-group competition. The payoff does not assume a joining male achieved a successful mating, but would have had more chance of mating after joining with a female compared to if alone.
Total seeker and singer payoffs were estimated for each 3-h time period, based on the number of times they were sighted joining a presumed female (benefit) and the number of additional males that then joined each group (cost). Baseline payoff models were then created for seekers (1) and singers (2) accounting for the presence of males and females using a GLM and assuming a negative binomial distribution. Note, the seeker payoff model only included times in which there was at least one presumed non-singing male in the area (N = 67) and the singer payoff model only included times in which there was at least one singer in the area (N = 35). As above, the covariate ‘air gun presence’ was then added to the full dataset to test if the payoff for seeker and singer males was significantly reduced in the presence of air guns (GAM a negative binomial distribution; N = 118 and N = 78 for seeker and singer models, respectively). With the observed reduction in joining interactions in the presence of air guns18, it was hypothesised that males (regardless of tactic) would be less likely to be observed joining with a presumed female during periods of active air gun activity.
Under baseline conditions, the total payoff for seeker males significantly increased with the number of males (GLM, N = 67; Z = 7.696; P = 0.0265; Fig. 3a) but not females (GLM, N = 67; Z = 0.794; P = 0.3729) in the area. This is because seekers engaged in more joining interactions, given there were more males available to join females. The number of additional joins to these groups also increased, as competitive groups tended to form in higher whale densities. Collectively, seekers were sighted joining with another adult or (most often) a female with a calf, up to four times during the 3-h period. Additional joins to these groups to form larger competitive groups were also observed up to three times in a 3-h period, more so in high male densities (Table 1). By contrast, even though the number of singers increased with the number of whales in baseline conditions (Fig. 2a, b), the total payoff of singing males did not significantly increase with an increase in the number of males (GLM, N = 35; Z = −0.710, P = 0.4774; Fig. 3b) or females (GLM, N = 35; Z = 0.659, p = 0.5097) in the area. Overall, in baseline conditions, singers were observed joining less frequently compared to seekers, at a maximum of two joins per 3-h period (Table 1), regardless of the number of males in the area.

Given the lack of relationship between joining behaviour and female numbers, only male numbers were included in the following analyses. In the presence of air guns, changes in male joining behaviour occurred. Although the total numbers of observed joins and additional joins were similar between baseline and air gun periods (Table 1), the joining behaviour of seekers and singers changed. During air gun periods, there were fewer seeker joins observed, meaning the total payoff for seeker males was less during periods of active air guns compared to baseline conditions (Table 1). There were approximately half the number of observed seeker joining and additional joining interactions for the same number of males in the area. Despite this, the overall payoff for seekers (GAM, N = 118, Z = 1.131, P = 0.258) did not significantly change between baseline and air gun periods because of the high variation in seeker payoff with male density. When accounting for the effect of male density, seeker payoff significantly increased with an increase in the number of males within baseline conditions (GAM, N = 118; χ2 = 21.286, P < 0.0001; Fig. 3a) but not when air guns were active (GAM, N = 118; χ2 = 2.188, P = 0.139; Fig. 3a). Therefore, in high male densities, when seeker activity should be greater, seeker interactions were less frequent per male when air guns were active.
The opposite occurred for singers. Overall, singer payoff did not significantly change when air guns were active (GAM, N = 78, Z = −0.468, P = 0.6394). However, when accounting for the numbers of males in the area, when air guns were active total singer payoff significantly increased with increasing male numbers in the area (GAM, N = 78; χ2 = 7.468, P = 0.006; Fig. 3b) whereas in baseline conditions it did not (GAM, N = 78; χ2 = 0.116, P = 0.7338; Fig. 3b). This was due to an increase in the number of observed joining interactions involving a singer in higher male numbers, combined with a decrease in the number of observed additional joins (by a seeker) to a singer group (Table 1). Specifically, in the presence of air guns, when male numbers were high, singers were sighted joining females more often without the cost of being additionally joined by seeker males.
Since the total amount of joining activity was similar between baseline and air gun periods, and there was a decline in the number of seeker interactions (joins and additional joins) within air gun periods (Table 1), it seemed that males primed to undertake breeding behaviours were more likely to do so whilst singing without the cost of attracting additional males to the group. This was tested at an individual level (i.e., dividing the singer payoff by the number of singers). There was no difference between the payoff per singer within baseline and air gun periods (GAM, N = 78, Z = 0.281, P = 0.779), with an average payoff of 0.26 and 0.25 per singer for baseline and air gun periods, respectively. However, when adding in the effect of male density (Fig. 4), individual singer payoff was higher in higher male densities during air gun activity (GAM, N = 78, χ2 = 4.352, P = 0.037) and not during baseline periods (GAM, N = 78, χ2 = 0.160, P = 0.6888). This was because, in baseline conditions, when there were more singers and more males in the area, singers did not join more often. When air guns were active, a higher number of singers led to more joining activity involving singers, with individual singers more likely to be engaged in joining interactions.

To summarise, in the presence of air guns, a higher number of males sang. Overall, there was no evidence that male breeding behaviour (joining interactions) declined in the presence of air guns. Rather, singers were more likely to be observed joining another animal or female-calf pair, more so in higher male densities. Given seekers were less likely to join and additionally join groups, joining singers did not attract an additional cost, meaning the total payoff, and individual payoff, for this behaviour increased.

## Discussion
Behavioural flexibility in mating strategies can enhance a species’ resilience against environmental challenges such as anthropogenic impacts30,31. During periods of rapid environmental change, this adaptability becomes crucial in that it can aid the population’s response to threats thereby potentially lowering its extinction risk52,53. For example, following the moratorium on whaling, humpback whales rapidly recovered, despite severely diminished numbers33,34. Given whaling likely removed mature animals, their population dynamics post-whaling would have been quite different to pre-whaling. Despite this, their breeding strategy, which likely favoured singing36, was obviously successful in that the population rapidly increased. Non-lethal disturbances, like ocean noise from seismic activity, does not cause mortality like whaling, but may affect breeding behaviour and vital rates. In response to seismic air gun activity, this study found that male humpback whales altered their mating strategy but did not cease to carry out breeding behaviours. Whilst air guns were active, a higher number of male humpback whales sang, particularly when whale density was low, and the social environment was dominated by lone animals and female-calf pairs. This tended to occur towards the end of the migratory season, which is when most females with calves migrate. Not only did more whales choose singing as a tactic, but in higher male densities, these singers were sighted joining with females, which contributed to an increase in the total payoff for this tactic. Previous studies found that, in higher male densities, singing escorts were more likely to attract additional males to the group21,51. This study found no evidence of an increase in risk to singing escorts during periods of active air guns (i.e., additional joins to singing escort groups were still rare), likely due to the decrease in seeker (non-singer) joining interactions. Therefore, both total singer payoff and average singer success (per singer) increased, meaning, collectively the tactic was more common, but individually, singers were not accruing any extra cost. Interestingly, these observations fit with a previous study at this site, where non-singing groups that were focal followed were less likely be involved in a joining interaction when air guns were active18. However, with the addition of singing behaviour in this study, it seems that overall breeding activity was not reduced. Again, like their response to whaling, it seems their flexible mating strategy helps to mitigate potential negative impacts of anthropogenic activity.
Before assuming the air guns caused males to choose singing over seeking, other potential effects should be discounted. This first is that air gun activity might displace whales, causing them to migrate further offshore. This could lead to reduced inshore male density and, as males are more likely to choose to sing in low density conditions36, more singers during active air guns periods. Prior studies found that singing activity in humpback27 and fin whales54 was reduced in the presence of air guns. Castellote et al.54 attributed this to the displacement of whales. However, the current study found that the numbers of males and females within baseline and active air gun periods were approximately the same meaning this reasoning is not supported. The second is that the air guns caused a change in singing behaviour in such a way as to increase the apparent number of singers. For example, if a singing whale temporarily ceased to sing then started again, one singer would be counted as two, or singers may have responded to the air gun sounds by increasing their source level so that more were detected more readily or further away. Again, there is no evidence for this. Singers were continuously acoustically tracked as they moved through the area with no evidence that a singer stopped then started again. Also only singers that were within 10 km of the array were counted, meaning they were all clearly audible and, even if they did increase their source level, they were no more detectable.
Instead, it appeared that the air guns triggered or stimulated an innate response in males, prompting them to choose the singing tactic more often. With more males choosing to sing, there was a higher potential for a singer to be observed joining another group. This is supported by fewer seeker males being observed joining with females, and fewer sightings of males additionally joining other groups to form competitive groups as also observed in Dunlop et al.18. The preference to sing in the presence of air guns seems odd given that seismic air guns create noise, and the singer tactic presumably relies on receivers hearing and interpreting an acoustic signal. One theory that has been presented in previous studies is that air gun ‘bangs’ sound like a much closer breaching whale55. Breaching has been associated with joining in several studies49,56,57 and is likely to be an acoustic signal that mediates social interactions20,58. In addition, studies on male breeding behaviour at this study site found that, for a male to sing, there must be at least one other whale within 2 km21, suggesting singing is a conditional response to there being other whales in the area. If air guns are triggering an innate response to ‘breaching’, then perhaps this explains the increase in singing activity. The stimulatory effect of impulsive seismic noise is supported by the findings of Di Iorio and Clark28, who found that blue whale call rates increased during seismic exploration in the Gulf of St Lawrence. There were, however, important differences between the studies in that they examined social calls, not song, and the source was a ‘sparker’ rather than air guns.
In this study, air guns were active for only 1 h. Other studies, such as Castellote et al.54 and Cerchio et al.27, measured the response of whales to a continuous seimsic survey, which can last for many days, if not weeks. Therefore, the study’s short exposure duration might not fully represent the effects of seismic surveys. This may explain the major difference in findings between the studies, in that if animals at this study site were exposed to a full seismic survey, perhaps singers would eventually cease to sing. Another difference between the current study and those of Castellote et al.54 and Cerchio et al.27 was that in both previous studies the range to the air guns was not known, but was certainly much further than in the current study. The lower received level of the sounds, and greater range, of the distant seismic surveys may have reduced any stimulatory effect on the whales. Bowhead whales, for example, were found to increase their vocal rates at low air gun received levels, but decrease their vocal rates as air gun received levels increased29 suggesting an effect of received level and/or range on their vocal response. In summary, baleen whale vocal responses to air gun signals are complex and further work is required to understand fully how these whales are responding vocally.
Regardless of the reasons behind it, the study has shown a shift in male humpback whale mating tactics in the presence of air guns, and at least for this exposure duration, there is no evidence that seismic air guns halt breeding behaviours in humpback whales. The impact of this shift, as yet, remains unclear, especially regarding female choice and mating interactions. Given we do not fully understand the role of female choice in these interactions, it is difficult to say if this is a positive effect. It is not known if females are still choosing to breed with singing males in the presence of air guns and what impact, if any, these signals have on mating success. For example, traffic noise exposure has been found to cause a decline in reproduction in birds as it disrupted the female’s ability to assess males by masking their sexual signals59. It is not known if this is the case here and care must be taken in interpreting these results. What may seem to be a positive response to anthropogenic noise in terms of behavioural flexibility, may not be, in that their normal mating system has shifted in the presence of air guns. Therefore it is important to understand the link between mating behaviour and population dynamics before making any assumptions on the potential for population-level impacts.

## Methods

## Study area and general observations
Data collection occurred over four field seasons (September and October 2010, 2011, 2014, and 2015) during the annual southern migration of humpback whales from breeding grounds in the Great Barrier Reef to feeding grounds in the Southern Ocean. The study site was located off the coast of Peregian Beach (north of Brisbane, in Queensland, Australia). Here, humpback whales were still exhibiting breeding behaviours such as singing, males joining females as escorts, and males forming competitive groups around a central female. The study received ethical approval from the University of Queensland’s Animal Ethics Committee, and we have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.
Lone whales and migrating groups (whales within ~100 m of each other that were diving and surfacing together) were tracked visually (7 a.m. to 5 p.m., weather permitting) from a land-based elevated survey point, Emu Mountain (73 m elevation). A theodolite (Leica TM 1100) was used in conjunction with a notebook computer running VADAR software (E. Kniest, Univ. Newcastle, Australia) to track the whales and groups in real-time as well as note group composition and behaviours. From here on, these will be referred to as ‘groups’, noting a group could contain one whale. Groups were observable to ~10 km, so the field of view was ~20 km in a north/south direction and 10 km offshore (Fig. 1). However, most groups migrated within 6 km of the coastline. Teams consisted of a theodolite operator, a computer operator, and three observers with binoculars. A ‘scan sampling’ approach was taken, where the team would continually scan from north to south of the study site and call every group surfacing observed. The three observers were assigned sectors (north, east, and south) and observed their assigned groups until the groups left their sector. When whale groups were sighted, observers called the group identification letter, group behaviour, group size and composition, whether a calf was present, and general direction of travel whilst directing the theodolite operator to the position of the group. The position of the group (using binocular bearing and reticule), as well as the group’s details, were entered by the computer operator. Given groups were tracking southwards, and tended to move in a consistent direction, the computer operator could assign a group identification letter to the sighting if observers were unsure which group had been sighted. The theodolite operator then attempted to get at least one fix per group during their surfacing, and if missed for that surfacing, would focus on fixing that group during the next surfacing. This meant all migrating groups were tracked as they moved southwards along the coastline but not all surfacings from these groups were captured. During post-processing, given groups were migrating southwards and usually along a consistent distance offshore and at a consistent speed, any group tracks that were not clear during data collection could be reconstructed. Joining and splitting groups were also noted by a change in the group’s identification letter. A join was defined as one or more animals actively moving towards a group to surface within 100 m of them and then matching the group surfacing times. Joining groups were usually obvious as groups that were not joining together were usually spaced at least 2 km apart. Splitting was where a group would simply split into two groups that generally moved apart.
Joining behaviours were the particular focus of this study. For mating to occur, a male must first join a female. Therefore, the success of the mating tactic of any given male (singing or seeking) was measured by whether the male managed to join with a female, noting that it is not itself a direct measure of mating success. If, after an initial join, more animals subsequently moved in and joined the group, this was termed an additional join to that group. These additionally joined groups usually comprised multiple adults with or without a calf, with additional joiners almost certainly male36. On rare occasions a singing lone whale remained in one place but was joined by another individual. Given the likelihood this was two males joining together19, these interactions were not counted.
Acoustic recordings were made from three to five hydrophone buoys moored in 18–28 m of water and arranged in a line or T-shaped array (Fig. 1). Each hydrophone buoy consisted of a surface buoy containing a custom-built pre-amplifier (+20 dB gain) and 41B sonobuoy VHF radio transmitter. A High Tech HTI-96-MIN hydrophone with built-in +40 dB pre-amplifier was suspended ~1 m above each buoy’s mooring and cabled into the buoy’s pre-amplifier and transmitter. The buoys’ VHF signals were received onshore at a base station 1.5–2.5 km away using a directional Yagi antenna connected to a type 8101, four-channel sonobuoy receiver. The acoustic tracks were digitised and recorded to a computer using Ishmael software60 as multi-track wave files with a sampling rate of 22 kHz (per channel). Singing whales were located by cross-correlating the same song sound arriving at the different hydrophones to determine time-of-arrival differences in Ishmael. These differences, together with an accurate knowledge of the positions of the hydrophones, were then used to determine the most likely location of the singer. Singers generally move slowly and calculating an acoustic position approximately every 10 min produced a detailed track of the singer. Weather was noted hourly.
In 2010, experiments were carried out using a 19 m West Coaster vessel, FV Ash Dar S, towing a single 20 in3 Bolt 600B air gun at 5 m depth12 eastwards across, or northwards against, the migration stream. In 2011, a 28 m, 210 tonne ship, RV Whale Song, was used to tow an array of six air guns in a 2.1 ×1.3 m cluster array at 6 m depth. The array consisted of the same 20 in3 single air gun as well as three 40 in3 and two 150 in3 GI air guns. Experiments were conducted using either (i) the single 20 in3 air gun as in 2010, (ii) four of the air guns (total volume 140 in3) for a ‘hard start’ exposure without a ramp-up, or (iii) a four-stage ramp-up using the full 440 in3 array61 towed eastwards. In 2014, a 65 m, 2031 tonne, dedicated commercial seismic source vessel RV Duke, with a 3130 in3 air gun array, was used with a ramp-up13,18 and also moved northwards. In all years, the air guns fired every 11 s during active trials for a maximum of 1 h. During control trials (not used in the current analysis), the ship towed the air guns on the same transect path, but the guns were not firing. Vessel speed during the transect was 7–8 km/h.
Unlike the original behavioural response studies at this site (e.g., 12,13,18), the current study was not designed to test for individual-level dose–responses, but rather testing for a general change in breeding interactions throughout the entire study area. It was assumed the hour-long air gun signals would cause a general change in male breeding behaviour as measured by the relationship between male tactics, joining interactions, and the social environment. It was also assumed array size and tow direction would have no significant effect18. Analysed baseline and air gun time periods were spread throughout the day and throughout each field season. Therefore, neither daytime nor Julian day (season) were considered to have a significant influence on the results.

## Statistics and reproducibility

## The effect of active air guns on male tactical choice
Daily observations were divided into three separate periods: 8 a.m. to 11 a.m., 11 a.m. to 2 p.m., and 2 p.m. to 5 p.m. For each 3-h period (N = 147), the number of singing males and the number of groups by composition (singing animal, lone animal, adult and calf pair, adult-calf and number of escorts, adult-only group with the number of adults) were counted. It was not logistically possible to biopsy and sex all migrating animals, therefore, to estimate the number of males within the study area several assumptions were made. These assumptions were based on a previous biopsy study carried out in the area36, the results of previous studies on sex ratio on the eastern Australian population40, as well as the assumptions of sex based on the behaviour of the group23,38,42,43,45. An adult with a calf was assumed to be female, so adult-calf pairs were counted as one adult female per group. It was assumed that adults escorting female-calf pairs were males and counted as such. Groups of multiple adults (three) were assumed to be comprised of a single female, principal male escort and a secondary male escort or challenger, i.e., one adult female with the rest counted as males. Although they may have also been comprised of three males40, the small number of occurrences meant it made little difference to the overall count. Lone animals that sang were known males and lone animals that joined a group were counted as male. This left lone animals not involved in any group interactions and adult pairs. Based on the results of the biopsy study36, lone animals that did not join and were not singing were given a 70% chance of being male since there was a male bias in biopsied lone animals. It is unlikely as high as 90% of lone adults were male given this would mean very few lone females. It is also unlikely as low as 50% of the lone adults were male given the prevalence of breeding behaviours such as adults joining other groups and singing whales. Given the male bias in adult pairs found in biopsy studies36,40, the percentage of males within adult pairs was also set at 70%. Because unsexed adults and adult pairs accounted for relatively low numbers compared to sexed animals, varying these proportions within a range of likelihoods made very little difference to the overall count of males and females. This gave, for every 3-h period, the number of singing males, the number of likely non-singing (seeker) males, and the number of likely females. From these data the female proportion was also estimated. It was not possible to age whales.
Each 3-h period was then allocated ‘baseline’ (N = 83) or ‘air gun’ (N = 64). During baseline periods, the source vessel was not in the area. Baseline periods were spread throughout the day (i.e., morning, late morning, and early afternoon) and across the season (i.e., throughout September and October). If air gun experiments were taking place that day, subsequent periods after the air guns had ceased firing were not used as baseline data as whales had likely been pre-exposed to the air guns. If, at any time during the 3-h period, the air guns were active, this was allocated ‘air gun’. Air gun activity (maximum of 1 h) usually occurred entirely within one of the 3-h periods given air gun experiments were either carried out in the morning or afternoon. If air guns straddled two analysis periods, the period with most of the air gun activity was chosen and the second period was not included. Air gun periods were therefore spread throughout the day and season. Periods in which control experiments were taking place were not used in this study. Whales migrated through the area quickly and reliably, and it was assumed that each day’s whales had not been exposed the previous day12.
Group number ranged from 1 to over 30 per 3 h, noting not all groups would have been in the area at the same time. However, to minimise issues with keeping track of multiple groups at a time, data were capped at a maximum of 20 likely males (<20 groups). This reduced the sample size to 67 baseline periods and 52 air gun periods. The first analysis aimed to determine if the number of singing males in the study area was significantly related to male number and/or female number using baseline data only. A generalised linear model structure was used (GLM), assuming a negative binomial distribution, with singer number as the response variable (given the response variable ranged from 0 to 3 with many time periods containing no singers). The number of (likely) males and females were included as the predictor variables.
Next, to test for an effect of active air guns, all data (‘baseline’ and ‘air gun’) were included and the term ‘air gun presence’ was added as an interaction effect. To account for trends between numbers of singers, males, and females, observed in the baseline data, these relationships were included as smooth terms with a GAM, with air gun activity (baseline or air guns) included as an additional covariate. A GAM structure was used given it does not assume a fixed relationship between the response variable (number of singers) and covariates (number of males or females) within baseline and air gun periods. Specifically, this tested if the relationships between the response and predictor variables found in the baseline data were maintained during periods of active air guns. Significance was set at p < 0.05 and model fits were assessed by comparing the residuals versus predicted values and expected distribution, testing for overdispersion, and testing for outliers.

## Tactical payoffs in the presence and absence of air guns
For every 3-h period, the number of observed joining interactions between single adults, with or without calves, were counted. These were classified into two categories; (i) singer join (defined as a singer changing direction to approach and actively joining a lone adult whale or a female with a calf; N = 54 observed singer joins within 35 time periods) and (ii) seeker joining (defined as a non-singing, presumed male, actively approaching and joining an adult or female with a calf; N = 134 observed seeker joins within 65 time periods). Each of these joins was counted as a payoff of 1 and totalled for the 3-h period. Most of the joining interactions involved a singer or lone animal joining a female-calf pair, with <10% involving an adult joining another adult of unknown sex to form an adult pair. Joins involving two adults of unknown sex were included, given, in many of these cases, further joins occurred indicating that a female was likley to be present. Instances where an adult actively approached and joined a singer (where the singer was stationary upon approach then stopped singing) were discounted due to the likelihood the joining animal was likely to be a male19. This gave a maximum payoff for singer and seeker payoff per 3 h based on the number of observed joins. The resultant groups were sometimes additionally joined by another (presumed) male. If this occurred in a singer group, the payoff for the singer was reduced to 0.5, as this additional male likely reduced the chances of the original joining singer accessing the female if receptive. In other words, if two singer joins were observed, but one of these groups was additionally joined by another male, the singer payoff for the 3-h period was 1.5. As this additional join to the singer group was a typical seeker tactic, seeker payoff for the 3-h period was increased by 0.5. This occurred six times in baseline conditions and three times in the presence of air guns, therefore, was not a common behavioural interaction. Additional joins to seeker groups, however, could involve up to three additional (likely) males (n = 74 additional joins within 56 time periods). Given there was still only one available female, the payoff for the seeker join was reduced from 1 to 1/number of males in the group, since there were multiple males potentially competing for access to the female. The most common interaction here was a male joining a female-calf pair to become a female-calf-escort group (payoff of 1), then being additionally joined by another male (payoff of 0.5). For every 3-h period, the final singer and seeker payoffs were the sum of all observed joins accounting for the costs incurred by additional joins. This gave an overall payoff for the singer tactic and seeker tactic.
Two sets of analyses were carried out. The first used two GLM models to test if seeker and singer payoffs were significantly related to the number of males and/or females in the area using baseline data only. Like the models above, a negative binomial structure was assumed, and model fits were assessed as described above.
If male or female numbers were a significant variable, they were included in the subsequent analyses. For seeker models, only time periods in which there was at least one presumed male were used (N = 67 in baseline conditions and N = 51 in the presence of air guns). This could include a singer, an escort, or a joining animal, and eliminated times in which there were only female-calf pairs in the area. For singer models, only times in which there was at least one singer in the area were used (N = 35 in baseline conditions and N = 43 in the presence of air guns).
The second set of analyses modelled the relationship between singer or seeker payoff and the presence of air guns (see below) whilst accounting for the number of males in the area, (given it was a significant effect within the baseline data). Total singer payoff was estimated using the total number of observed singer joins and additional joins to their group, then estimated per individual by dividing this by the number of singers in the area. Here it was assumed the presence of air guns would significantly change the prevalence of male joining, and additional joining, interactions. Significance was set at p < 0.05 and model assumptions were checked as described above.
