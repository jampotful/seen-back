Abstract

25

Dolphins are known to recognize their environment through echolocation. Previous studies have reported

26

that they can discriminate the shape, size, thickness, and even material of objects through echolocation. However,

27

little is known about the discrimination of quantities other than size and thickness (e.g., the number of objects). It

28

is also unclear whether Weber's law (i.e., ratio dependency) holds for quantity discrimination through echolocation.

29

In this study, we examined relative quantity judgments of visually occluded objects presented underwater by

30

bottlenose dolphins. We found that they could discriminate pairs of same-sized objects ranging from one to eight,

31

with performance improving as the difference ratio between the two numbers increased. In addition, their

32

performance also improved as the magnitude of the number of objects involved increased. An additional test

33

revealed that the accuracy of discrimination through echolocation was comparable to that of visual relative quantity

34

judgments of the objects presented above water. On the other hand, under the condition that the overall size of each

35

object (i.e., the sum of areas) was incongruent with the number of objects, performance was lower than when number

36

and size were covarying. However, even within the incongruent condition, the effect of the number ratio was still

37

observed, suggesting that the dolphins might have used various types of quantity information, such as number and

38

size, flexibly to solve the task.

Introduction

44

Odontocete cetaceans are known to use echolocation to orient themselves in their environment and to

45

recognize the structure of the environment (Au, 1993; Jones, 2005). They have also been reported to use

46

echolocation to detect prey (Jones & Sayigh, 2002; Nowacek, 1999; cf. Au et al., 2009; but see also Gannon et al.,

47

2005), and it has been suggested that they may detect other individuals by echolocation (Brownlee & Norris, 1994).

48

Many experimental studies have shown that they can discriminate the physical properties of objects (size, length,

49

thickness, etc.; Evans & Powell, 1967; Nakahara et al., 1997; for review, see also Au, 1988; Nachtigall, 1980) and

50

even materials (Au & Turl, 1991; Evans and Powell, 1967; Harley & DeLong, 2008). For example, Au and Turl

51

(1991) showed that bottlenose dolphins could use echolocation to discriminate between aluminum, stainless steel,

52

and coral rock. Evans and Powell (1967) showed that bottlenose dolphins discriminated the thickness of metal plates.

53

Discrimination of cylinder wall thickness has been reported in a bottlenose dolphin (Au & Pawloski, 1992) and a

54

finless porpoise (Nakahara et al., 1997). Furthermore, there is increasing evidence that dolphins can discriminate

55

not only these relatively simple physical properties but also the complex shapes of objects through echolocation and

56

visual-echo cross-modal matching of these objects (e.g., Harley et al., 1996, 2003; Herman et al., 1998; Pack &

57

Herman, 1995; Pack et al., 2002; see also Bruck & Pack, 2022, for a review).

58

Thus, it is clear that dolphins can use echolocation to discriminate quantitative properties of objects.

59

However, there is very little empirical evidence that dolphins are sensitive to the numerosity of discrete objects by

60

echolocation (Abramson et al., 2013), whereas visual discrimination studies have reported that they are capable of

61

relative numerosity judgments (Jaakkola et al., 2005; Kilian et al., 2003; Yaman et al., 2012; cf. Mitchel et al., 1985).

62

For dolphins, as for other animals, quantity information (including number) should be important in social and

63

ecological (e.g., foraging) contexts (Bortot et al., 2021; Bryer et al., 2021; Lorenzi et al., 2021; Nieder, 2020).

64

Because they reportedly use echolocation during foraging, quantity judgments of prey from echolocation should be

65

important to them. Abramson et al. (2013) conducted simultaneous quantity discrimination of fish presented behind

66

opaque panels with one beluga and three bottlenose dolphins and showed that the beluga passed this task, but the

67

dolphins did not (see also Figure 8). However, the beluga's performance did not follow Weber's law, which states

68

that the accuracy of relative quantity judgments is proportional to the ratio of the quantities being compared: there

69

were no trends between the beluga's performance and the ratio of fish numbers.

70

In the present study, we conducted experiments with two bottlenose dolphins using objects (made of bolts,

71

nuts, and washers) in a more systematic way to investigate whether they could discriminate relative quantity

72

differences between objects by echolocation and, if so, whether their performance followed Weber's law

73

(Experiment 1). In addition, we conducted a visual discrimination experiment using the same objects to compare

74

the dolphins' ability to make relative quantity judgments across modalities (Experiment 2). Importantly to note,

75

when examining relative quantity judgments, it is not so clear whether observers actually use the number of objects

76

as a cue to perform the task, as discrete numerical properties are often covariant with other continuous quantitative

77

properties (e.g., area, circumference, convex hull, density, spatial frequency, etc.; Bryer et al., 2021; Kilian et al.,

78

2003; Potrich et al., 2022; Tomonaga, 2008; Vallortigara et al., 2022; Zanon et al., 2022). Therefore, in Experiment

79

3, we created a condition in which the number and one of the other quantitative properties were incongruent by

80

manipulating the size of each object to address this issue.

Experiment 1

83

In Experiment 1, we tested whether the dolphins could use echolocation to make relative quantity

84

judgments of same-sized washers attached to the panels occluded by the opaque panels. We presented various pairs

85

from one to eight objects and examined whether their relative judgments were affected by the ratio of the number

86

of objects.

Methods

89

Participants and setting

90

The present study was conducted at the Minamichita Beachland Aquarium, Mihama Town, Aichi, Japan.

91

Two adult female bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops truncatus), Neri and Thihra (Figure 1), participated in this study,

92

and they were tested in accordance with their exhibit schedule. They were wild-born and approximately 12 years

93

old at the beginning of the present study. They had previous experiences with visual shape and object discrimination

94

training prior to this study (Sakurai & Tsuzuki, 2019; Tomonaga et al., 2023c). They had also been trained in material

95

discrimination through echolocation as part of the public program repertoire. According to the breeding management

96

plans and the public programs, they lived in pools in the dolphin exhibit area (approximately 13 m in diameter x 3

97

m in depth). They also participated in daily 15-minute public programs. The experiment was also conducted in these

98

pools. No food deprivation was used during the present study.

Stimuli

114

As an object, we used a circular washer fixed to an opaque polyvinyl chloride (PVC) panel with a hex

115

bolt and three nuts (all made of stainless steel; Figures 2A, 2B). The panel was 450 mm wide, 300 mm high, and 2

116

mm thick. The circular washer was 40 mm in diameter and 2 mm thick. The hex bolt was 10 mm in diameter and

117

40 mm long, with heads 16 mm wide and 8 mm thick. The nut was 16 mm wide and 8 mm thick. The weight of

118

each object was 100 g. The bolts and washers were fixed by drilling holes in positions randomly selected from the

119

predetermined 5 x 3 grid on the PVC panel. As shown in Figure 2B, a gap of approximately 20 mm was set between

120

the panel and the washers using two nuts and two small washers.

121

Nine different numbers of washers were prepared, ranging from 0 (none of the bolts were installed) to 8

122

(see Figure 1). For each number, six different panels were prepared; each of the six panels had a different

123

configuration of objects (Figure 3; for all configurations, see Table S1).

The apparatus for presenting these panels is shown in Figure 2C (see also Figure 1). The apparatus was

128

made of PVC pipe (outer diameter 30-40 mm), 1130 mm wide, and 480 mm high. There were frames on both sides

129

to hold the stimulus panels in place, and the panels were fixed by fitting them into slits in the frame pipe. Opaque

130

132

General procedure

134

In Experiment 1, one or two experimenters conducted the experiment. When conducted by two

135

experimenters, each trial began with the first experimenter holding the dolphin with her head out of the water on

136

the side opposite to where the stimulus was presented and the second experimenter presenting the apparatus with

137

the stimulus panels underwater. After confirming that the apparatus was presented, the first trainer signaled the

138

dolphin to swim to the apparatus on the opposite side (see Supplementary Video). On the other hand, when

139

conducted by one experimenter, each trial began with the dolphin waiting in front of the experimenter. After setting

140

the panels on the apparatus, the experimenter instructed the dolphin to leave the apparatus and then placed the

141

apparatus underwater. The dolphin then swam back to the apparatus.

142

The dolphin was required to locate the left or right panel with the more objects and shake her head up and

143

down. The head-shaking behavior was shaped by the experimenter prior to this experiment. If the dolphin's choice

144

was correct (i.e., the panel with the more objects), the trainer immediately whistled and then gave a piece of fish as

145

a reward. If the choice was incorrect, the second trainer pulled up the apparatus above the water. With this as a

146

signal, the dolphin returned to the starting point.

147

The trainers made every effort, consciously, to avoid giving the dolphins any cues to make decisions.

148

However, we did not use other strict procedures, such as double-blind methods. In addition, as the experiments were

149

conducted in the exhibit areas, the audience was not prevented from observing during the experimental sessions.

150

The dolphins' behavior during the experiment was video-recorded from the poolside or the underwater

151

viewing area of the pool. When approaching or in front of the panels, the dolphins, after initially orienting their

152

heads toward either the left or the right panel, often reoriented their heads toward the opposite panel (Figure 4). This

153

behavior has been observed in the previous study (Evans & Powell, 1967). We defined this behavior as "head-

154

turning" and counted the number of head turns for each trial from the video recordings. However, for Neri, the

155

underwater head turns could not be clearly identified because we recorded her behavior above the water. Therefore,

156

underwater head turns were only analyzed for Thihra, whose behavior was recorded from the underwater viewing

157

area.

Preliminary training

162

Before starting the main experiment, a series of preliminary training phases was conducted. First, after

163

shaping the behaviors of shaking the head in front of the panel by the successive approximation method, a triangular

164

shape made of a vinyl hose or a panel with eight objects attached was paired with another panel without any objects,

165

and the dolphin was trained to choose the panel with the stimuli. The correct position was randomized in each trial.

166

This initial training was performed for a total of 34 trials in 2 sessions for Thihra and 34 trials in 4 sessions for Neri.

167

After that, 8 vs. 0 training was introduced using the apparatus. Each session consisted of 12 trials, with

168

the left and right positions randomized. The same stimulus panel was used in every trial, with a maximum of two

169

sessions per day. While we did not set strict criteria for the termination of the training, we conducted it until the

170

percentage of correct responses reached at least 80% in one session and then until it became stable at 80% or higher

171

for at least two sessions.

172

Following this training, we moved on to 8 vs. 1 training. As in the previous training, one session consisted

173

of 12 trials, and the left and right positions were randomized. First, training was conducted with the same stimulus

174

panel. This condition was also conducted until the percentage of correct responses reached at least 80% in one

175

session and then until it became stable at 80% or higher for at least two sessions. Then, we moved to a condition in

176

which a different panel was used for each trial (pseudo-random condition). This condition was conducted for eight

177

sessions for each dolphin.

178

Main experiment

179

The dolphins shifted to the main experiment immediately after the preliminary training with 8 vs. 1 was

180

completed. All combinations of numbers from one to seven were presented in the main experiment. In principle,

181

each pair was conducted for eight sessions, and when completed, the pair was changed to another pair. The order of

182

the stimulus pairs was randomized for each dolphin (Table S2). One session consisted of 12 trials, half of which

183

were 8 vs. 1 as the baseline trials, and the rest were test trials in which the test pair was presented. Baseline and test

184

trials were alternately presented. The left and right correct positions were randomized and counterbalanced. The

185

baseline trials were introduced to prevent a deterioration in overall performance due to the presentation of difficult

186

trials.

187

Data analysis

188

The dolphins' responses were recorded on-site by the experimenter (first author), but to check the coding

189

reliability, the second author also coded the responses from the video recordings of one session of each condition

190

(12.5% of all trials). The percentages of agreement were 96.8% for Neri and 97.2% for Thihra.

191

As mentioned above, only two dolphins participated in this experiment, so we analyzed the data for each

192

individual separately using general linear mixed modeling (GLMM). The response variable of interest was the

193

proportion of correct choices, which followed a binomial distribution. We included two types of fixed effects in our

194

analysis. Firstly, we incorporated the difference ratio between the two numbers. In this study, the ratio was defined

195

as the difference between the larger and smaller numbers divided by the smaller number [(Large - Small) / Small]

196

(Tomonaga et al., 2023a). Secondly, we included the larger of the two numbers as a measure of the magnitude of

197

the number of objects. Both explanatory variables were log-transformed using ordinary logarithms. Furthermore,

198

we treated sessions as a random effect. We assessed various random-intercept models, incorporating these effects

199

and all possible interactions, and selected the model with the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC).

200

Additionally, we evaluated each parameter estimate based on 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using Wald's test

201

statistic. The data from baseline trials (8 vs. 1) was not used for the analysis, but the data from 8 vs. 1 trials in the

202

last phase of preliminary training was incorporated into the analysis.

203

To further examine the generalization or transfer of relative judgments across pairs, we ran GLMMs on

204

the data from the first session, using null models with each pair as random factors, and evaluated the models based

205

on estimates of the intercept.

206
207

For head-turning behavior, we also conducted GLMM analyses using Poisson distribution. The results of
the model selection processes in the present study are presented in Table S3.

208

These analyses were performed using lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017) and MuMIn (Bartoń, 2022)

209

packages in R version 4.2.0 (R Core Team, 2022). The data sets used for these analyses for each dolphin are provided

210

in the Supplementary Material.

Influence of limited number of stimulus panels
In this experiment, we prepared six different panels for each number. This was to avoid the possibility

248

that the dolphins would solve the task by attending to a particular location or pattern in the panel rather than making

249

relative quantity judgments of objects in the panel. However, six panels may be too few to accomplish this purpose.

250

Therefore, to investigate this possibility, we ran simple simulations. The detailed procedure is described in the

251

Supplementary File.

252

In these simulations, we randomly generated patterns of object configurations in the panels for each pair

253

and calculated the expected correct response rate, assuming that the dolphins always attended to only one specific

254

location in the panel or to two locations (minimal requirements for constituting a geometric pattern; Figure SF1).

255

As a result of 100,000 random simulations for each pair, the estimated correct response rate was 59.5%

256

(SEM = 1.3) for one specific location and 64.6% (SEM = 1.0) for two locations, respectively (Table SF1). Using

257

the estimated and actual correct response rates, we ran Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for paired data, in which we

258

corrected p-values with Holm’s procedure for multiple comparisons. As a result, These estimated rates were

259

significantly lower than the actual correct response rates for the dolphins (see Figure SF2; one location; Neri, V =

260

5, p < 0.001; Thihra, V =0, p < 0.001; two locations; Neri, V = 23, p < 0.001; Thihra, V = 1, p < 0.001).

Head-turning behavior

263

Thihra made at least one head turn under the water in 98.0% of the trials analyzed. She showed more head

264

turns on incorrect trials than on correct trials (Figure 7A; GLMM, β = 0.219, SE = 0.040, 95% CI = [0.141, 0.298]).

265

Figure 7B shows the mean number of head turns for correct test trials as a function of the difference ratio and the

266

larger number. As can be seen from the graph, the number of head turns decreased with increasing ratio and

267

increasing larger number. The results of the GLMM analysis also supported this visual inspection; the model with

268

the smallest AIC included the fixed effects of difference ratio and larger number, and their interactions (Table S5).

Discussion

270

The present experiment showed that the bottlenose dolphins were able to discriminate the arrays with

271

different numbers of objects using echolocation. This finding was consistent with the results of Abramson et al.

272

(2013) in a beluga. The dolphins' relative quantity judgments were clearly influenced by the difference ratio of the

273

numbers of objects. In addition, for one of the dolphins, Thihra, the magnitude of the number of objects involved

274

also affected her performance: the larger the magnitude, the better the performance.

Furthermore, their performance was better than chance from the first session of each pair. This strongly

280

suggests that they performed the task by making "relative" quantity judgments rather than attending to a specific

281

location or pattern in the panels. This conclusion is also consistent with the results of the additional simulation

282

experiment.

283

Figure 8 compares data from previous studies of relative quantity judgments in bottlenose dolphins and

284

beluga with the results of the present experiment. Our results are consistent with a previous study showing the effect

285

of difference ratio using visual stimuli (Jaakkola et al., 2005). We re-analyzed Jaakkola et al.'s data based on the

286

published dataset provided by Bryer et al. (2021) and also found a positive magnitude effect in addition to the ratio

287

effect, similar to our results (Table S6).

288
289

290

condition (see also Figure 10). Black circles represent the results from this study (averaged across dolphins), and

291

the other symbols represent results from the previous research.

292
293

The fact that the ratio effect was observed in relative quantity judgments using echolocation suggests that

294

the dolphins may have used information in the echoes that systematically changed in proportion to the quantities as

295

discriminative cues. However, we should not immediately conclude from these results that they did use numerosity

296

as a cue. Since all the objects used in this experiment were of the same size, it is not clear whether they used the

297

number of discrete objects or some other quantitative cues (area, circumference, density, etc.). This issue is further

298

investigated in Experiment 3.

299

Our results contradict the previous results from the beluga (Abramson et al., 2013; Figure 8), which

300

showed no ratio effects in either the echo or visual conditions. One possibility for this discrepancy could be the

301

material difference between fish and stainless steel. The use of fish with a high reward value may also have affected

302

the beluga's performance. In a study with tortoises (Tomonaga et al., 2023a), the ratio effect was observed in an

303

experiment using food pieces as stimuli, but the magnitude effect was opposite to the present results. That is, the

304

larger the magnitude, the worse the performance. When using materials with high reward values, such as food, a

305

ceiling effect of the sum of reward values or a bias toward non-quantitative properties may affect relative quantity

306

judgments. These possibilities should also be investigated in cetaceans (see also Beran et al., 2007, 2008, for results

307

with nonhuman primates).

308

As already noted, we found an effect of the magnitude of the number of objects in one of the dolphins.

309

This finding suggests that the quantitative change in echo information due to the increase in the magnitude of the

310

number itself may have facilitated discrimination by the dolphins to some degree. This point will need to be further

311

examined in the future.

312

In this experiment, the two dolphins often turned their heads from side to side before doing head-shaking

313

responses, as though they were "visually" comparing the two panels. Due to the panels with objects being occluded,

314

they did not actually "look at" the arrays of objects, but they must have produced clicks while orienting their

315

rostrums toward each panel. Given that clicks emitted by bottlenose dolphins are highly directional (Branstetter et

316

al., 2012), the head-turning might have enabled them to receive reflection signals from each panel individually.

317

Unfortunately, however, recording and analyzing the specific clicks emitted during this study was not feasible.

318

Interestingly, this head-turning behavior by one of the two dolphins, Thihra, was more frequent on

319

incorrect trials than on correct trials and more frequent for smaller difference ratios and smaller magnitudes. These

320

trends are consistent with the relationship between accuracy and the effect of the difference ratio and the larger

321

number. In other words, as the difficulty of the task increased, so did the number of head turns, and presumably the

322

number of clicks emissions (cf. Evans & Powell, 1967). Depending on the difficulty of the task, the dolphins may

323

have tried to seek more information necessary for decision-making. Similar results have been reported in a

324

chimpanzee (Tomonaga & Matsuzawa, 2002). The chimpanzee performed a matching task in which she had to

325

choose an Arabic numeral corresponding to the number of dots presented on the screen of the monitor, and she

326

looked back at that screen more frequently as the number of dots increased (i.e., as the task became more difficult).

327

Recently, Harley et al. (2022) reported a dolphin's behavior similar to the head-turning behavior observed in the

328

present study. They tested a dolphin on the matching to sample task under the echo condition and found that the

329

number of clicks emitted by the dolphin varied depending on the task difficulty. These behaviors may also be

330

discussed in relation to information-seeking behavior in the context of metacognition (cf. Call & Carpenter, 2001;

331

Tomonaga et al., 2023b).

Procedure

351

Experiment 2 was conducted approximately four months after Experiment 1. A single experimenter

352

mainly conducted experimental sessions. Sometimes, a second experimenter assisted in preparing the panels and

353

Experiment 3

418

One of the problems that always arises in relative quantity judgments in nonhuman animals is that it is

419

unclear which quantitative cues observers actually used because the number of discrete objects and the other

420

quantitative cues (area, circumference, convex hull, density, spatial frequency, etc.) derived from the arrangement

421

and size of the items usually covary (e.g., Tomonaga, 2008). One way to address this issue is to include a condition

422

in which the number of objects is incongruent with the other quantitative cues. In Experiment 3, we manipulated

423

one of the other quantitative cues, the size of the washers, to gain some insight into the cues for relative judgments

424

in the echo condition.

In Experiment 3, we manipulated washer size to assess which of the two quantitative types of information,

498

the number of washers and total area (or total circumference) of the washers were cues the dolphins used in the

499

500

501

total area and number of items were covariant. This finding suggests that the total area cue may have been primarily

502

used in the dolphins' relative quantity judgments rather than the number of items. These results are also consistent

503

with previous studies in which dolphins and a finless porpoise discriminated object size and thickness using

504

echolocation (Au & Pawloski, 1992; Nakahara et al., 1997).

On the other hand, interestingly, when compared within the incongruent condition, discrimination

performance improved with increasing the number difference ratio. This result suggests that dolphins used not only

the total area but also the number of washers as a cue in some conditions (cf. Cantlon et al., 2007; Ferrigno et al.,

510

this time. Previous studies on chimpanzees have demonstrated that an attentional bias towards a specific object that

511

is larger than the others, rather than the total number or area, influenced their responses (Beran et al., 2008, 2009;

512

Tomonaga, 2004; cf. Tomonaga, 2001). Although these findings were derived from studies using visual stimuli,

513

specific cues, such as the size of a particular object, could have caused a response bias in relative quantity judgments,

514

even when non-visual cues are involved as in the present study with dolphins. Systematic examination of this point

515

by further manipulating the number of pairs and size of washers will be necessary.

516

In this experiment, we focused on the total area of the washers to create conditions where the ratio of

517

object numbers was incongruent with that of the total area. However, changing the washer sizes also affected the

518

weight of each object. The weight is determined by the size (or volume) and material (or density) of the object.

519

Considering that dolphins have shown the ability to discriminate materials using echolocation (Au & Turl, 1991;

520

Evans and Powell, 1967; Harley & DeLong, 2008), we cannot rule out the possibility that, in this experiment, they

521

used not only the total area but also the total weight of the objects as a cue.

522

However, when calculating the difference ratio of the total weights of objects, the panel with the larger

523

number and smaller area (S) was consistently heavier than the panel with the smaller number and larger area (L)

524

across all S-L conditions (range of the difference ratio: 0.43 to 1.05). This indicates that the difference ratio of the

525

total weights was congruent with that of the number. Taken together with the results that the dolphins tended to

526

choose smaller numbers and larger areas in many S-L pairs, it seems unlikely that the dolphins utilized weight

527

information contained in the echoes as a cue.

General discussion

529

In this study, we examined the performance of relative quantity judgments of the discrete objects by

530

echolocation in two bottlenose dolphins. The results of Experiments 1 and 2 showed that the dolphins did make

531

relative quantity judgments not only in the visual condition but also in the echo condition. Furthermore, as in many

532

previous studies in various species (Bryer et al., 2021; Jaakkola et al., 2005; Tomonaga, 2008), their relative

533

judgments in the echo condition were found to follow the ratio of quantitative differences, that is, Weber’s law.

534

Interestingly, not only the ratio but also the magnitude of the number of objects (the larger number of

535

the two) was found to affect the performance of one of the two dolphins. For Thihra, in both the echo and visual

536

conditions, the larger the size of the number being compared, the better the performance. This finding is consistent

537

with a previous study with dolphins in the visual condition (Jaakkola et al., 2005). If this finding is considered

538

rigorously, it might be concluded that one dolphin’s relative quantity judgments in the present study and Jaakkola

539

et al. deviated from Weber's law, which states that only the ratio affects the relative quantity judgment regardless of

540

the magnitude. Tomonaga et al. (2023a) reported that the performance of African spurred tortoises was also affected

541

by the larger amount of food, but they found that the performance of the tortoises decreased as the number of food

542

items to be compared increased. In contrast, no such magnitude effects were found in our previous experiments with

543

chimpanzees under visual conditions (Tomonaga, 2008).

544

Bryer et al. (2021) collected and analyzed data from published studies involving 33 different species of

545

birds and mammals. Using the dataset they used, we also examined the effect of the magnitude of the number of

546

objects. Summary of these analyses is shown in Table S7. We found that among these 33 species, 10 had 95% CIs

547

of parameter estimates for the magnitude, or the interaction between magnitude and ratio did not contain zero,

548

indicating that the magnitude of the number of objects affected the relative quantity judgments. Of these 10 species,

549

six had positive parameter estimates, indicating that their performance improved with increasing the magnitude,

550

while five had negative values (with one species having a positive magnitude estimate and a negative interaction

551

estimate). The study of one of the species with negative estimate values (North Island robins, Garland et al., 2012)

552

used food as stimuli, as in the study of tortoises (Tomonaga et al., 2023a). On the other hand, only seven species

553

showed a positive effect of the ratio alone, six of which were nonhuman primates, including chimpanzees.

554

Based on the present findings from dolphins and the reanalysis of published studies described above, it is

555

suggested that the effect of ratio and the effect of magnitude should be considered when studying relative quantity

556

judgments in nonhuman animals. To answer whether the magnitude effect is due to species differences or

557

experimental procedures, further studies are needed in the future.

558

In the present study, we could not fully clarify whether the dolphin's relative quantity judgments using

559

echolocation were based on numerical cues. Based on the main results of Experiment 3, the answer seems to be

560

"no." When the ratios of the number difference and the total area of the washers were incongruent, the dolphins'

561

judgments deteriorated substantially. This finding differs from previous studies in the visual condition where the

562

total area was controlled (Kilian et al., 2003; Jaakkola et al., 2005) and suggests that the dolphins may have been

563

primarily sensitive to other quantitative properties of objects such as area size, not the number, in the present

564

condition.

565

However, a closer look at the results also revealed that the ratio of the number of objects affected their

566

performance within the incongruent condition: accuracy improved as the ratio of the number of objects increased.

567

This finding suggests that the "number-related" echo cues reflected from discrete objects, in addition to other

568

quantitative cues, play some role in the dolphin's decision-making. In the future, it will be necessary to compare the

569

relative judgments based on the various quantitative properties as well as the number by dolphin echolocation from

570

a psychophysical perspective.

571

In this study, we manipulated the number and size of objects. These manipulations were proposed on the

572

basis of previous studies using visual stimuli. Of course, the cues actually used by the dolphins were not visual but

573

auditory (echo) cues reflected from the physical objects. Studies investigating cylinder thickness discrimination by

574

echolocation in cetaceans have reported that the time difference in arrival of the second echo highlights, the

575

frequency difference, the time separation pitch, and the intensity difference of the echo signal components change

576

linearly as a function of the difference in thickness of the two cylinders being compared (Au & Pawloski, 1992;

577

Nakahara et al., 1997). Unfortunately, those studies did not clarify which of these covariant features (or mixture of

578

them) was "actually" used primarily as a discriminative cue by cetaceans.

579

Which components in the echo signal did the dolphins use for relative quantity judgments? This is a very

580

important question that needs to be addressed. However, because we did not record the dolphins' clicks during the

581

tasks in this study, no firm conclusions can be drawn about this issue from the results of this study. Many studies

582

have examined what information dolphins use in their echoes as cues for discrimination and recognition (for review,

583

see Au, 1993; Harley & DeLong, 2008). As briefly described above, for example, it has been reported that very

584

subtle difference in intensity of echoes may have been used as a cue in discriminating between plates of different

585

sizes. (cf. Harley & DeLong, 2008). Other studies have also suggested that dolphins use differences in frequency,

586

arrival time shift, and time separation pitch as discriminative cues (Au & Pawloski, 1992; Au &Turl, 1991; Hammer

587

& Au, 1980; Nakahara et al., 1997). In addition, simulation studies using neural network models have reported that

588

models incorporating temporal as well as acoustic spectral information were able to discriminate object properties

589

with relatively high accuracy (Au, 1994; Au et al., 1995). As suggested by the modeling studies (cf. Branstetter et

590

al., 2007; Delong et al., 2006), it is more plausible that these multiple features were used for discrimination in an

591

integrated manner (for other species, see also Cantlon et al., 2007; Ferrigno er al., 2017).

592

The results of Experiment 3 suggest that if the physical (i.e., visual) properties are appropriately controlled

593

(e.g., by making the panels equal in area, circumference, weight, etc., and differing only in the number of objects;

594

e.g., Zanon et al., 2022), we may be able to more clearly demonstrate the dolphin's relative "numerosity" judgments

595

through echolocation. Further studies under such conditions and analysis of which components in the echoes of

596

such stimuli correspond to number will be needed in the future.

597

It is also known that the echo signal components described above vary in response to other non-

598

quantitative properties of the objects to be compared (e.g., material; Nakahara et al., 1997). Based on this fact,

599

testing dolphins to make relative judgments of objects of a particular material in arrays composed of a mixture of

600

objects of different materials or properties (e.g., stainless steel vs. aluminum; cf. rats, Taniuchi et al., 2016,

601

chimpanzees, Tomonaga & Imura, 2002; horses, Tomonaga et al., 2017), or about the number of total objects

602

irrespective of materials (cf. human children, Gelman, 1980) would provide further insight into echolocation-based

603

number perception in dolphins.

604

Throughout the present study, the dolphins frequently exhibited head-turning behavior while approaching

605

and in front of the panel in the echo conditions. Although we did not record the clicks they emitted in this study,

606

given the high directionality of the clicks (Branstetter, 2012), this behavior strongly suggests that they emitted clicks

607

to the panel multiple times. The head-turning behavior changed with task difficulty (as indicated by the number

608

ratio or accuracy), consistent with the patterns of head-turning behavior in previous studies (Evans & Powell, 1967).

609

This behavior may be reflective of an information-seeking strategy in the context of metacognition, as noted by

610

Harley et al. (2022). These results may suggest that the echo condition is one of the best conditions for investigating

611

metacognition and decision-making processes represented by information-seeking behavior in dolphins (Call &

612

Carpenter, 2001; Hampton, 2009; Harley et al., 2022; Smith et al., 1995; Tomonaga & Matsuzawa, 2002; Tomonaga

613

et al., 2023b).

614

The present experiment was conducted in a face-to-face setting. As well-established in the classic case of

615

Clever Hans (Pfungst, 1911), animals may be sensitive to intentional or unconscious cues presented by the

616

experimenter. Since previous studies have shown that dolphins are able to use social cues given by the experimenter

617

(Pack & Herman, 2004, 2006, 2007; Tschudin et al., 2001; cf. Tomonaga et al., 2010), this issue needed even more

618

attention. The best way to avoid this possibility is to conduct experiments in which the experimenter facing the

619

dolphins is unaware of the correct side by using a double-blind method (e.g., Jaakkola et al., 2005), but the present

620

experimental setting did not allow us for such strict control. In addition, because the experiments were conducted

621

in the exhibit area of the aquarium, it was not possible to completely prevent visitors from presenting social cues to

622

or interacting with the dolphins during the experimental sessions. In this study, efforts were made not to give social

623

cues from the experimenters as much as possible. If the dolphins were using the experimenter-given cues, their

624

performance should show some degree of high accuracy independent of the task difficulty indicated by the ratio of

625

the stimuli. However, as the results clearly show, there were clear ratio effects on their performance in Experiments

626

1 and 2. Furthermore, in the incongruent condition of Experiment 3, the dolphins chose the opposite stimulus to the

627

correct one set by the experimenter. These results strongly suggest, albeit indirectly, that the cues provided by the

628

experimenter did not contribute to their performance, but it will be necessary to implement stricter controls in the

629

future. It may also be possible to conduct experiments that directly assess the effects of experimenter cues, for

630

example, by examining the behavior of dolphins when presented with social cues that are inconsistent with correct

631

responses (cf. Scandurra et al., 2018).

632

In this study, we found that dolphins are able to make quantity judgments about discrete objects using

633

echolocation (and also visual properties). This ability should play an important role in social and ecological contexts

634

(cf. Nieder, 2020). However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have been conducted on wild dolphins from

635

this perspective. Further field studies are needed to explore the functional significance of echolocation in more detail,

636

although observing echolocation-related behavior in the wild may be extremely difficult. We should not forget the

637

fundamental question of whether "numbers" are important to dolphins from an adaptive standpoint. For dolphins,

638

approximate quantity judgments, rather than numerical judgments, may be sufficient for survival.

639

The question of whether number is a primary cue or a last resort for animals has been discussed by many

640

researchers (for a review, see Nieder, 2020). A clear bias toward number has been reported in several studies of

641

primates, including humans (Clanton et al., 2007; Ferrigno et al., 2017; Hauser et al., 2000; Piantadosi & Cantlon,

642

2017; Strandburg-Peshkin et al., 2015). On the other hand, it has been suggested that cues other than the number

643

(sometimes non-quantitative cues) may control behavior in many contexts (e.g., Beran et al., 2008, 2009; cf.

644

Tomonaga et al., 2023a). The role of number in spontaneous quantity judgments by dolphins in a variety of contexts

645

will require further investigation in the future.

646

The present study examined relative quantity judgments in dolphins under echo conditions, but the

647

discussion so far has been based on the results of only the two individuals. Furthermore, a previous study in a

648

different species (beluga) found no effect of the difference ratio, and bottlenose dolphins did not pass the echo

649

condition (Abramson et al., 2013). Therefore, we must be cautious about whether our results are representative of

650

bottlenose dolphins. For example, we have discussed the magnitude effect, but our results indicate that this effect

651

was salient only in one of the two dolphins (see Figures 6 and 10, and Table 1). This may suggest a potential

652

limitation in our conclusions. Studies with larger numbers of dolphins are essential to overcome this weakness.

653

However, the number of individuals available in each aquarium or other facility is currently quite limited: most

654

studies of discrimination by echolocation to date have been based on only one to a few individuals (e.g., Abramson

655

et al., 2013; Au & Pawloski, 1992; Harley et al., 1996; Nakahara et al., 1997).

656

On the other hand, dolphin echolocation is very popular in public programs in aquariums, and there should

657

be many dolphins mastering echolocation-based discrimination tasks in a number of aquariums. The dolphins in our

658

study had also performed echolocation-based tasks in the public program before the onset of the present study. As

659

a future prospect, it would be possible to collaborate among such multiple aquariums and conduct replication studies

660

using a large number of individuals from the various species under the same controlled procedures. Such

661

collaborative efforts are already underway with human infants, nonhuman primates, dogs, and birds (Alberghina et

662

al., 2022; Lambert et al., 2022; ManyPrimates, 2019a, b; The ManyBabies Consortium, 2020). Odontocetes would

663

be one of the best species groups for such research projects. Certainly, it is true that further collaboration among

664

researchers is needed to understand dolphins better (cf. Tomonaga et al., 2023b).

Supplementary experiment: Influence of limited number of stimulus panels

In the present study, we prepared panels with six different configurations for each number
and randomly paired these panels in the experiments. These manipulations were designed to eliminate
as much as possible the use by the dolphins of cues unrelated to the quantities, such as the presence or
absence of an object at a particular location in the panel or a particular geometric pattern in the panel.
However, only six patterns might not be enough, given that nonhuman animals can remember a very
large number of stimuli. Our dolphins in the present study may not have been making "relative"
quantity judgments but rather doing something completely different, such as memorizing all the
patterns.
Therefore, we conducted an additional simulation experiment to gain some insight into this
possibility. In this experiment, we assumed that the (simulated) dolphins did not make "relative"
quantity judgments but instead chose a particular panel by attending to only one or two locations in
the panel. If there was an object at the attended location(s), they chose that panel with 100% probability,
whereas if there was an object (or no object) at that location in both panels, they chose the panel with
50% probability.
The simulation experiment was conducted under two conditions: one in which only one
location was attended and the other in which two locations that could form a minimal geometric pattern
were attended. In each simulation trial, we first generated a random pattern of object configurations
on the panel. Then, for each trial, the simulation program also randomly determined which of the 15
(5x3) locations on the panel would be attended.
Figure SF1 shows examples of panel patterns generated in this way (the case of 5 vs. 3).
Black or dotted circles indicate the attended location and black or white circles indicate the location
where the object was placed. Dotted circles indicate the attended but empty location. If there is an

object in the attended location in the 5-object panel but not in the 3-object panel (the top row of the
figure), the simulated dolphin will always choose the correct panel (five objects). On the other hand,
if there is an object in the attended location but only in the 3-object panel, the dolphin will always
choose the 3-object panel (always incorrect; the bottom row of the figure). If there is an object or no
object in the attended locations of both panels, one of the panels is chosen 50% of the time, and so on.
In the case of two attended locations (right side of the figure), if there is an object in only one of the
two locations, that panel is chosen. Under these rules, 100,000 random simulations were performed
for each pair to obtain the expected correct response rate. The simulation program was written in
Visual Basic 6.0®.
The results of the simulation are presented in Table SF1. Figure SF2 also shows the
comparisons of the correct response rates between the simulations and the real dolphins in
Experiments 1 and 2. These results were analyzed using paired t-tests (results are presented in the main
text).
