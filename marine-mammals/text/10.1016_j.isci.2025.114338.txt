Functional trade-offs and innovation shape the adaptive landscape of aquatic mammal feeding.

# Functional trade-offs and innovation shape the adaptive landscape of aquatic mammal feeding

## Abstract
SummaryFunctional trade-offs are inherent in phenotypes due to the need to balance multiple competing selection pressures. Traditionally regarded as constraints on evolution, trade-offs have recently been reframed as facilitators of adaptation via the changing relative importance of competing functions. Here, we examine these ideas through the lens of aquatic mammal feeding, testing a behavioral aquatic feeding framework where feeding strategies form an evolutionary continuum from terrestrial to increasingly more specialized water-based feeding styles. Specifically, we hypothesized that suction, suction filter, and ram filter feeding would have adaptive peaks closer together than raptorial feeding, and that taxa follow the functionally optimal evolutionary path (Pareto front) between adaptive peaks. Constructing morphofunctional adaptive landscapes from cetacean mandibles revealed strong support for this framework. Surprisingly, most cetaceans do not lie along the Pareto front between peaks, suggesting that novel functional innovations— most likely the specialized cetacean auditory pathway—are also influencing mandibular evolution.Subject areas: Biological sciences, Zoology, Evolutionary biology

## Introduction
A fundamental aspect of evolution is the relationship between complex structures and their function.1 Organisms need to perform multiple tasks, all of which contribute to fitness2; however, a given phenotype cannot be optimal at all tasks and functional trade-offs ensue,1,3,4 influencing the phenotype of an organism, its ecology, and its behavior.5 Consequently, the examination of these systems can provide useful insights into macroevolutionary patterns. One particularly well-suited system in this regard is tetrapod feeding. It possesses a large degree of variation of essentially homologous parts, the alteration of which will have significant fitness effects, resulting in trade-offs and constraints.6 One of the best examples of this is aquatic mammal feeding. These lineages (e.g., cetaceans, pinnipeds, and sirenians) have explored numerous innovations in feeding phenotypes and performance, and act as consumers at almost every trophic level, with some species capable of switching between levels.7,8,9,10,11,12 They additionally play crucial roles in aquatic ecosystems (e.g., as nutrient distributors13), highlighting the importance of understanding the evolution of their feeding strategies.
Traditionally, aquatic mammal feeding has been divided into three distinct groups: raptorial, suction, and filter feeding,14 but this simple heuristic does not capture the complexity and plasticity displayed by these animals. As a result, Hocking et al.7,15 proposed a new behavior-based framework for aquatic mammal feeding that attempted to resolve these issues, with more nuanced feeding strategies and the feeding cycle split into four functional stages. They proposed that the feeding strategies of living aquatic mammals form a parsimonious evolutionary continuum that recalls the land-to-water transition of their ancestors. Along this continuum, there is a key boundary between feeding strategies where water is a hindrance (terrestrial, semi-aquatic, and raptorial feeding) and where the physical properties of water are exploited and incorporated as part of the feeding strategy (suction, suction filter, and ram filter feeding) (Figure 1). In this study, we quantify this feeding framework by constructing morphofunctional adaptive landscapes, an approach that has previously been used to investigate other higher-level functions such as limb posture and locomotion, as well as feeding.16,17,18,19,20,21 We then reconstruct the optimal evolutionary paths between feeding strategies to test the proposed sequence of transitions between them.

Adaptive landscapes are the dominant paradigm in evolutionary biology for visualizing the effect of selection and functional tradeoffs on macroevolution. First developed for use in genetics by Wright22 almost a century ago, Simpson23,24 repurposed the idea for deep time analyses, with the topography of the landscape relating morphological variation to fitness. The primary method of estimating adaptive landscapes has been through macroevolutionary models, e.g., Ornstein-Uhlenbeck models, where shifts in trait evolution represent adaptive peaks.25,26,27,28 More recently, there have been alternative approaches developed. Some use the density of phenotypes in a morphospace,29,30 whereas others use functional trait data to produce performance surfaces or spaces which are combined into adaptive landscapes or Pareto landscapes.16,19,31,32 We employ these latter approaches to study cetacean feeding for the first time. To do so, we use a broad sample of living and extinct cetacean mandibles (n = 107 species), the key component of the feeding apparatus, as our case study. The mandible also has strong links established between form and function,4,19,33,34 which can also be extensively taxonomically sampled across deep time, facilitating macroevolutionary analyses.
Here, we aim to test the proposed evolutionary continuum of the aquatic mammal feeding framework by examining the functional trade-offs that characterize the transition between feeding strategies. We test the following hypotheses: (H1) feeding strategies that require water will have peaks that are closer together; and (H2) given the high selective pressure of feeding, taxa follow the optimal evolutionary path (Pareto front) between adaptive peaks.
We find that water-dominated feeding strategies tend to occupy a similar region of the adaptive landscape. Surprisingly, competing selective pressures on the cetacean mandible have been sufficient to drive the evolution of morphologies that are not only optimized for feeding.

## Results

## Geometric morphometrics
Principal components analysis of 546 landmarks describing mandibular shape variation in 107 species of cetacean revealed insights into feeding evolution. Principal component 1 (PC1) accounted for 51.51% of mandibular shape variation (Figure 2). The negative values of this axis represent mandibles that are long, narrow, and pointed, with a long mandibular symphysis and a relatively small mandibular foramen. The positive values of this axis represent mandibles that are short, wide, and blunt, with a relatively large mandibular foramen. PC2 accounted for 19.81% of the mandibular shape variation (Figure 2). The negative values of this axis represent mandibles that are wide and laterally convex, with relatively low coronoid processes, anteroposteriorly shortened mandibular foramina, short symphyses, and circular mandibular condyles. The positive values of this axis represent mandibles that are narrow and laterally concave, with relatively high coronoid processes, anteroposteriorly elongate mandibular foramina, long symphyses, an upturned tip of the mandible, and reniform mandibular condyles.

## Performance surfaces
Using measurements from the 107 taxa and 30 theoretical shape warp meshes evenly spaced across the morphospace, six functional proxies that capture the functionality of cetacean feeding strategies were calculated (snap, relative mandibular body length, relative symphyseal length, mandibular bluntness index (MBI), opening ability, oral volume). These were then used to construct performance surfaces, which were interpolated across the morphospace. Each proxy covaries differently across the morphospace, with the snapping proxy and relative symphyseal length being the most similar (Figure 3). The varying positions of the peaks on these performance surfaces highlight that the morphologies that perform best are different among functional proxies, indicating functional trade-offs in the mandible. Larger oral volumes and large mandibular bodies are optimized across the lower part of the morphospace, whereas rapid jaw opening speeds are optimized in the lower right portion of the morphospace. Strong suction ability, in the form of a high MBI (i.e., a wide and short mandible), is optimized in the top right corner of the morphospace, as is having a relatively small mandibular body.

## Adaptive landscapes
Adaptive landscapes were generated by combining the six performance surfaces and optimizing their individual contributions to maximize the height of the landscape at the feeding strategy mean shape (Figure 4). The probability of significant differences among landscapes was determined pairwise as the proportion of models that were shared by both landscapes in the top 5% of models (Table 1). The raptorial landscape was significantly different from the suction filter and ram filter landscapes (p < 0.001), shown by a distinct peak on the landscape in a separate region of the morphospace (Figure 4). It is heavily weighted toward relative symphyseal length and the snapping proxy (50%) (Table 2). It is not significantly different from the suction landscape. The other three landscapes (suction, suction filter, ram filter) show sequential overlap (Figure 4; Table 1), with the peaks of the latter two in a similar region of the morphospace. The suction landscape is heavily weighted toward MBI and the snapping proxy (67%). The suction filter landscape is mainly weighted between MBI (47%) and oral volume (29%), but relative mandibular body length also plays a role (21%). Lastly, the RAM filter landscape is equally weighted between oral volume (37%) and MBI (35%). For all adaptive landscapes, the taxa that employ the modeled feeding strategy fall close to the adaptive peak, but none lie directly on the center of any peak, suggesting no mandible morphology is perfectly optimized for the mean of any one feeding strategy.

## Pareto landscapes
To determine the “optimal” path through morphospace from one feeding strategy to another, we calculated five Pareto optimality landscapes (Figure 5). Each Pareto landscape was generated by combining two adaptive landscapes for each feeding strategy (representing a transitional step in the feeding framework). There are clear regions of high optimality between peaks that together form a path across the top of the morphospace, down the right-hand side of it, and then along the bottom. This path mirrors the sequence of transitions of the behavioral feeding framework. However, the majority of taxa lie far from the Pareto front on all landscapes, implying the exploration of novel morphologies and functions, and either the presence of new functional drivers or the weakening of functional constraints.21

## Discussion
Feeding is a core biological process and a key interaction between a predator and its environment. Its fundamental importance and the wide range of phenotypic diversity in tetrapod feeding anatomy enable us to address major evolutionary questions. Extant aquatic mammals are large-scale consumers and have key roles as ecosystem engineers.13,35,36 They have likely affected aquatic ecosystems in a similar manner throughout their evolution,37 compelling us to discover how their feeding ecology has changed over time. Here, we quantified a behavioral feeding framework in predatory aquatic mammals using morphofunctional adaptive landscapes to test if feeding strategies that require water will have peaks that are closer together and if taxa follow the optimal evolutionary path between adaptive peaks.
We found H1 to be mostly supported by our morphofunctional adaptive landscape analyses. Adaptive peaks of two of the three feeding strategies (suction filter and ram filter), where water is an essential component, lie very close together and overlap one another. The adaptive peak of raptorial feeding, where water is an encumbrance to efficient feeding, only overlaps with suction feeding (Table 1; Figure 4). In an adaptive landscape context, it should therefore be easier to traverse between the suction, suction filter, and ram filter feeding adaptive peaks (i.e., evolve toward one of these feeding strategies from another) than it is to move from the raptorial feeding adaptive peak toward any of the filter feeding strategy peaks. The sequence of overlapping peaks mirrors that of the behavioral feeding framework,7 providing robust morphofunctional support for it.
Functionally, raptorial feeders are optimized to have relatively stiff, strong jaws which are used to snap at and restrain prey items, as seen in living river dolphins (e.g., Platanista, Inia) and taken to the extreme in fossil hyperlongirostrine taxa (e.g., Schizodelphis or Xiphiacetus38). However, in the feeding strategies where water becomes important, the emphasis changes to having a large oral volume. In both filter feeding strategies, this ability has been exapted for the engulfment of prey, allowing them to consume larger volumes of prey in each single feeding event. Suction feeders do not require large volumes to initially generate the suction forces to draw prey into the oral cavity, although other non-mandibular features are also important in living species, e.g., rapid, piston-like hyolingual retraction and the size of the oral cavity in relation to the oral commissure.39,40 These non-mandibular features may also explain why ziphiids (beaked whales), who are unquestionably obligate suction feeders, possess a mandibular morphology that is not optimal for suction feeding.39 This phenomenon, where a function can be performed via disparate morphologies, is known as “many-to-one-mapping”41 and can also be seen in raptorial feeders, e.g., longirostrine “snappers” versus shorter-snouted taxa.
One noteworthy effect of this “many-to-one mapping” is that relatively few taxa lie on the actual adaptive peaks of each feeding strategy. The diverse morphologies of these feeding strategies mean adaptive peaks result in most species being far away from the peak itself. But are there other reasons why most taxa lie on the slopes instead? Biological reality is complex, and rarely sorts neatly into categories. An organism can only have a single morphology that cannot simultaneously be optimized for every functional role it fulfills (i.e., the principle of frustration, sensu Marshall42). While intermediate morphologies may not perform optimally for any one function, they may represent the optimal solution in any environment in which an organism experiences competing functional demands.43 Living animals are often behaviorally plastic, and their feeding anatomy reflects this, expressed as a tradeoff that can perform multiple functions well rather than being specialized for any one task. This can be seen in cetaceans8,44,45 (e.g., orcas), pinnipeds11,46 (e.g., leopard seals, harbor seals), and even semi-aquatic mammals47,48 (e.g., otters), where animals can employ different feeding strategies in alternative scenarios. The inherent nature of feeding underwater also means that even raptorial taxa will still be initiating some degree of suction when they open their jaws, or during intraoral transport, without displaying any specialized anatomy to do so.7 This is also reflected by the weighting given to the MBI proxy across all feeding strategies.
Functional tradeoffs are important in a macroevolutionary context. Rather than always being a constraint on phenotypic evolution, tradeoffs can instead be the catalyst for transition, as has recently been demonstrated.1,43,49 If an organism’s environment changes so that a previously underutilized competing function becomes more favored, the change in relative importance can ferry a phenotype across an adaptive valley. Evidence of this is the incidental suction ability of raptorial feeding cetaceans, as the fossil record displays multiple instances where lineages that were ancestrally raptorial feeders evolved to become specialized suction feeders.50,51,52,53,54 Alteration of developmental constraints can also change macroevolutionary trajectories. For cetacean mandibles, the release of functional constraints underlying dental occlusion may have facilitated the evolution of polydonty and the resulting changes in mandibular morphology and feeding behavior.21,55,56,57
We surprisingly found that H2 was rejected. Taxa overwhelmingly do not lie on the Pareto front, and therefore do not follow the optimal evolutionary path between adaptive peaks2,32 (Figure 5). This is unexpected for a behavior that is as acutely tied to fitness as feeding, as selection would be hypothesized to keep jaw morphology as close to the Pareto front as possible.
Low Pareto optimality implies that cetaceans have explored novel mandibular morphologies and functions, caused by new functional drivers or previous functional constraints being removed,19,21 e.g., loss of tooth occlusion as mentioned above. This can result in mandibles being free to explore the morphospace, even under relatively weak selection and with suboptimal morphologies.18,21,43 Alternatively, new functional drivers can arise and lead to decreased optimality. This is likely an important factor in cetaceans, as they are unique among mammals in that their mandible has a dual function as part of their auditory pathway as well as being part of the feeding apparatus.55,58,59,60,61 This takes the form of a massively enlarged mandibular foramen which houses a deposit of specialized acoustic fats that provide a preferential pathway for sound waves to reach the middle ear bones.62 Whilst this is usually associated with extant toothed whales’ ability to echolocate, the enlargement of the mandibular foramen is seen early on in cetacean evolution and was a key component of the improvement of their underwater hearing abilities.63,64,65 Nevertheless, the retention of this feature in echolocating toothed whales has clearly driven the clade to explore regions of morphospace they would likely not have if their mandible were solely dedicated to feeding.
The few taxa that do lie in regions of high optimality tend to be hyperspecialized for their respective feeding strategies, such as Platanista for raptorial feeding, Kogia for suction feeding, and Megaptera for ram filter feeding. The majority of taxa, however, have functional tradeoffs (as discussed above) and lie in regions of lower optimality.
Overall, we find that our results offer strong quantitative support for the behavioral feeding framework proposed by Hocking et al.7,15 (Figure 6). Both behavioral and functional data appear to indicate that when aquatic lineages specialize to feed in water, they follow a “path of least resistance.” Feeding strategies that incorporate water as part of the behavior share a common functional underpinning, and this is expressed in macroevolutionary terms as their adaptive peaks being closer to one another on the adaptive landscape. The incorporation of non-mandibular feeding structures, such as the skull, teeth, hyoids, and baleen, could potentially cause the position of the adaptive peaks to even more closely emulate the sequence described in Hocking et al.,7,15 and future work should aim to incorporate them. Another potentially fruitful line of enquiry would be to create feeding adaptive landscapes for pinniped mandibles; we propose that their monofunctional nature will see their distribution across the adaptive landscape fall much closer to the Pareto fronts between feeding strategies than seen in cetaceans. Other non-mammalian clades could also be analyzed in this framework, e.g., birds and fish, which have been the focus of many previous feeding studies.66,67

Adaptive landscapes offer a promising approach to investigating macroevolutionary patterns of complex behaviors such as feeding. By quantifying the inherent tradeoffs that a phenotype must express, we can gain insights into the behaviors of extinct taxa and better reveal the transitions between feeding strategies, features that are unlikely to be preserved in the fossil record. Our analyses show how novel morphological innovations can shift evolutionary trajectories from optimal paths, yet still result in highly diverse and successful animal lineages.

## Limitations of the study
The three-dimensional shape of the cetacean mandible was captured in detail for this study. However, even more comprehensive landmarking schemes may reveal different functional signals. This is especially true for the theoretical morphologies, as they rely on the landmarks derived from the morphospace. As with almost all studies, a more extensive sample size would be preferable, particularly a greater sampling of suction filter feeders. However, this requires sufficiently preserved fossils to be found.

## Experimental model and study participant details

## Data sample
Mandibles were chosen as they are the primary skeletal element used in feeding. Mandibles from a sample of fossil and extant cetacean taxa (n = 107, 29 fossil, 78 extant; 8 archaeocete, 8 mysticete, 91 odontocete) were used in analyses. Each species was assigned to one of the six feeding strategies from two recent papers7,15: terrestrial, semi-aquatic, raptorial, suction, suction filter, ram filter. Cetaceans were used in this study as they cover multiple feeding categories with their varied feeding ecologies. Intraspecific variation in mandibular morphology was not considered a factor as it is expected to be less than interspecific variation, as has been shown for other parts of the cetacean skeleton,74 and was not the focus of this study. Designations of feeding strategy were made based on the literature, in particular7,15,75 and sources within. Three-dimensional models of the mandibles were collated, primarily from previous studies55,76 as well as the online repositories MorphoSource (www.morphosource.org/) and Sketchfab (www.sketchfab.com/). For scans not available on these sites please contact the corresponding museum curator. Meshes were cleaned and decimated to 1,500,000 faces in Geomagic Wrap68 (v2021), unless the number of faces was already below this threshold. This process reduces computational demand but does not compromise on scan detail and quality. See Table S1 for a list of specimens used.

## Method details

## Geometric morphometrics
Mandible shape was quantified using a landmark scheme that included both single point fixed landmarks and sliding semilandmark curves (12 fixed landmarks, 24 curves, 546 landmarks total). Each specimen was landmarked using Stratovan Checkpoint69 (v2024.03.07). See Table S2 for a list of landmarks. We then conducted a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on Procrustes-transformed landmark coordinates to show shape variation across the dataset. The resultant morphospace was used in the creation of the adaptive landscapes. Analyses were conducted in R70 (v4.3.1) using the geomorph package71,77 (v.4.0.7). PCA may be conducted on all specimens (extant and fossil) to produce a total morphospace, or alternatively only on extant specimens with fossils being projected into the space a posteriori.32 Here, we conducted a total morphospace analysis because it considers morphologies and functions outside the range of extant variation, thereby capturing the distinctive aspects of the fossil sample that is overlooked in an a posteriori analysis.

## Functional traits
To fully sample shape variation, in addition to the actual taxa sampled, 30 theoretical three-dimensional shape warps were generated in a 6 × 5 grid across the first two principal component axes of the morphospace, respectively. The shape warp landmark configurations were made into surface meshes using the ‘mesh2ply’ function of the R package Morpho.72 For each of the 107 cetacean taxa and the 30 theoretical shape warps, six functional traits were measured: 1) total mandibular length; 2) relative body length; 3) relative symphyseal length; 4) mandibular bluntness index; 5) opening ability; and 6) oral volume. See ESM for measurements. Whilst feeding performance could be influenced by other functional traits, all the measurements can be repeatedly and reliably measured and have been used in previous studies, and overall capture the range of functionality required to examine the highly variable feeding behaviors of cetaceans.
Snap proxy: this proxy helps to differentiate between different types of raptorial feeding. It is calculated as the symphysis length divided by the body length of the mandible (i.e., the non-ramus component). Taxa that are specialised snappers e.g., river dolphins will have similar lengths for the symphysis lengths and have larger proxy values, whereas more generalist raptorial feeders, e.g., dolphins, will have a shorter symphysis relative to the mandibular body length.
Relative body length: this proxy is the proportion of the mandible that is dedicated to the feeding apparatus. It is calculated as the inverse of the relative proportion of the mandible that is made up by the ramus (which itself is calculated as ramus length divided by mandibular length). This is used instead of tooth row length in this study due to multiple taxa being edentulous.
Relative symphyseal length: During feeding, the mandibular symphysis undergoes significant stresses. The relative length of the symphysis is an important characteristic for determining the jaw’s mechanical response to biting, shaking and twisting, and it has been demonstrated that the behavior of the symphysis under loading conditions can be accurately predicted by linear measurements.78,79 The diversity of mandibular symphysis morphology is extensive in cetaceans, ranging from unfused symphyses in mysticetes,80 to extensive sutural contacts extending over 30–70% of the jaw’s length, seen convergently in multiple clades, including platanistids, eurhinodelphinids and physeterids.55 This character was measured as the longest dimension of the symphysis at the point of union between the two rami. This value was then divided by the total length of the jaw to produce a ratio of symphysis length/jaw length.
Mandibular bluntness index (MBI): this index is taken from Werth,39 and is mandibular width (measured as distance between lateral edges of mandibular condyles) divided by mandibular length. In every specimen measured the lower jaw is longer than it is wide, so that width divided by length provides a unitless number <1.
Opening ability: this proxy is the height of the mandible at the coronoid process divided by the relative proportion of the mandible that is made up by the ramus (calculated itself as ramus length divided by mandibular length). Some taxa require a greater angular gape (e.g., mysticetes when lunge feeding) or to open their mouths rapidly (to generate large suction forces). They can achieve this by having a smaller proportion of the mandible being the ramus. Higher values of this proxy correlate with a rapid opening speed and large gape.
Oral volume: This proxy is the proportion of the width of the jaw at the midlength point to the maximum width of the jaw. A key feature of filter feeding cetaceans is the increased ability to engulf large amounts of water containing prey items in a single gulp. To increase oral volume, taxa should maintain a wider jaw for a greater proportion of their length, and therefore have midlength widths that are a larger proportion of their maximum width.

## Quantification and statistical analysis

## Performance surfaces
Performance surfaces were then created using the R package Morphoscape.73 For each of the six functional traits measured, values were standardised by scaling them to range between 0 and 1, with 0 representing lowest measured performance, and 1 representing highest measured performance. Functional traits measured on the theoretical mandible models were mapped onto the morphospace using ordinary Kriging to interpolate trait values across the space, with the location of 1 and 0 on the performance surface being the position of the best and worst performing theoretical morphologies, respectively, in the morphospace. This resulted in six unique performance surfaces, showing high-level performance gradients and form-function relationships (Figure 3).

## Adaptive landscapes
Adaptive landscapes were created using the R package Morphoscape.73 For each feeding strategy performed by cetaceans, landscapes were calculated by iteratively summing the six performance surfaces, where each one had their contribution to overall performance weighted. All possible combinations of weights were tested, ranging from 0 to 1 in increments of 0.1, which for six functional traits resulted in a total of 3003 possible adaptive landscapes. The top 5% of these landscapes were then used to calculate the final landscapes.

## Pareto landscapes
Lastly, we determined the optimal evolutionary paths through the morphospace between feeding strategies. This was done by constructing several Pareto landscapes. Each Pareto landscape was calculated based on combining two adaptive landscapes for each feeding strategy: raptorial, suction, suction filter and ram filter. Each point in a landscape is a trade-off between the different performance traits, and can therefore be considered as solutions to an optimisation problem. A subset of these will be Pareto optimal, where no other solution has a better or equal performance. To generate the Pareto landscapes, these solutions on all landscapes are placed in a Pareto ranking system where the optimal subset of solutions is assigned rank 1, and then removed from the list of solutions before a second optimal subset (assigned rank 2) is determined, with the process continuing until all solutions have been ranked. Optimality was ranked using a modified Goldberg ranking system, the Pareto rank-ratio approach,19,81 first calculating the optimal ranking (RO) and then ranking again with the optimality of each metric reversed (suboptimal ranking, RS).
