Abstract
a) The Landscape of Fear (LOF) concept proposes that a prey individual’s perceived risk of predation can affect their distribution and
activity. Prey may perceive the risk of predation following the detection of predator cues, such as auditory, visual, and chemical signals;
this has been demonstrated in numerous studies.
b) There is limited understanding on the extent of the role that chemical predator cues play under the LOF concept. Within this study, a
range of olfactory cues, including those of native and invasive predators, were applied to artificial feeders used by eastern grey
squirrels (Sciurus carolinensis) in a single urban site in the United Kingdom. Motion- activated cameras recorded squirrel feeding and
vigilance behaviours. The durations of such behaviours were collated and analysed to determine whether the simulated presence of
predators using affected squirrel foraging.
c) Domestic cat (Felis catus) odours significantly reduced proportional squirrel feeding duration at feeders, compared to passive and
active controls (distilled water and rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus domesticus) urine, respectively). Pine marten (Martes martes) faeces
significantly reduced proportional squirrel feeding duration compared to feeding under passive control applications.(Werner and Anholt
1993; Maynard-Smith et al. 2003; Scott-Phillips 2008)
d) Our results confirm previous knowledge on the LOF theory; olfactory cues of a predator disrupt prey foraging. These observations
provide an insight into how predator scents can affect the distribution, feeding, and anti-predation behaviours of prey.
e) Our results are discussed in the context of the following areas: the application of predator scents as a deterrent of domestic cats in
an urban environment; the wider effects of pine marten reintroductions on introduced eastern grey squirrel populations; the
evolutionary significance of predator chemical cues.

Introduction
Predation is a strong evolutionary force acting upon multiple species traits including size, morphology, colouration, and behaviour
(Werner et al. 1993; Maynard-Smith & Harper 2003; Scott-Phillips 2008). These traits ultimately predict the distribution, composition,
size, and ecological function of a population or community (Hall et al. 1976; Kneitel and Chase 2004; Beauchamp et al. 2007; Hawlena
and Schmitz 2010; Letnic et al. 2012). At the individual level, predation can result in reduced growth, fecundity, foraging efficiency,
survival, and ultimate fitness of prey (Sih 1980; Lima and Dill 1990; Peckarsky et al. 1993; Abrams et al. 1996; Peacor and Werner 1997;
Zanette et al. 2011).
Mammalian predators frequently use chemical scents to transmit information about sexual condition, group membership, social
status, and territory ownership (Sankar and Archunan 2008; Gelperin 2008), as well as optimising the use of food caches (Henry 1977),
the defence of food resources (Piñeiro and Barja 2015), and for active defence (Medill et al. 2011). In the case of territory ownership,
scent cues enable territorial boundaries to be delineated without the need for direct contact between conspecifics, thus reducing the
risk of conflict and injury (Relyea 2003; Kusch et al. 2004; Ferrari et al. 2006; Hawkins et al. 2007; Smith et al. 2008). In these contexts,
scent markers are used actively and deliberately. In addition to deliberate scent cues, some are left inadvertently by individuals as they
commute, often in the form of skin cells and/or fur (dander) (Apfelbach et al. 2005). These scents, as well as those associated with
urine, faeces, and excretions from scent glands (Burger 2005), contain a complex mixture of compounds that vary in their molecular
weight and can vary over time as the different compounds aerosolise at different rates (Riffell et al. 2008; McFrederick et al. 2009).
Consequently, conspecifics are likely able to use the composition of these chemical cues to indicate the time since their deposition and
hence the likely presence or absence of an individual (Wyatt 2014). Prey species consider the presence of predator chemical cues when
choosing habitats (Turley and Findlay 2009; Ferrero et al. 2011). Upon detection of chemicals associated with predators, known as
kairomones (Schoeppner & Relyea 2009), prey may lower activity and feeding levels or increase the use of refugia to lessen the
chances of an encounter (Mathis 2003; Foam et al. 2005; Šmejkal et al. 2018). The extent of a prey individual’s response to the
detection of kairomones may be relative to the predator species detected (Schoeppner and Relyea 2009). The presence of predators
creates a ‘landscape of fear’ (LOF), within which prey species ‘fear’ one or more predatory species and thus avoid regions deemed highrisk (Kohl et al. 2018).
Avoidance may have wider ecological impacts; for example, increased vegetation and crop growth in areas that are avoided by prey
species (Manning et al. 2009). Crops have been protected from African elephants (Loxodonta africana) by using active African
beehives (Apis mellifer scutellata) (Vollrath and Douglas-Hamilton 2002; King et al. 2007, 2009). Furthermore, LOFs could be used to
Page 2/14

inform pest management plans that focus on areas considered by prey individuals to be of low predation risk, with research showing
that damage to trees from rodent pests reduced in the presence of predatory odours (Sullivan et al. 1988; Krijger et al. 2017). Thus, a
LOF could be induced artificially with the presence of predatory odours to reduce the settlement and persistence of problematic prey
species. Such an intervention may prove useful in invasive species management.
Since its introduction to Britain more than 140 years ago, the range of eastern grey squirrels (Sciurus carolinensis) has increased
substantially (Gurnell et al. 2009). The expansion of grey squirrels is often associated with the decline of the native Eurasian red
squirrel (Sciurus vulgaris), through a range of factors including the transmission of squirrel poxvirus (SQPV) (Sainsbury et al. 2000;
Gurnell et al. 2012), superior foraging efficiency (Mazzamuto et al. 2016), behavioural dominance (Gurnell et al. 2004), and forest cover
(Gurnell et al. 2014). Currently, grey squirrel management may be achieved through trapping and shooting (BASC 2016). These
methods may effectively remove individuals but are unlikely to significantly reduce populations. Other methods, including
immunocontraception and gene editing, are being pursued with little evidence of successful application thus far.
Grey squirrel management may be achieved through reintroducing native predators that have since been extirpated. Pine martens
(Martes martes), which have been confined to Scotland and Ireland since the late 1800s and 1900s due to severe persecution and
habitat loss (Langley and Yalden 1977; Birks et al. 2005; Couzens et al. 2017), have recently been introduced to receptor sites in
England and Wales (Vincent Wildlife Trust 2022). Though considered predators of both red and grey squirrels, pine martens potentially
predate upon greys more than reds and their presence is negatively correlated with grey squirrel presence (Sheehy and Lawton 2014;
Sheehy et al. 2018). Recovering pine marten numbers have also been linked to a decline of grey squirrels in Ireland (Sheehy and
Lawton 2014). Consequently, there is interest in how the spatial distribution patterns of grey squirrels may change over time as pine
marten populations increase and expand, and how this might encourage the recovery of red squirrels in England and Wales.
Though there are numerous studies demonstrating the effects a LOF has upon prey behaviour, little is known on the concept in grey
squirrel populations. This study aimed to investigate whether grey squirrel activity at artificial feeders was reduced under the simulated
presence of (a) domestic cats (Felis catus), an invasive predator, and (b) pine martens, a native predator, through the application of
chemical cues. The behavioural response of grey squirrels under the perceived presence of these predators, and any discrepancies
between the duration or extent to which these behaviours are displayed, may shed light on the LOF concept and its potential
applications in invasive species management.
H 0 = Eastern grey squirrels would show no significant alteration in their observed foraging behaviour in the presence of predator
olfactory cues, compared to control cues.
H 1 = Eastern grey squirrels would significantly reduce foraging efforts in the presence of predator olfactory cues, compared to control
cues.

Materials And Methods

Study location
The study was conducted from July to November 2019. The data were collected at the University of Reading’s Whiteknights Campus,
Berkshire, UK. The campus covers an area of approximately 130 ha and consists of a combination of amenity grassland, native and
introduced shrub, mixed woodland, cultivated land, and buildings. Data were collected at 18 feeding stations placed around the
campus within a variety of habitat types (Appendix 1).

Study design
Each individual feeding station consisted of a commercial squirrel feeder and a trail camera. Feeders measured 190 x 210 x 240 mm,
with a perching platform of 150 x 150 mm in front of a food reservoir (Appendix 2). The internal dimensions of the feeder measured
approximately 130 x 100 x 100 mm. Transparent, plastic screens covered the front of the feeders. A Petri dish was placed at the base
of the feeder containing one of four cue samples; this was separated from 175 g of unsalted peanuts by a perforated wooden platform
to allow detection of chemical cues (86 x 90 x 5 mm (Appendix 2). Squirrels could access the peanuts by lifting the lid of the feeder.
Feeders were mounted to a tree with a motion sensitive Campark Wildlife Trail MP 1080p (Campark Electronics Corporation Limited,
Hong Kong) camera trap set up directly opposite (≤ 5 m away). A Bushnell Essential E3 Trophy Cam HD (Bushnell Corporation, United
Page 3/14

States) was used at one location due to a technical fault with a single Campark camera. Camera traps were set to record video for 180
seconds uninterruptedly, when activated by any motion with a 30 second interval between recordings. Each feeding station was left for
a minimum of 20 hours each day. The average distance between the squirrel feeders was 300 m (SE 30 m).
The Petri dish in each feeder contained 15 mL of either a passive control (distilled water), an active control (domestic rabbit urine), or
an experimental treatment (experiment 1: domestic cat urine, experiment 2: pine marten faeces solution). Rabbit urine, used as an
active control since rabbits are neither the prey of or the predator of grey squirrels, was collected from a healthy female captive rabbit
in Reading, UK. A total of 45 mL of cat urine were collected from a healthy female pet cat in Reading, UK using non-absorbent cat litter.
The urine was diluted with 315 mL of distilled water. A 421 g sample of pine marten faeces was provided by the British Wildlife Centre
(Surrey, UK). This sample was mixed with 1 L distilled water and sealed for 48 hours, resulting in 900 mL of a liquid faeces solution.
Cue samples were applied in four-day ‘replicates’, where each feeder was subject to three treatment periods with interval periods
providing a 'recovery' for squirrels to resume regular foraging. During each interval, food was replenished daily but cue samples were
removed and cameras did not record. In an attempt to account for carryover effects, where remnants of the preceding treatment may
contaminate the proceeding treatment, the specific ordering of the cue treatment was altered after the cat urine experiments. Table 1
and Table 2 illustrate the design of the cat urine and pine marten faeces experiments, respectively.

Behaviour observations
Observations were collated from the camera trap video analysis using visual and/or ImageJ software (Schneider et al. 2012). Our
behaviour analyses took inspiration from previous studies on squirrel behaviour (e.g. Jayne et al. 2015). The durations of the following
squirrel behaviours were recorded for each 180 second video recording: a) active feeding and foraging, b) vigilance or scanning the
environment, and c) activity in proximity to (but not directly on) the feeder. Feeding activity was defined as an individual squirrel
feeding on peanuts upon the feeder platform or while their head is inside the feeder. Scanning was defined as an individual facing
outwards from the feeder towards the environment without attempting to access or feed upon peanuts. Proximity behaviour was
judged based on an individual’s position, either stationary upon or below the tree where a feeder is mounted, investigating the feeder
while not upon it. Running and climbing along the tree to and from the feeder were not included in the behaviour.
The durations were summed, per behaviour type, for each 180 second video. The total presence of squirrel(s) was calculated, by
summing the durations of all behaviour types, for each 180 second video. Thus, the following values were obtained from each video:
total squirrel presence, where the presence of multiple squirrels within a single video would yield 180s total presence; total feeding
time; total scanning time; total proximity time. Appendix 3 provides illustrations of each behaviour type recorded. Researchers visited
each camera at the end of each recording day to collect the footage and replenish batteries. All videos captured were stored according
to the camera and recording day.
The behaviour durations were averaged across all videos recorded by a single camera during a treatment period. To reduce the effect
of external factors, such as human interference, we averaged the duration values across each camera within the same treatment
group. For instance, all videos recorded by cameras in Group 1 (Table 1) would yield four datapoints, each of which an average of total
presence, feeding, scanning, and proximity durations. Using these average values, we then calculated the proportion of total presence
duration that squirrels spent feeding.

Statistical analysis:
A series of repeated-measures ANOVAs were used to analyse the behaviour duration data obtained from both experiments, with
treatment type as a predictor of behaviour duration as the response variable. Climatological data, including average temperature and
wind speed, were collected from the Meteorological department, University of Reading (Reading University Atmospheric Observatory
Page 5/14

2020). Three climatological factors, temperature, scent, and wind speed were used in general linear mixed models (GLMMs), along
with the experimental period, using Poisson distribution (for count data) and the camera used as a fixed effect variable. We used the
lme4 package (version 1.0.2.1) of R software (version 4.2.1) for model evaluation and selection of the best model, based on AIC
(Akaike Information Criteria) values. We assessed different models based on the AIC value and experimental and marginal R2 values
(Table 3). The total feeding time as a proportion of total squirrel presence under different treatments was plotted using the gglpot2
package in R.

Results

Cat urine experiments
Feeding, scanning, proximity, nor total presence significantly differed across the three replicates for the passive control and active
control groups. Feeding duration decreased once cat urine cues were introduced for the experimental group (F2,10 = 16.221, P = 0.001).
Scanning and proximity durations did not significantly differ between the three replicates for the experimental treatment group, but
total squirrel presence decreased once urine cues were introduced.
Duration of behaviours differed between feeders of the same treatment group. Feeding duration differed between feeding stations
under passive control (N = 6, F1,5 = 16.222, P = 0.010), active control (N = 6, F1,5 = 11.926, P = 0.018), and experimental (N = 6, F1,5 =
24.293, P = 0.004) treatment groups. Scanning duration differed among feeding stations of the passive control (N = 6, F1,5 = 18.713, P
= 0.008), active control (N = 6, F1,5 = 17.266, P = 0.009), and experimental (N = 6, F1,5 = 37.526, P = 0.002) groups. Proximity durations
among feeding stations of the passive control (N = 6, F1,5 = 8.830, P = 0.031) and experimental (N = 6, F1,5 = 13.256, P = 0.015) groups.
Total presence differed between the feeding stations of the passive control (N = 6, F1,5 = 18.398, P = 0.008), active control (N = 6, F1,5 =
13.606, P = 0.014), and experimental (N = 6, F1,5 = 28.920, P = 0.003) groups.

The deterrence effects created were consistent across seasons; while under predator cue applications, compared to the passive
controls, squirrel feeding was reduced in both summer (cat experiments) and winter (pine marten experiments) periods. The GLMM
with temperature, scent, wind speed, and experimental period as factors revealed that the best predictive variable associated with

Discussion
Invasive species pose a threat to biodiversity in Europe (Edelman and Koprowski 2006, 2009). Among other factors, introduced grey
squirrels outcompete Eurasian red squirrels through disease-mediated competition in the UK; red squirrel populations decline 25 times
faster in the presence of SQPV compared to co-existence with grey squirrels without SQPV (Rushton et al. 2006). Grey squirrels also
compete with garden birds for food (Bonnington et al. 2014), posing a major concern for conservation. The amount of time grey
squirrels spent feeding decreased under the presence of cat urine and accounted for only 6.6% of the total feeding time. In response to
pine marten faeces cues, feeding time accounted for 24.9% of the total time present at feeders. It is noteworthy that feeding durations
under pine marten faeces applications were not significantly different to feeding durations under an active control application.
Nonetheless, the observed results align with previous findings obtained in controlled experiments, which demonstrate the use of
predator chemical cues as a prey deterrent (Kats and Dill 1998; Dielenberg et al. 2001; Mahlaba et al. 2017).
Urban areas are associated with a number of risks for semi-cursorial species such as the grey squirrel, most notably a high volume of
road traffic but also a high density of free-ranging pet cats. As pet cats receive all or most of their nutritional requirements from their
owners (Baker et al. 2005), cat density substantially exceeds the carrying capacity of the environment; UK studies have recorded urban
cat densities ranging from 200 to > 1000 cats per km2 (Baker et al. 2005, 2008; Sims et al. 2008; Thomas et al. 2014). Despite being
well-fed, many pet cats kill wild mammals and birds, including squirrels (Woods et al. 2003); in fact, some individual cats become very
adept at taking squirrels (Baker et al. 2005; Sheehy and Lawton 2014). The 60% reduction in time squirrels spent feeding while cat
urine deterrents were applied may be associated to the predatory threat that pet cats pose. Thus, pet cats, or at least their simulated
presence, may pose an effective grey squirrel deterrent. The 60% reduction in feeding time during the cat urine experiments exceeded
that of the pine marten experiments. Pet cats are already ubiquitous and hyper-abundant in the urban centres of Britain, thus they may
pose a notably larger predatory threat to grey squirrels in the UK. The significant reduction in feeding duration during the cat urine
applications may, therefore, be explained by the likelihood of previous exposure to this predator.
Grey squirrels in Reading are less likely to have encountered pine martens or their simulated presence previously, considering the
scarce distribution of pine martens in the UK (Birks et al. 2005), compared to pet cats. However, the simulated pine marten presence
Page 7/14

resulted in a significant reduction in grey squirrel feeding, albeit less so than during cat urine applications. This result stipulates
carnivore cues are universally avoided by prey species due to the presence of kairomones (Mathis 2003; Foam et al. 2005; Šmejkal et
al. 2018). The discrepancy between feeding duration reductions, under cat urine applications compared to pine marten faeces, may be
explained by species-specific responses; anti-predatory displays may differ depending on the predatory species, and their kairomones,
present (Turner et al. 1999). Furthermore, avoidance or defensive displays are less likely to be employed under the simulated presence
of ‘low-risk’ predators (McCarthy and Fisher 2000; Relyea 2001). If previous exposure is considered a measure of predation risk, pine
martens could be considered ‘low-risk’ predators of grey squirrels and account for the less significant, compared to that of cats,
reduction in feeding time. The significant reduction in feeding time, under exposure to pine marten cues, may contradict studies in
which prey species did not display defensive behaviours against a predator they have not experienced previously (Wanger et al. 2011;
Twining et al. 2020). Overall, the simulated presence of domestic cats and pine martens may be applied to deter grey squirrels and
perhaps adapted to deter other mammalian pests. However, further investigation could distinguish between the efficacy of the
simulated presence of a known predator against a predator unlikely to have been encountered.

The use of predator cues in pest management
Primarily, homeowners attempt to alleviate detrimental behaviours upon the arrival of grey squirrels, rather than attempt to create an
area that grey squirrels would tend to avoid and seldom visit (RSPB 2020). The application of predator cues, such as those deployed in
this study, may prove a useful technique for deterring the initial visit of grey squirrels in the first place, thus preventing detrimental
behaviours. Deterrence using predatory cues could alleviate the need for chemical pest control, which is often associated with
detrimental impacts such as poor welfare and exposure to non-target species (Krijger et al. 2017); the use of ecologically-based rodent
management (EBRM) practices, which incorporate behavioural ecology concepts similar to the LOF, is increasing (Hone 1994; Krijger et
al. 2017). However, the application of predator cues requires further research and is unlikely to be effective across all species. Some
natural pest populations may not exhibit similar avoidance behaviours; montane voles (Microtus montanus) nor meadow voles
(Microtus pennsylvanicus) significantly reduced foraging upon apple trees after treatment with red fox (Vulpes vulpes) urine (Sullivan
et al. 1988). The nature of a predator-prey relationship may also affect the extent of avoidance behaviours exhibited; an arboreal
predator is likely to trigger more defensive behaviours in grey squirrels than predators without such abilities (Makin et al. 2012; Krijger
et al. 2017). The ability of prey species to detect minute predator odours, determined by the number or type of trace amine-associated
receptors (TAARs) in rodent prey, will also affect the extent of avoidance responses and should be considered in potential applications
of a scent-based deterrent (Borowsky et al. 2001; Ferrero et al. 2011).
Current UK guideline approved codes of practice for the use of pesticide justify lethal control where pest damage is extensive (Mayle,
2007). The use of a simulated predator presence as a grey squirrel deterrent, perhaps in combination with lethal control, could reduce
populations that are problematic for homeowners or for the recovery of red squirrels. Where problematic individuals persist, for
instance where garden bird foraging is disturbed, the application of a commercialized cat urine sample may be a cost-effective method
of deterrence compared to lethal measures. However, the effects of such a product on the desired species, such as garden birds, should
be considered.

Grey squirrel management through pine marten reintroduction
Pine martens are largely absent from mainland UK and are predominantly found in rural areas and woodland habitats, especially more
remote and ancient woodlands (Balestrieri et al. 2019; Twining et al. 2020). Thus, the reintroduction of this carnivore in Southern
England may become a logistical challenge dependent on the availability of suitable receptor sites (IUCN 2018, 2021) and societal
acceptance (Ripple and Beschta 2012). Evidence to support pine-marten reintroduction as a means of grey squirrel management is
mounting; red squirrel vigilance increases after exposure to pine marten scent cues (Twining et al. 2020) and may allude to their
decreased susceptibility to pine marten predation. Our results indicate that the simulated presence of pine martens can effectively
reduce squirrel feeding durations. As such, the grey squirrels may have commuted further to foraging sites without a simulated
predator presence, thus increasing energy expenditure, creating a less favourable feeding trade-off, and eventually reducing individual
or evolutionary fitness. McNicol et al. 2020 found male squirrels significantly increased daily range coverage and distance travelled as
the exposure to pine martens increase. Eighteen pine martens were successfully reintroduced to Gloucestershire, England in 2019 (The
Wildlife Trusts 2019). Should future pine marten reintroductions continue to prove successful, pine marten populations may offer
effective grey squirrel deterrence and eventual decline, promoting the recovery of native red squirrel populations. If pine martens are
considered to prey upon grey squirrels more so than red squirrels (Sheehy and Lawton 2014; Sheehy et al. 2018), their reintroduction
may enable red squirrel recovery. Aside from direct predation, grey squirrel populations may decline under a LOF induced by the cues of
Page 8/14

reintroduced pine martens via (1) increased vigilance behaviour under the presence or pseudo-presence of predators, reducing feeding
durations and individual fitness, or (2) the absence of predator evasion behaviours towards pine marten cues resulting in inflated
predation rates.
The methodology employed for this study may be improved in future studies. Since the results were obtained from a natural
landscape, we could not account for the presence of other species. It is plausible that reductions in foraging efforts may have been
responses to the presence of another predator (e.g. a red fox). Grey squirrels may have exploited the calls of other species, such as
birds, to determine localized predation threat (Crowley et al. 2018). Grey squirrels use both alarm calls and non-alarm calls from birds
to determine the presence or absence of a predator (Lilly et al. 2019) and, therefore, may have reduced foraging efforts in response to
these calls rather than the presence of chemical cues. Secondly, we could not account for the composition of the chemical cues. Prey
species may respond differently to urine from predators fed different diets (Apfelbach et al. 2005). The diets of domestic cats in the UK
range from food provided by their owner (e.g. house cats) to those that also catch additional items such as rodents and birds (e.g. freeranging cats) (Kays and DeWan 2004; Baker et al. 2008). If predator cues were to be developed as a grey squirrel deterrent, the diet of
the predator used would likely need to be controlled.
Reductions in feeding duration may be due to contamination of the food source (Kats and Dill 1998); this may explain the reduction,
albeit insignificant, in feeding duration from passive control to active control. Pine marten samples were difficult to obtain since only
captive populations can be accessed locally (Birks 2017). Faeces samples elicit strong responses in terms of avoidance and reduction
in feeding time; similar behaviour was classified in black rats in a laboratory scenario, compared to a solution containing red fox urine
(Burwash et al. 1998). Aside from the differential chemical potency and extent of the deterrence, faeces may cause increased
contamination with food sources, remnant faecal matter, and possibility of infection or parasitism. The latter was observed in studies
on Taiwan field mice (Apodemus semotus), where contamination of food patches by faeces occurred more than urine-contaminated
patches (Hou et al. 2016). Pine marten faeces has been applied via a spray solution to the external parts of feeders (Twining et al.
2020), a method likely resulting in less contamination of food sources compared to petri dishes inside a feeder. This method could be
adopted in similar studies.
The apparent absence of scanning behaviour in the cat or pine marten experiments may be attributed to the lack of highly threatening
stimuli. Further, odor cues may not indicate immediate predator presence but rather occupancy; laboratory rats (Rattus norvegicus

domesticus) showed reduced grooming and exploration behaviours when cat urine was present, while hiding and vigilant exploration
behaviours increased (Dielenberg et al. 2001). Finally, the use of field cameras often provides limitations in a study’s methodology
(Martin and Bateson 2007), including a limited field of view and observer bias when analysing and quantifying defensive behaviours.

Conclusion
Few studies have explored grey squirrel anti-predator behaviours in response to predators, particularly using scent cues as opposed to
other forms. Chemical cues are essential in mammalian predator-prey relationships and should therefore be considered to manage
grey squirrels in the UK. This study found that grey squirrels exhibit at least one anti- predator behaviour (reduction of time spent
feeding) in response to the simulated presence of novel (pine martens) and known predators (domestic cats). Thus, our findings that
the simulated presence of domestic cats and pine martens elicits a change in foraging behaviour may have important applications to
the management of grey squirrels in urban areas, providing deterrents become commercially available. However, the application of
such a product would require further research; for instance, it should be established at which stage of dissipation the cue achieves a
negligible deterrence effect.
