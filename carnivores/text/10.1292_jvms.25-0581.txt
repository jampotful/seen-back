# Localized oral histiocytic sarcoma in a leopard gecko (Eublepharis macularius)

## Abstract
A two-year-old female leopard gecko presented with a mass in the oral cavity with apparent exophthalmos. Despite medical management, the animal died. Grossly, the firm and tan to white mass extended from the oropharyngeal region to the external surface overlying the cornea of the left eye. Histologically, the mass was a poorly-demarcated, densely cellular, invasive neoplasm. The neoplasm was composed of solid sheets and interlacing bundles of round, polygonal, and short spindle-shaped cells. Immunohistochemically, the neoplastic cells were positive for Iba-1 and CD204. These findings were consistent with a localized histiocytic sarcoma. This is the first documentation of the gross, histopathological, and immunohistochemical features of localized histiocytic sarcoma in a leopard gecko.
The leopard gecko (Eublepharis macularius) is a terrestrial, crepuscular, insectivorous lizard native to Pakistan, Iran, Afghanistan, Nepal, and India [3]. A distinctive feature of this species is the presence of movable eyelids, whereas most geckos lack eyelids and instead have a transparent spectacle [3, 6]. The leopard gecko is a popular pet species due to its diverse color morphs [3]. It also has a long lifespan, with several individuals reported to live for more than 20 years [3]. As common diseases in this species, ophthalmic disorders, iridophoroma in the Lemon Frost morph, and cholesterol granuloma with apolipoprotein E–derived amyloid deposition have been reported [5, 9, 21]. As captive reptiles live longer, neoplastic diseases are being increasingly recognized. In a retrospective study, the prevalence of neoplasia in lizards, including geckos, has been reported to be 8.5%, second only to that in snakes [4]. However, the incidence of neoplasms varies among reptile species, with leopard geckos showing a significantly lower incidence than bearded dragons and chameleons [12].
Histiocytic sarcoma is a rare neoplasm in reptiles [13]. Most cases have been reported only in retrospective surveys without detailed information about the individuals [4, 7, 13, 14, 20]. Even among these articles, there have been no reports of histiocytic sarcoma in geckos (infraorder Gekkota). Existing detailed documentation of reptilian histiocytic sarcoma is limited to the pathological characteristics of retrobulbar histiocytic sarcoma in a central bearded dragon [8]. To expand the knowledge of reptilian neoplasms and to contribute to comparative pathology, the detailed pathological description of localized histiocytic sarcoma in a leopard gecko is presented.
The animal was a two-year-old female leopard gecko, weighing 39.5 g and measuring 20 cm in total length, identified as a Super Mack Snow Albino Leucistic color morph. Radiographs revealed a relatively homogeneous soft-tissue opacity mass on the left side of the head, involving the oral cavity to the pharyngeal region (Fig. 1b). Since the mass continued to increase in size following presentation at the clinic, a partial incision was made from the eyelid region under anesthesia for histopathological examination. A subsequent histopathological examination resulted in a diagnosis of sarcoma, although the authors could not review the slide because the diagnosis was made at another laboratory. Since the owner did not wish to pursue further surgical treatment, medical management was elected; however, the animal died 40 days after the initial visit. The whole body was fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and was submitted to the Laboratory of Veterinary Pathology at Nihon University, where a necropsy was performed. Grossly, the tan to white, firm mass was observed in the oropharyngeal region, measuring 1.6 × 1.5 × 1.2 cm(Fig. 1c). The corneal surface of the left eye was dried, with reddish-brown discoloration. The liver was diffusely ochre-colored and slightly friable. Postmortem radiography did not clearly show osteolysis of skull or mandible, although the mass was larger than that seen on the antemortem radiograph (Fig. 1d). No significant gross lesions were noted in other organs. Complete blood count (CBC) and serum biochemistry were not performed in this leopard gecko because restraint and venipuncture can induce marked stress to trigger tail autotomy. Representative tissue samples were trimmed, embedded in paraffin, cut into 5-μm-thick sections, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Immunohistochemical staining was performed on sections using routine methods. Scale bar=50 μm.). The neoplasm exhibited an outward extension from the left lower eyelid to overlie the surface of the left cornea, without invading the corneal tissue (Supplementary Fig. 1). No neoplastic tissue was observed in the retrobulbar tissues or within the globe, and the neoplastic cells did not invade the skull or mandible. These findings were consistent with apparent exophthalmos. The neoplasm was composed of solid sheets and interlacing bundles of round, polygonal, and short spindle-shaped cells (Fig. 2b). The neoplastic cells had distinct or indistinct cell borders depending on their cellular characteristics, with minimal to moderate amounts of eosinophilic, foamy cytoplasm. The nuclei were round to oval with coarsely stippled chromatin and prominent nucleoli. Moderate anisocytosis and anisokaryosis were observed, with a total of 18 mitoses identified in 2.37 mm2. There were numerous, large, bizarre mononuclear or multinucleate giant cells. Phagocytosis of cellular debris or erythrocytes was occasionally observed. Immunohistochemically, more than 90% of the neoplastic cells showed intense positivity for Iba-1 (Fig. 2c). Compared with Iba-1, fewer neoplastic cells were positive for CD204, and the stain intensity was mild to moderate (Fig. 2d). Since the control tissues of the leopard gecko showed no immunoreactivity for E-cadherin, S-100, CD3, CD20, or CD79a, immunoreactivity for these antibodies in the neoplasm could not be evaluated. No metastasis was found in the coelomic organs. Based on these findings, the mass was diagnosed as localized histiocytic sarcoma in a leopard gecko. The reddish-brown, friable tissues on the corneal surface also showed histopathological findings similar to those of the mass. Regarding other significant lesions, hepatocytes were diffusely swollen with vacuolated cytoplasm and peripherally compressed nuclei, consistent with hepatic lipidosis (Supplementary Fig. 2). In addition, in the lamina propria of the esophagus, severe infiltration of lymphocytes and plasma cells was observed.

The differential diagnoses of histiocytic sarcoma include lymphoma, undifferentiated sarcoma, and tumors of muscle such as rhabdomyosarcoma and leiomyosarcoma. Immunohistochemistry is useful for differentiating these neoplasms; however, definitive diagnosis in reptilian tumors can be challenging because of limited antibody cross-reactivity [13]. In the present study, immunohistochemistry for lymphoid markers (CD3, CD20, and CD79a) showed nonspecific, even when normal spleen from this species was used as a positive control, indicating limited antibody cross-reactivity for evaluation. Tumors of muscle were also considered based on the histopathological findings; however, the neoplastic cells showed immunopositivity for histiocytic markers (Iba-1 and CD204), and there are no findings to support muscle lineage. Therefore, immunohistochemical staining for Iba-1 and CD204 was useful for differentiating histiocytic sarcoma from these differential diagnoses in this species. Notably, several reports have documented immunoreactivity for Iba-1 and CD204 in reptilian cells [1, 8, 10]. In mammals, histiocytic sarcoma originates from interstitial dendritic cells, macrophages, and/or Langerhans cells [16]. In reptiles, macrophages, dendritic cells, and Langerhans cells have also been identified as components of the immune system [17]. However, it is challenging to determine the origin of the neoplastic cells based solely on the presence of phagocytosis, since both macrophages and immature dendritic cells are known to exhibit phagocytic activity. In addition, Iba-1 is expressed in both macrophages and dendritic cells, whereas CD204 is expressed in macrophages and shows variable expression in dendritic cells [16]. S-100 can also be expressed by dendritic cells and Langerhans cells in dogs [2]. However, antibody cross-reactivity in this species could not be confirmed; therefore, S-100 did not used to determine of the tumor cell lineage. Given the limited characterization of histiocytic lineage immunophenotypes in reptiles, the use of immunohistochemical staining should be considered an ancillary diagnostic tool.
In leopard geckos, non-neoplastic histiocytic diseases are occasionally reported. Granulomatous inflammation, often associated with bacterial, fungal, and parasitic infections, is relatively common in reptiles, including leopard geckos [19]. In addition, cholesterol granulomas with intralesional apolipoprotein E-derived amyloid deposition have recently been reported as a species-specific lesion [9]. These conditions are characterized by the proliferation of macrophages in response to infectious or degenerative diseases, rather than neoplastic diseases. In this case, the lesion was considered a neoplasm rather than a granuloma because it comprised a densely cellular, relatively monomorphic proliferation of atypical cells with overt cytologic atypia. In addition, the lesion lacked histopathological features of granulomatous inflammation, including nodular aggregates of epithelioid macrophages with central necrosis and a prominent inflammatory infiltrate; therefore, there was no convincing evidence to support an inflammatory lesion. To date, histiocytic diseases in leopard geckos have been limited to these non-neoplastic conditions. Therefore, the present case provides novel insight into histiocytic diseases in leopard geckos, specifically as a neoplastic condition.
Oral masses in reptiles include a variety of lesions, such as abscesses, cysts, granulomas, and neoplasms [11]. Among neoplastic lesions, various types of oral tumors have been reported in reptiles. In the present case, the gross findings suggested that the tumor originated from the oropharyngeal region, although the exact site of origin could not be determined. According to a retrospective study, oropharyngeal neoplasms account for 29.4% of reptilian alimentary neoplasms, with various neoplasms such as adenocarcinoma and hemangiosarcoma being reported [12]. In previously documented cases, histiocytic sarcoma in reptiles has been reported in the retrobulbar space, skin, coelom, musculoskeletal system, and large intestine, with no evidence of metastasis [7, 8, 14, 20]. In the present case, the tumor extended from the oropharyngeal region through the dermis to the surface of the cornea, indicating aggressive local invasion.
Most neoplasms in reptiles, similar to those in mammals and birds, are believed to develop with aging. However, both cases of histiocytic sarcoma in reptiles were diagnosed at remarkably young ages, at two years and at one year and eight months, although this should be interpreted in light of species-specific lifespan differences [8]. For example, lymphoma has been reported in a Savannah monitor lizard, aged six months, as a neoplasm occurring in a young reptile [18]. Histiocytic sarcoma typically occurs in middle-aged to older individuals in dogs, but there is also a report of its development in a nine-month-old dog [15]. These findings suggest that histiocytic sarcoma can develop even in young reptiles. Evaluation of further cases is needed to clarify potential predisposing factors and the pathogenesis in reptiles.
This study is the first to document histiocytic sarcoma in a leopard gecko. In addition to previously recognized non-neoplastic histiocytic diseases, the identification of this neoplastic lesion expands the understanding of histiocytic disorders in this species and contributes to the field of comparative pathology in reptiles.
