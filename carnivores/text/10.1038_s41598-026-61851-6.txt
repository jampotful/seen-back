Abstract: Glaucoma is a primary cause of permanent vision loss, and it often gets worse without anybody noticing.
This makes it very important to find it early to stop vision loss. Manually evaluating retinal fundus images frequently
necessitates considerable effort and is prone to observer-dependent discrepancies. To address these constraints, a new
automated method for detecting glaucoma is presented. It combines fractal-inspired Sierpinski triangle spatial
decomposition with multi-scale triangular segmentation to reliably identify clinically important areas of the retina.
Handcrafted descriptors that include statistical, frequency-domain, wavelet, morphological, and texture-uniformity
data are taken from small areas, giving a detailed and unique picture of the structures in the retina. A hybrid deep
learning system that combines bidirectional LSTM, bidirectional GRU, and CNN with Bi-LSTM models that have
attention layers captures spatial-temporal connections while highlighting essential visual cues for diagnosis. The
Harris Hawks Optimizer, Grey Wolf Optimizer, Red Fox Optimizer, and Social Feature Optimizer are all examples
of meta-heuristic algorithms that improve the feature subset. The HHO-based version gives the best results. A
thorough examination using benchmark datasets shows that the system works very well, with 98.48% accuracy on
Drishti-GS, 98.99% on Origa, 97.44% on RimOne-V2, 97.80% on HVD Binary-Class, 99.02% on HVD-Advance,
98.05% on HVD-Early, and 97.11% on HVD Multi-Class trials. The proposed system shows high reliability and
stability in glaucoma diagnosis, delivering consistent performance across multiple datasets. It provides a scalable,
non-invasive, and efficient solution for automatic detection, supporting doctors in early treatment and improving
patients chances of recovery.
Keywords: Glaucoma Detection, Deep Learning, Handcrafted Feature Extraction, Sierpinski Triangle, Feature
optimization.

Glaucoma is a progressive eye disorder and a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide. It primarily
results from damage to the optic nerve head (ONH), often linked to increased intraocular pressure. In 2020,
more than 11 million people were affected globally, and this number is expected to rise to over 110 million
by 2040 [1]. Since the disease usually shows no early symptoms, timely detection is essential to prevent
vision loss. Measuring intraocular pressure and capturing retinal images are key steps in a complete eye
examination. In color fundus photographs (CFP), a major indicator of glaucoma is optic disc “cupping,”
where the optic cup becomes noticeably larger relative to the disc-reflected by an increased vertical cup-todisc ratio (VCDR). A change greater than 0.2 in this ratio can be a warning sign [2]. Thinning of the retinal
nerve fiber layer (RNFL) around the disc is another sign, often appearing after significant nerve fiber
damage. Automated screening methods are becoming more popular because they are faster and more
consistent than traditional manual evaluations that rely heavily on human expertise.
CFP is an affordable and gentle method for capturing images of the optic nerve head, its surrounding
regions, and the retinal blood vessels. Identifying the optic disc and cup, as well as measuring the VCDR,
remains a challenging task. Many different types of glaucoma exist. Primary open-angle glaucoma develops
slowly and often goes unnoticed, as it typically does not present any apparent symptoms. Angle-closure
glaucoma can happen suddenly and may cause significant discomfort. It could cause blurry vision and make
lights appear to have halos around them. Normal-tension glaucoma can damage the optic nerve even when
the eye pressure is normal [3]. Glaucoma comes in numerous kinds of problems. Primary open-angle
glaucoma progresses gradually and typically lacks of distinct symptoms. Angle-closure glaucoma can
emerge without warning and be quite painful. It could cause hazy vision and make you feel like you see
halos around lights. Normal-tension glaucoma affects the optic nerve, even though the pressure in the eyes

Some early indicators include persistent eye pain, redness, blurry vision, headaches, and halos around
objects. Fig. 1 illustrates the use of tonometry, gonioscopy, ophthalmoscopy, fundus imaging, visual field
testing and Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) in diagnosing and measuring RNFL thickness. Machine
learning has been very important in making it possible to automatically find glaucoma by focussing on the
optic disc area and using features that were made by hand, such as texture patterns, shape information, and
brightness values [4]. But these methods don't always work well when used on photos from diverse sources
because variations in lighting, image clarity, or patient anatomy make them less reliable. Deep learning has
solved many of these problems by letting computers learn functional patterns straight from raw retinal
pictures. Conventional machine learning and deep learning approaches for glaucoma detection still struggle
to effectively represent localized retinal structures, reduce feature redundancy, maintain robustness across
heterogeneous datasets, and achieve stable classification performance under varying imaging conditions.
Most existing studies primarily rely on conventional CNN architectures, standard retinal segmentation
techniques, or isolated handcrafted feature extraction methods that often overlook fine-grained retinal
variations associated with glaucoma progression. Therefore, this study develops an efficient and robust
glaucoma detection framework by integrating recursive Sierpinski triangle-based spatial partitioning,
handcrafted feature extraction, meta-heuristic feature optimization, and hybrid sequential deep learning
architectures within a unified retinal image analysis pipeline. The proposed framework aims to improve
retinal feature representation, optimize discriminative feature learning, and enhance glaucoma classification
performance across multiple benchmark datasets.
Motivation and contributions
Traditional deep learning approaches for retinal image analysis often generate generic features that overlook
subtle structural and chromatic variations in the vicinity of the optic disc and retinal vasculature. Standard
CNNs struggle to capture spatial relationships between distinct retinal regions, requiring large datasets and
high computational resources. A hierarchical segmentation approach divides square retinal images into four
equilateral Sierpinski triangles on each side, recursively generating hierarchical sub-triangles to maintain
local geometric structures. Each sub-triangle is decomposed into RGB channels, histogram-based statistical
descriptors are computed, and an extensive set of handcrafted features, including fractal dimensions, local
ternary patterns, Gabor and wavelet energies, vessel density, cup-disc offsets, Fourier descriptors, Radon
GLCM, intensity gradients, contrast, entropy, skewness, and kurtosis are extracted from the images. To
identify the most discriminative information, four meta-heuristic optimizations algorithm -Harris Hawks
Optimizer (HHO), Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO), Red Fox Optimizer (RFO), and Social Feature Optimizer
(SFO)-are applied to the extracted features. The optimized feature vectors are then processed through a

Recent developments in medical data analysis have made it easier to use deep learning and machine learning
to classification and prediction challenges. While machine learning techniques have been applied for
glaucoma detection, using manually extracted features can become difficult and less effective when dealing
with large or complex datasets. Essential information can be automatically learned by DL models from both
structured and unstructured sources. Finding subtle patterns in clinical data is facilitated by this. Models
have performed well in assessing medical images, including segmenting them, grouping them, and
identifying anomalies. The use of DL techniques to detect glaucoma in retinal fundus images is therefore
becoming more and more popular. This paper focuses on assessing existing ML and DL methodologies,
pointing out developments, and suggesting possible lines of inquiry.
2.1 Machine Learning Approaches for Glaucoma Detection
A hybrid method that incorporates ML and DL classifiers for the analysis of OCT images was presented by
Juneja et al. [4]. The method integrates forecasts using majority voting machinery and weighted decision
fusion. To help doctors better comprehend the results, gradient-weighted class activation maps were
generated to highlight areas of interest.
Y.Kaya [5] introduced an automatic optic disc detection method for retinal images using the Cuckoo Search
Algorithm and SSIM. The approach used average optic disc templates with adaptive histogram equalization
to improve image quality and locate the optic disc accurately. The method achieved high detection accuracy
on ONHSD, DRIONS, and DRIVE datasets.
Gautam [6] suggested an approach that uses a flexible analytical wavelet transform on the green channel of
fundus images. We extracted and normalized texture descriptors, including GLCM and CHI histograms.
Using PCA with t-value ranking reduced the number of features, and the least squares support vector
machine was utilized as the final classifier.

2.2 Deep Learning Approaches for Glaucoma Detection
Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have greatly improved the analysis of medical images by allowing
for automatic and accurate illness identification through direct learning of picture patterns. The CNN-based
methods make it easier to get discriminative features without having to do it by hand, which improves
diagnostic accuracy and helps find diseases earlier. Table 2 shows a comparison of several of the most
recent deep learning-based systems for finding glaucoma.
Shyamalee et al. [4] put out a glaucoma detection framework utilising Attention U-Net with a ResNet-50
backbone for optic disc and cup segmentation, alongside a modified Inception V3 architecture for the
classification of fundus images. We combined Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)
and Grad-CAM++ to make it easier to understand by using visual heatmaps to show diagnostic areas.
Balasubramanian et al. [10] put forward a model for diagnosing glaucoma that uses correlation-based
feature selection together with bio-inspired optimization methods including Genetic Algorithm, Particle

Swarm Optimization, and Ant Colony Optimization. A kernel extreme learning machine (KELM) was
utilized to categorize the selected features. To enhance the accuracy and stability of the KELM, adjustments
were made to its parameters. A research by Pinheiro et al. [11] used chromatic pupillometry and machine
learning to detect glaucoma. LassoCV and RFE selection approaches were used in the research to assess
specific filters, low-pass algorithms, balancing strategies, and customized features. Conventional classifiers
like Random Forest, Extra Trees, and SVM were contrasted with deep models like CNN and Transformer
networks.
Habeb et al. [12] introduced a new feature selection method that merges Caputo fractional order with the
Cuckoo search algorithm, aiming to enhance the classification of glaucoma images. The system created a
unified representation by combining deep features from MobileNet and VGG19 with handcrafted features
such as HOG and LBP. The k-NN method was applied to classify this representation. This method provided
an improved way to find important features when analyzing eye images.
Velpula et al. [13] presented a glaucoma detection system that combines deep learning techniques with
retinal fundus image analysis. The primary objectives of the system were to differentiate between the cup
and the optic disc, apply classification strategies, and extract features using both manual and automated
methods. Singh et al. [14] developed a glaucoma prediction system utilizing a gravitational search
optimization technique, which identifies important features in fundus images of the retina. The approach
used various descriptors, including wavelet transformations, higher-order spectra, GLCM, GLRLM, and
the cup-to-disc ratio all at once.To identify the problem, it utilized machine learning classifiers.
Aljohani et al. [3] introduced a hybrid glaucoma detection framework that integrates deep learning with
federated machine learning, enabling secure collaboration among medical institutions without sharing
patient data. Their system was trained on a diverse collection of retinal fundus images while maintaining
strict data privacy, demonstrating an effective and scalable diagnostic support approach for ophthalmology
applications.
Yan et al. [15] proposed a glaucoma identification method that employs MixDA-based data augmentation
to enrich retinal fundus image datasets. This enhanced dataset was subsequently used to train convolutional
neural networks capable of distinguishing glaucomatous eyes. Their work incorporated hierarchical
prediction models, improved feature extraction mechanisms, and the synthesis of artificial samples to boost
detection accuracy.
Chowdhury et al. [16] designed a rim-learning architecture for glaucoma screening using a Twin Supervised
Generative Adversarial Network (TS-GAN). In this approach, fundus images are processed through both
generator and discriminator modules to improve the representation of the optic rim region an essential
structure for glaucoma classification. The framework leverages a combination of supervised and adversarial
learning to enhance diagnostic precision.
Jyoti et al. [17] proposed a diagnostic strategy combining the Fourier–Bessel Series Expansion (FBSE) with
the Empirical Wavelet Transform (EWT) for feature extraction from fundus images. These extracted
features are mapped onto a memristive crossbar array, mimicking neuromorphic computing behavior. The
crossbar-based system facilitates automated glaucoma detection using conventional machine learning
classifiers.
Abhishek et al. [18] proposed the glaucoma detection framework the first step involves segmenting retinal
fundus images to identify the optic disc and cup regions. Features are then extracted from these segmented
areas to capture distinctive patterns. The classification performance is further improved by combining
several base learners using a stacking-based ensemble approach.

Despite the remarkable progress made by existing machine learning and deep learning methods for
glaucoma diagnosis, several critical challenges continue to affect their reliability and generalizability.
Most conventional CNN-based frameworks emphasize global retinal representations while insufficiently
capturing subtle spatial abnormalities around the optic disc and retinal nerve fiber regions that are closely
associated with glaucoma progression. Likewise, handcrafted feature-driven and optimization-assisted

approaches often introduce redundant feature representations and exhibit inconsistent performance across
diverse retinal datasets and imaging conditions. Furthermore, many existing methods depend heavily on
conventional segmentation and feature extraction strategies, which may limit the discriminative
representation of complex retinal structures. These challenges highlight the need for a more effective
glaucoma detection framework capable of combining efficient spatial partitioning, optimized feature
representation, and robust hybrid deep learning classification.

3. Proposed Methodology
The glaucoma detection framework consists of four-fold architecture: data collection, dataset
preprocessing, feature extraction and feature Optimization, and classification. Authors used fundus images
from four publicly available datasets: Drishti-GS, Origa, RimOne-V2, and Harvard (HVD). In the proposed
framework, 80% of the dataset was used for training and 20% for testing. The preprocessing stage was
designed to enhance retinal contrast and suppress image noise, thereby improving the visibility of
glaucoma-related features. Each retinal image was normalized to a square shape and systematically divided
into smaller triangular regions using recursive Sierpinski triangle segmentation. The geometric
decomposition facilitated the extraction of localized image features. The red, green, and blue (RGB) three
colour channels were analysed separately, and statistical histogram-based evaluations were performed to
capture variations in intensity, colour, and spatial distribution. Additionally, handcrafted descriptors
encompassing structural, textural, and morphological attributes were incorporated to provide a
comprehensive representation of retinal characteristics.
The feature vectors that are generated from the feature extraction process are put into a hybrid deep learning
architecture that includes bidirectional LSTM [19], bidirectional GRU [54], and CNN [55] with Bi-LSTM
models. Attention mechanisms are added to these models to make patterns that are important for diagnosis
stand out. The framework can do both binary classification, which separates glaucoma from non-glaucoma,
and multiclass classification, which separates early, advanced, and normal instances. The framework's
capacity to detect things accurately, precisely, and quickly, as well as its F1-score, shows that it is successful
for automated glaucoma screening across all datasets.
3.1 Dataset-Prepressing
The proposed framework uses four publicly available benchmark datasets: Drishti-GS [20], Origa [21],
RimOne-V2 [22], and Harvard (HVD) [23]. People often use these datasets to find and classify glaucoma.
Table 3 presents the detailed specifications and data distribution of each dataset, while Figure 2 illustrates
representative retinal fundus images. RimOne-V2 has 455 high-resolution pictures, 255 of which show
healthy eyes and 200 of which show glaucomatous eyes. Ophthalmologists manually divided the
photographs into parts. They were obtained at a resolution of 400 × 469 pixels from Hospital Universitario
de Canarias and Hospital Universitario Miguel Servet. There are 1,544 photographs in HVD, including 788
normal, 289 early-stage glaucoma, and 467 advanced glaucoma cases, enabling evaluation across different
disease stages. Drishti-GS comprises 101 images, including 31 healthy and 70 glaucomatous eyes, with
optic disc and cup annotations provided by four clinical experts. The ORIGA dataset, developed from the
Singapore Malay Eye Study, contains 650 retinal fundus images, including 482 normal and 168
glaucomatous eyes, facilitating large-scale automated glaucoma diagnosis. All retinal fundus images were
resized to 224 × 224 pixels to maintain a uniform input resolution and improve computational efficiency.
Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) was applied to enhance local image contrast,
while median filtering was employed to suppress noise and preserve important retinal structures [4]. To
ensure an unbiased experimental evaluation, each dataset was first divided into training and testing subsets
using an 80:20 stratified split based solely on the original retinal fundus images. Data augmentation was
subsequently applied only to the training subset, whereas the testing subset remained completely unchanged
throughout the experiments. Consequently, augmented images derived from an original image were
confined exclusively to the corresponding training set and never appeared in the testing set, thereby
eliminating data leakage and ensuring a fair performance evaluation. The augmentation process included
horizontal flipping, rotation, zooming, and brightness variation to increase training sample diversity, reduce
overfitting, and improve the model's generalization capability [24]. The detailed distribution of the original

3.4 Feature Optimization
Feature optimization plays a crucial role in the proposed glaucoma detection framework, positioned
between the feature extraction and classification process. Its primary goal is to refine the high-dimensional
features obtained from retinal fundus images. The initial feature set 𝑭 ∈ ℝ𝒅 often includes redundant, noisy,
or weakly discriminative attributes, which can increase computational complexity, reduce classification
accuracy, and lead to overfitting. To address these challenges, meta-heuristic optimization algorithms such
as the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) [32], Harris Hawks Optimizer (HHO) [33], Red Fox Optimizer (RFO)
[34], and Social Feature Optimizer (SFO) [35] are employed. These methods utilize population-based
search strategies inspired by collective animal behaviors in nature. Each algorithm maintains a balance
between global exploration of the feature space and local exploitation of promising feature subsets, ensuring
effective selection of the most relevant and discriminative features. Through this adaptive search process,
the optimization phase significantly enhances model generalization and stability, paving the way for
improved classification accuracy in glaucoma detection. The fitness function employed in the proposed
HHO-based feature optimization framework is designed to maximize the discriminative capability of the
selected retinal feature subset while reducing redundant feature representations. Each candidate solution
generated by the optimization algorithm is represented as a continuous vector and converted into a binary
feature-selection mask using a threshold value of 0.5, where ‘1’ indicates a selected feature and ‘0’ indicates
an unselected feature. The fitness value is computed using the negative mean variance of the selected
features as follows:

3.5 Classification
In the proposed glaucoma detection framework, the classification stage serves as the final module. It
operates on the optimized feature vector 𝑭 ∈ ℝ𝒏 ,which is derived through meta-heuristic feature selection
employing algorithms such as the Red Fox Optimizer (RFO), Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO), Harris Hawks
Optimizer (HHO), and Social Feature Optimizer (SFO). The optimized feature vector 𝑭∗ ∈ ℝ𝒏 , obtained
after meta-heuristic feature optimization, consists of statistical, texture, wavelet, frequency-domain, and
morphological descriptors arranged in a predefined and consistent order. Although retinal fundus images
are inherently spatial rather than temporal, these optimized handcrafted features are represented as a onedimensional ordered sequence before being provided as input to the Bi-LSTM, Bi-GRU, and CNN-BiLSTM architectures. This feature sequencing strategy preserves the structural relationships among
complementary retinal descriptors, enabling the recurrent networks to model long-range inter-feature
dependencies and contextual interactions that cannot be effectively captured by conventional feed-forward
classifiers. Consequently, the proposed hybrid framework learns more discriminative feature
representations, resulting in improved robustness and classification performance for both binary and
multiclass glaucoma detection. The sequential feature representation is subsequently processed using
several deep learning architectures to capture complementary structural and contextual feature relationships
for glaucoma diagnosis. While a Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) network represents
contextual dependencies in both forward and backward directions, a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)
extracts local spatial correlations. Spatial and temporal learning are combined in a CNN with a Bi-LSTM
hybrid model, while a Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit (Bi-GRU) structure uses reset–update techniques
to further improve sequential representation. An expanded setup creates unique feature embedding’s by
having the optimized feature vector individually travel through CNN with Bi-LSTM, Bi-LSTM, and BiGRU models. First, a 1D CNN layer refines the concatenated output to improve discriminative
representation. Batch normalization is used to improve convergence behaviour and stabilize learning
dynamics when the generated convolutional features flow into a dense layer of 256 neurons. Two
classification heads produce the final predictions: one for multiclass categorization into Normal, Early
Glaucoma, and Advanced Glaucoma, and one for binary differentiation between Normal and Glaucoma

A detailed comparative study evaluated the effectiveness of the proposed hybrid deep learning framework
in comparison to several established state-of-the-art models. Benchmark systems included four machine

learning models: ResNet50[39], VGG19 [39], and VGG16 [40], along with two hybrid models: ResNet50Xception [41] and VGG19-MobileNetV2 [42]. In addition, the analysis assessed the performance of the
proposed hybrid architecture, integrating Stacked BiLSTM, BiGRU, and CNN with BiLSTM modules,
under four meta-heuristic optimization algorithms: Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO), Harris Hawks Optimizer
(HHO), Social Feature Optimizer (SFO), and Red Fox Optimizer (RFO).

Origa, RimOne-V2, HVD Binary Class, HVD Advance, HVD Early, and HVD Multiclass. All the
experiments are follow 80:20 train-test ratio to maintain uniformity across models. Additionally, 5-fold
cross-validation experiments were conducted to further evaluate the robustness and generalization
capability of the proposed framework. The comparative analysis was based on the performance metrics
Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score with the results for all datasets and models described in the

Statistical Analysis (Wilcoxon signed-rank test)
In the statistical analysis, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test is performed over the accuracy, recall, precision,
and F1-score across all datasets, comparing the performance of the proposed model with that of existing
models.

Although the sign test can be used to test both one-sample and two-sample paired data, the Wilcoxon
signed-rank test is more powerful than the sign test for these tasks because it makes use of the magnitudes
of the differences rather than just their signs [52].
The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was developed by Frank Wilcoxon in 1945. The basic idea behind the
Wilcoxon signed-rank test is [52]:
• Form null and alternative hypotheses and choose a degree of confidence. The null hypothesis is
that the median of the population of differences between the paired data is zero. The alternative
hypothesis is that the population median difference is not equal to 0.
• Compute the test statistic. First, compute the differences between the paired data samples; then,
rank the differences according to magnitude only, that is, without regard to their sign; next, sum
the ranks of the positive and negative differences; finally, pick the minimum of the sums as our test
statistic.
• Compare the test statistic to a critical value. If the test statistic is less than the critical value, then
we reject the null hypothesis.
The Wilcoxon signed-rank test is a non-parametric statistical hypothesis test used as an alternative to the
paired t-test. The test provides an additional safeguard against biases that may occur with modest sample
sizes. Additionally, the test is used to evaluate the null hypothesis that in the underlying population of
differences among pairs, the median difference is equal to 0. The alternative hypothesis is that the
population median difference is not equal to 0. P-values lower than 0.05 were considered significant [53].
Under the proposed method, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (two-sided) was used to determine whether the
median accuracy of the proposed model is significantly different from that of the existing models across
four datasets. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test calculates a p-value, which is then compared to a chosen level
of significance (alpha(α)) to decide on the null hypothesis. Common choices for alpha(α) include 0.05 (5%),
0.01 (1%), or 0.10 (10%).

Advantages of the Proposed Model
• Recursive Sierpinski triangle-based partitioning improves localized retinal structure representation and
enhances fine-grained spatial feature extraction associated with glaucoma progression, resulting in superior
classification performance compared with full-image and uniform grid-based retinal representation
approaches across all benchmark datasets.
• Integration of handcrafted feature extraction, HHO-based feature optimization, and hybrid BiLSTMBiGRU deep learning classification improves discriminative retinal feature learning, reduces redundant
feature information, and achieves stable classification performance for both binary and multiclass glaucoma
detection tasks.
• Fold-wise cross-validation analysis, confusion matrix-based evaluation, and comparative experimental
analysis confirmed strong generalization capability, stable classification performance, and improved
robustness under varying retinal data distributions across multiple benchmark glaucoma datasets.
• Experimental evaluation across multiple benchmark glaucoma datasets containing varying retinal image
characteristics and imaging conditions demonstrated stable classification performance and improved
robustness of the proposed framework under heterogeneous retinal data distributions.
• Confusion matrix-based evaluation across binary and multiclass glaucoma classification tasks provided
clinically meaningful performance analysis and demonstrated stable classification capability across
multiple benchmark retinal datasets.
Limitations of the Proposed Model
• Integration of recursive partitioning, handcrafted feature extraction, HHO-based feature optimization, and
hybrid deep learning classification increases computational complexity, training time, and memory
requirements during retinal image analysis.

• Extensive robustness evaluation under controlled image degradations including blur, noise, contrast

• Current experimental analysis does not include extensive calibration analysis, subgroup-wise clinical

baseline approaches due to dataset and computational limitations.

5. Conclusion
The proposed study introduced an automated glaucoma detection framework integrating recursive
Sierpinski triangle-based spatial partitioning, handcrafted retinal feature extraction, HHO-based
feature optimization, and hybrid BiLSTM-BiGRU deep learning classification. Retinal fundus
images from benchmark datasets including Drishti-GS, Origa, RimOne-V2, and HVD subsets
were preprocessed using CLAHE enhancement, median filtering, and data augmentation to
improve image quality and classification generalization. Recursive Sierpinski triangle-based
partitioning enabled effective multi-scale retinal representation by preserving localized retinal
structures and spatial retinal variations associated with glaucoma progression. Handcrafted
descriptors extracted from statistical, frequency, wavelet, morphological, and texture-based retinal
characteristics provided detailed retinal feature representation, while HHO-based optimization
improved discriminative feature selection and reduced redundant feature information.
Comparative experimental analysis against full-image representation, uniform grid-based
partitioning, classical machine learning models, and modern deep learning architectures
demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed framework across all benchmark glaucoma
datasets. Experimental results achieved classification accuracies of 98.48% on Drishti-GS, 98.99%
on Origa, 97.44% on RimOne-V2, and above 97% across all HVD subsets. Fold-wise crossvalidation analysis and confusion matrix-based evaluation further confirmed stable classification
performance, strong generalization capability, and robust glaucoma detection performance for
both binary and multiclass classification tasks under varying retinal image characteristics and
imaging conditions. Although the proposed framework incorporates an attention mechanism to
improve discriminative feature learning, qualitative visualization techniques such as Gradientweighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM), attention heatmaps, and region-importance
overlays were not investigated in the present study. Future work will incorporate these
explainability methods to verify that the model focuses on clinically relevant retinal structures,
including the optic disc, optic cup, neuroretinal rim, and retinal nerve fiber layer. In addition, the
robustness of the proposed framework will be further investigated under challenging real-world
imaging conditions, including blur, noise, low contrast, poor illumination, and non-centered optic
disc regions, to better assess its clinical applicability. Such analyses will further improve the
interpretability, transparency, clinical reliability, and robustness of the proposed decision-support
system.
