Abstract

14

Urban habitats can serve as wildlife refuges, or as ecological traps. As global

15

urbanization continues to increase, native prey species may experience reduced threat from

16

natural predators that tend to avoid human-populated areas. However, such protection often

17

comes at the expense of susceptibility to synanthropic predators, one of the most prevalent being

18

domestic house cats (Felis catus). While cats are a known driving force of mortalities in urban

19

wildlife, their sublethal effects remain understudied. To better understand the behavioral effects

20

of synanthropic predators on native urban prey, we examined responses of western fence lizards

21

(Sceloporus occidentalis) to Artificial Intelligence (AI)-generated visual stimuli of native

22

predators (snakes) and synanthropic predators (house cats). Visual stimuli included: California

23

striped racers (Masticophis lateralis), a native predator; domestic cats, a synanthropic

24

mammalian predator (Felis catus); and desert cottontails (Sylvilagus audubonii), a native

25

mammalian non-predator. We quantified lizard responses as the change in rates of looking and

26

locomotion during and following the video presentation. Our results revealed that lizards

27

discriminated cats from the control by looking more within the first 90 seconds following

28

exposure. Lizards looked more across the entire 5-minute observation period following exposure

29

to all stimulus types compared to control conditions, indicating that AI generated visual stimuli

30

effectively elicit behavioral responses. Together, these findings indicate that urban lizards

31

initially detect and respond to visual cues of cats, suggesting that cat-induced mortality is not a

32

result of inability to detect such predators. Thus, cats influence urban prey not only through

33

direct predation risk, but also changes in behavior associated with the presence of synanthropic

34

predators.

Introduction
As urbanization alters natural landscapes, it is becoming increasingly important to

41

understand the effects of human development on ecological systems. Habitat loss and

42

fragmentation often lead to reduced native predation pressure in urban environments, altering

43

predator-prey dynamics (Seto et al. 2012; Grimm et al. 2008; Moller et al. 2012; Muhly et al.

44

2011). The decline of natural predators in increasingly urban habitats may provide increased

45

protection to organisms that are typically preyed upon in rural areas (‘the urban refuge

46

hypothesis’, Moller et al. 2012; Rebolo-Ifrán et al. 2017). While such environments can serve as

47

refuges, they may also act as ‘ecological traps’ if native prey populations are forced to coexist

48

with synanthropic predators that thrive in human-modified habitats (Tomialojc 1970; Johnston et

49

al. 2001; Moller et al. 2012; Salo et al. 2007; Loss et al. 2013).

50

Domestic house cats (Felis catus) are a synanthropic species that poses a significant

51

predation risk to a broad range of wildlife in urban areas (Loss et al. 2013). Cat predation in the

52

United States accounts for an estimated 6.3 to 23.3 billion small bird and mammal mortality rates

53

annually (Loss et al. 2013). The diet of free-roaming domestic cats is influenced by locally

54

available prey, resulting in the consumption of substantial amounts of small wildlife (Lepczyk et.

55

al. 2023). Domestic cats were first brought to North America around 450 years ago (Welker et al.

56

2025), and their effects on urban biodiversity are enormous (Magle & Crowther 2023). Despite

57

their recognition as a notable mortality risk to urban prey, there is little known about the

58

sublethal effects of the species, including impacts on behavior. Changes in wildlife behavior can

Animals detect predators using a variety of modalities, including visual cues (Caro 2005).

61
62

Antipredator vigilance is often used in visual discrimination experiments to quantify risk

63

assessment and changes in looking behavior after being exposed to a threatening stimulus (e.g.,

64

Blumstein et al. 2000; Kong et al. 2022). It is important to control for a variety of features when

65

studying stimulus discrimination. Using live animals creates novel welfare issues (for both the

66

experimental subjects and the models), an issue that can be avoided by using video playback.

67

While video playback has been used to study opponent assessment (Ord & Evans 2002), there is

68

a long history of taxidermic mounts used for studies on predator recognition (Blumstein et al.

69

2000; Carlson et al. 2017, Tinbergen 1989; Curio et al. 1978). However, lizards exposed to

70

taxidermic mounted cats do not engage in natural behavior (Lishmund et al. 2025). An additional

71

shortcoming of taxidermic models is that often only one or a few mounts are used in an

72

experiment, which then limits the inference to the mount rather than to the predator type. Until

73

now, studies using visual predator cues have been limited by the inability to create controlled

74

replicates for each stimulus. However, recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) now permit

75

the creation of a collection of replicated visual stimuli to simulate ecologically relevant, yet

76

controlled, encounters. This approach allows one to standardize the background and movement

77

while varying the stimulus in a controlled way. Thus, differential responses can be attributed to

78

the stimulus characteristics alone rather than confounding variables associated with specific

79

videos.

Materials and Methods

91

Study Species and Site

92

Western fence lizards are widely distributed across Western North America, making them an

93

abundant and easily accessible species for study (Bishop et al. 2023). As ectotherms, they

94

occupy a broad range of habitats with high levels of sun exposure, such as grasslands,

95

woodlands, and chaparral, and have been effectively used as model organisms in a wide range of

96

studies on environmental change and urbanization (Bishop et al. 2023; Buckley et al. 2008;

97

Sinervo et al. 2010; Putman et al. 2019; Putman et al. 2025; Putman et al. 2024). They also live

98

in and around cities, including Los Angeles, CA (USA), where they are exposed to cats, making

99

them a suitable focal species for our study.

100

This study took place on the University of California Los Angeles (UCLA) campus under

101

approved IACUC protocols (ID: ARC-2025-016) and state collection permits (SC-2480). We

102

collected sixteen wild adult western fence lizards from a single population in Sage Hill, a natural

103

reserve on the UCLA campus. The lizards had no previous history of rearing or use in

104

experiments. Upon capture, we measured snout-vent length (mm) and weight (g). We

Stimulus Videos

121

We created four, 15 s long videos for each stimulus type (snake, cat, and rabbit) using

122

Google Veo 2 (Google, 2025) to ensure biologically accurate movement and features of each

123

species. The four unique videos per stimulus type depicted slightly different individuals of the

124

same species displaying similar movement patterns. The animals were scaled to fill the frame to

125

ensure that all behavioral responses could be attributed to species-specific visual characteristics

126

rather than differences in size. All stimuli were superimposed onto a standardized background

127

image of Sage Hill (Figure S2) and were introduced through faded transitions to avoid overly

We presented visual stimuli on an 18.5-inch portable IPS monitor (CoolHood; 1920 x

131

1080 (FHD) resolution, 120 Hz refresh rate, 100% sRGB color gamut). Although this display

132

lacked ultraviolet (UV) wavelengths detected by lizards (Fleishman et al. 2024), it served as an

133

optimal alternative to cathode ray tubes (CRT) used in past studies on exposure to visual stimuli

134

(Macedonia et al. 1993). There is evidence that visual stimuli can appear distorted to subjects

135

when presented on CRT screens which tend to have lower refresh rates compared to LCD

136

technology (Railton et al. 2010). Because lizards have high critical flicker fusion frequencies

137

(Jenssen & Swenson 1974), using an IPS monitor with a high refresh rate ensured that visual

138

stimuli would be clearly presented to subjects.

Experimental Design

141

Prior to the start of each trial, we placed monitors against the plexiglass panel of the

142

enclosure and mounted a camera on the edge of the enclosure. We lifted the roof of the enclosure

143

to make sure that we could visibly observe the subject. After a five-minute acclimation period

144

following experimental setup, we displayed the static image of Sage Hill with no stimulus

145

present for five minutes (Figure 1). We then presented the 15 s stimulus video (snake, cat, rabbit,

146

or control) which transitioned into the static image of Sage Hill for an additional five minutes

147

before the monitor was turned off. Observations continued for five minutes after the monitor was

148

turned off (Figure 1). The observer (E. Pfister) quietly dictated behavioral transitions into a

149

digital recorder starting 1 min prior to the stimulus onset and for 10 min following stimulus

150

exposure. The total focal observation time per individual was 11 minutes and 15 seconds,

Statistical Methods

158

We quantified focal observations using the behavioral event recorder JWatcher 1.0

159

(Blumstein & Daniel 2006). All behaviors recorded in JWatcher were defined as mutually

160

exclusive. We separated focal observations per trial into a 60 s pre-stimulus baseline, followed

161

by ten 30 s time intervals. Focal observations for the 15 s stimulus exposure were included in the

162

first 30 s time interval.

163

We analyzed focal observations in R (R Core Team 2025) using RStudio (Posit Team

164

2026) where we calculated behavioral rates per time interval as the quantity of behaviors divided

165

by the total time in sight within each time interval. To determine the changes in behavioral rates

166

over time, we subtracted 60 s pre-stimulus baseline rates from the behavioral rate from each time

167

interval.

168

To examine the change in rate of key behaviors over time following stimulus exposure,

169

we fitted general linear mixed models (GLMMs) with a Gaussian error structure to model the

170

change in rates of horizontal looking, vertical looking, horizontal locomotion, vertical

171

locomotion, combined looking, and combined locomotion. We fitted our initial model for the

172

first 60 s and the first 90 s using the lme4 (Bates et al. 2015) and lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al.

173

2017) packages in R, which analyzed immediate responses to visual stimuli. To examine broad

Results

200

Lizards modified the rate at which they looked (horizontally) within the first 90 s

201

following stimulus exposure. This change in rate was influenced by stimulus type (ANOVA: F =

202

3.86, p = 0.011; Figure 2A). Specifically, cats elicited increased rates of horizontal looking

203

relative to control conditions (t = -3.28, p = 0.007, Figure 2A), but other stimuli did not (all p >

204

0.05; Figure 2A).

205

Lizards modified their vertical locomotion behavior within the first 90 seconds following

206

stimulus exposure as a function of stimulus type (ANOVA: F = 2.67, p = 0.049; Figure 2D).

207

Specifically, rabbits elicited higher rates of vertical locomotion than snakes (t = 2.81, p = 0.029;

208

Figure 2D). No stimulus type elicited significant differences in vertical locomotion relative to

209

control conditions (p > 0.05 in all cases; Figure 2D). Stimuli did not influence changes in

210

horizontal locomotion (ANOVA: F = 1.03, p = 0.381; Figure 2B) or vertical looking rates

211

(ANOVA: F = 2.10, p = 0.103; Figure 2C) within the first 90 seconds following stimulus

212

exposure.

216

F = 4.92, p = 0.002; Figure 2G), and vertical locomotion (ANOVA: F = 6.84, p < 0.001; Figure

2H). All stimuli elicited elevated rates of horizontal looking and locomotion relative to control

conditions (all p < 0.05; Figure 2E). Lizards exhibited increased vertical looking rates when

Discussion

225

As urbanization continues to force wild populations to coexist with synanthropic

226

predators, like cats, it becomes increasingly important to understand the behavioral implications

227

on wildlife and the degree to which native prey are able to assess the risk of such predators. To

228

address this need, we sought to determine whether western fence lizards responded differently to

229

native predators (snakes), synanthropic predators (cats), and native non-predators (rabbits). We

230

found that cats elicited increased horizontal looking behavior in lizards immediately after

231

stimulus exposure, a response not initially elicited by other stimuli, indicating a rapid ability to

232

detect and respond to cats above and beyond other potential threats. By the end of the trial

233

period, all visual stimuli evoked elevated looking behavior relative to control conditions, such

234

that cats were no longer differentiated from other stimuli. Effects of visual stimuli on locomotion

235

were more nuanced. Rabbits and cats elicited higher rates of vertical locomotion behavior than

236

snakes across the complete observation period, but differences from control conditions were not

237

evident early on. Collectively, our findings reveal that lizards distinguish cats from native

238

predators and non-predators immediately following exposure. However, behavioral responses to

239

all visual cues become heightened over time such that cat-specific discrimination is temporary.

240

Given the substantial contribution of cats to wildlife mortality in urban areas,

241

understanding whether urban prey are capable of responding to cats is important from a

conservation perspective. Cats elicited the strongest immediate behavioral response, suggesting

243

that lizards distinguish between cats and other animals upon initial exposure. This evidence is

244

consistent with studies on lizard antipredator responses to cats. In New Zealand, lizards living in

245

areas populated by cats modify their time budgets by reducing basking when exposed to live cats

246

(Cliff et al. 2022). Similarly, lizards in areas of high cat density in Greece remain closer to refuge

247

and exhibit greater flight initiation distance and tail shedding when approached by cat decoys (Li

248

et al. 2014). Together, our findings contribute to growing evidence that threat perception of cats

249

in lizards is widespread, reinforcing the apparent non-lethal effects of cats on urban wildlife

250

behavior.

251

Because cats have been present in North America for approximately 450 years, they are

252

no longer considered novel predators (Welker et al. 2025). Therefore, urban prey which coexist

253

with cats have been able to develop recognition of cats as a threat (Steindler et al. 2020; Banks et

254

al. 2018; West et al. 2018), particularly in urban environments where cat density is substantially

255

higher (Bogrand et al. 2017; Flockhart et al. 2016). While urban lizards in North America may

256

be impacted by cats (Loss et al., 2013; Lepczyk et. al. 2023), our findings suggest that mortality

257

is not due to their inability to identify cats as predators upon initial exposure. Because urban prey

258

exhibit stronger antipredator responses in areas of high cat density (West et al. 2018), increased

259

exposure may lead to more accurate predator recognition over time. Therefore, high levels of

260

urban wildlife mortality caused by cats may be better explained by cat density rather than

261

predator recognition alone.

262

The immediate and unique response to cats highlights the importance of studying

263

immediate behavioral changes following exposure to visual cues of predators. Because prey must

264

engage in rapid risk assessment when they visually detect a predator (Cronin 2005), similar work

on antipredator vigilance to visual cues has often focused on immediate responses upon detecting

266

the sight of a predator (e.g., Blumstein et al. 2000; Zoratto et al. 2014; Carlson et al. 2017).

267

Consistent with previous findings on antipredator behavior, cat-specific responses were distinct

268

and immediate, but discrimination diminished over time. This pattern may reflect that that visual

269

detection of a predator creates an immediate risk that must be evaluated, ranked according to its

270

threat, and managed.

271

The shift in behavior elicited by all stimuli demonstrates that animal videos can be

272

effectively used to study how prey may respond to potential predators. Moreover, our study

273

justifies further use of AI tools to advance current approaches of studying antipredator behavior.

274

Historically, taxidermic mounts have been used to study prey responses to predator cues

275

(Tinbergen 1989; Curio et al. 1978). However, recent studies on lizard perception found no

276

significant behavioral responses to taxidermic mounts of cats (Lishmund et al. 2025). In contrast,

277

our dynamic videos of cats triggered behavioral changes not achieved by static mounts,

278

indicating that movement may be crucial for accurate predator recognition. More recently,

279

animated videos and video playback have been used to study antipredator behavior (Gerlai et al.

280

2009; Watve et al. 2019), which are currently the most realistic substitutions for live animals.

281

Even so, such mechanisms often fail to ensure that behavioral responses do not arise from one

282

singular stimulus depiction. Our creation of multiple exemplars per stimulus type guaranteed that

283

each lizard was exposed to a slightly different depiction of each animal, allowing us to attribute

284

behavioral responses to predator type rather than to a single stimulus depiction.

285

Future studies of lizard responses to videos of predators, like cats, may benefit from

286

better standardizing the starting position of each individual. We intentionally did not begin trials

287

until the individuals were relaxed and free of disruption, which resulted in variation in lizard

While our study focused on visual predator discrimination, prey rely on multiple

297

modalities when assessing risk (Kats & Dill 2016; Hettena et al. 2014; Caro 2005). Prior studies

298

have found more robust responses when individuals were exposed to multiple modalities, such as

299

observations of heightened responses to visual and chemical cues of snakes relative to a single

300

modality (Jones et al. 2024; Amo et al. 2004). Although lizards perceive dynamic visual

301

representations of cats and adjust behavior accordingly, their inability to discriminate between

302

stimuli over time suggests that visual cues alone may be insufficient when studying cat

303

recognition in urban prey. Integration of additional modalities such as olfactory cues may

304

provide more information on the behavioral effects of cats on lizards as well the ability of urban

305

prey to identify and evade cats.

306

Overall, our findings indicate that urban fence lizards detect and respond to AI-generated

307

videos of moving cats, but this cat-specific discrimination is short-lived, as responses to visual

308

stimuli of other animals emerge over time. The ability of lizards to rapidly respond to cats

309

indicates that urban prey may not be naive to synanthropic predators associated with

310

urbanization. Ongoing mortality may be more of a consequence of cat density levels or their
