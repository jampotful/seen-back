# Leopard spot retinopathy represents the initial sign of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment in children: a report of two cases

## Abstract

## Background
Leopard spots, also known as giraffe spots, are commonly associated with bilateral diffuse uveal melanocytic proliferation (BDUMP), acute syphilitic posterior placoid chorioretinitis (ASPPC), primary intraocular lymphoma (PIOL), monoclonal immunoglobulin light chain deposition syndrome (MILCDS), and uveal effusion syndrome (UES) [1–8]. Cases of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment presenting initially with leopard spots are relatively uncommon, especially in children. This report discusses two such cases treated at our institution.

## Case presentation

## Case 1
A 4-year-old boy had previously been diagnosed with left eye uveitis and received systemic steroid therapy at a local hospital, but symptoms persisted. There was no history of trauma, developmental abnormalities, refractive errors, or systemic diseases. We performed a systematic examination of the child, including complete blood count, markers of syphilis, human immunodeficiency virus, and other infections, as well as immunization-related tests on the child, all of which were normal. Examination of the right eye revealed no abnormalities in the anterior and posterior segments, while extensive leopard-spot changes were observed in the posterior pole of the left eye (Fig. 1A and B). Fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) of the left eye showed no vascular leakage, with fluorescein staining was observed at the border of retinal detachment (Fig. 1C). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) showed multifocal, shallow retinal detachment in the left eye (Fig. 1F, G, H). Peripheral retina examination revealed a retinal teal at the 3 o’clock position in the peripheral retina. (Fig. 1D). The patient was diagnosed with rhegmatogenous retinal detachment in the left eye. Scleral buckling with cryocoagulation was performed, resulting in successful retinal reattachment. After 5 months, the multifocal retinal detachment had completely resolved (Fig. 1I, J), with visible coagulation scar at the 3 H position (Fig. 1E). After 2 years, the patient’s fundus remained stable with no recurrence of retinal detachment (Fig. 1K, L).

## Case 2
A 16-year-old girl presented to our clinic with decreased vision in her right eye. There was no history of trauma, developmental abnormalities, or systemic disease, but she had binocular ametropia. Examination revealed no abnormalities in the right anterior segment, but leopard-spot changes were observed at the posterior pole (Fig. 2C), and a retinal hole was found near the 11 o’clock position in the peripheral retina in her right eye fundus (Fig. 2A). OCT of the right eye showed multifocal retinal detachment (Fig. 2D, E). No evident abnormalities were noted in the left eye. Laser photocoagulation therapy was performed around the hole (Fig. 2B). Follow-up examinations showed improved multifocal retinal detachment. The patient had significant absorption of subretinal fluid at the first review compared to before retinal laser photocoagulation (Fig. 2F, G). After 1 year, the OCT showed complete absorption of subretinal fluid (Fig. 2H, I). Her best-corrected visual acuity was 0.8, and there was no recurrence of retinal detachment.

## Discussion and conclusions
Retinal detachment in children typically arises from congenital developmental abnormalities or structural issues, with ocular trauma and myopia also posing common risk factors [9–11]. Here, we present two rare cases of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment in children, initially manifesting with leopard spot retinopathy. Neither patient had a history of congenital abnormalities or trauma, leading us to suspect that the fundus manifestations and pathogenesis were linked to abnormal vitreous liquefaction. The vitreous body in a normal individual consists mainly of water, type II collagen, and glycosaminoglycans. However, it undergoes liquefaction with aging, forming cavities [12]. In our cases, we speculate that despite some vitreous liquefaction, strong adhesion between the posterior vitreous cortex and retina in these children resulted in less subretinal fluid of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment [12–14], leading to multifocal lesions resembling leopard spots on the fundus.
When leopard or giraffe spots appear on the fundus, they should be differentiated from conditions such as BDUMP, ASPPC, MILCDS, PIOL, and others [1–8]. BDUMP presents with multiple round or oval red patchy lesions of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and exudative retinal detachment in the posterior pole [2]. ASPPC, a characteristic ocular manifestation of syphilis, displays yellow-white, patchy, circular, or elliptical lesions on the retina [3, 4]. MILCDS manifests as bilateral leopard spot-like fundus on fundus autofluorescence imaging (FAF), characterized by green-gray subretinal deposits resembling leopard spots [5]. PIOL typically shows low fluorescence circular spots resembling leopard spots on FAF and FFA [6, 7]. UES, a rare condition in healthy middle-aged men, manifests as choroidal elevation, exudative retinal detachment, and leopard grain-shaped changes in RPE [8]. When evaluating patients with leopard spots at the fundus, a thorough inquiry into systemic disease history and careful fundus examination are imperative. In our cases, the absence of trauma, developmental abnormalities, or systemic illness, along with clear retinal tears discovered during fundus examination, aided in distinguishing them from other conditions.
In summary, we present two cases in which a leopard spot retinopathy represents the initial sign of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment. When leopard-spot changes are observed, it is crucial to carefully examine the fundus for any potential retinal holes, especially in pediatric patients.
