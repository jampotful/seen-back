Abstract
Survey work constitutes a fundamental analytical tool in the study of Paleolithic art, providing a
structured basis for documenting figures and their supports, and facilitating the interpretation of
complex representations. The choice of an appropriate recording medium is therefore crucial for
producing clear and reliable analytical documents. This is especially relevant for the Magdalenian
engraved stones from La Marche (West-central France), whose extreme finesse renders visual analysis
challenging. Although 3D digitization methods are gradually applied to cave art, their use in the study of
portable art, considered too complex, remains limited. This study presents a comparative evaluation of
3D digitization methods applied to three engraved stones, each characterized by different engraving
depths (fine, moderate, and deep). Surface scanning was carried out using an Artec Space Spider
structured light scanner and a Medit i700 intraoral scanner, and the results were compared with
photogrammetric models produced using a Nikon D7200. The accuracy, acquisition limits, and postprocessing potential of each method were assessed in Geomagic Wrap, alongside Blender software for
enhancing engraving legibility and producing a 2D image used as a survey medium. The results identify
the Medit i700 as the most appropriate tool, thanks to its ergonomics and optimal capture of fine
engravings. Our investigations on Blender have enabled the production of high-contrast 2D study images,
for which we propose a suitable protocol. This approach supported a revised interpretation of two canids
and the discovery of a new felid figure from La Marche, highlighting the analytical value of optimized 3Dbased survey methodologies.

INTRODUCTION
In the context of the European Upper Paleolithic, the earliest graphic expressions appear both in parietal
art, including caves such as Chauvet, Altxerri B, and La Baume-Latrone, in rock shelters including
Castanet or Blanchard, and in portable art such as the well-known ivory statuettes from the Swabian
Jura. Evidence of graphic production throughout the following 27 millennia (ca. 40,000–13,000 cal. BP)
is therefore documented both on cave walls and on a wide range of portable supports made from
diverse materials, including hard animal materials, limestone, schist, sandstone, etc. While
archaeological remains primarily inform us about the subsistence strategies, mobility patterns, and
habitats of nomadic hunter-gatherer societies, prehistoric art constitutes a distinctive source of evidence
for exploring the cognitive, symbolic, and perceptual worlds of Upper Paleolithic societies (Clottes 2010;
Lewis-Williams 2002).These visual expressions are commonly interpreted as materializations of
complex symbolic systems, social practices, and shared cosmologies rather than mere decorative
productions. Consequently, they offer valuable insights into how these populations understood,
represented, and related to their environment.
Prehistoric art manifests in different forms, including drawings, paintings, engravings, and sculptures,
produced on both parietal (cave walls) and portable (mobile) supports. Prehistorians examine these
works within their archaeological and technical contexts in order to understand their composition,
production processes, and interpret their cultural significance. Among the successive cultural phases of
Page 2/40

Western Europe, the Magdalenian period (ca. 21,000–13,000 cal. BP) represents a peak in artistic
production. This period is characterized by an exceptional abundance and diversity of artworks, both in
terms of the supports employed, ranging from cave walls to portable objects, and the richness,
complexity, standardization and mastery in graphic conventions (Fritz 1999). Within this prolific artistic
corpus, stone engravings constitute a particularly prominent form of expression, yet their study presents
significant methodological challenges.
Survey and recording practices are fundamental and indispensable methods in the study of Paleolithic
art. They play a central role in the scientific analysis and interpretation of artistic expressions by
providing a structured framework for documenting engraved figures as well as natural surface features.
Beyond simple documentation, survey work represents an analytical process in its own right and serves
as a crucial interpretative aid, especially when dealing with complex, superimposed, or poorly legible
representations. This methodological issue is particularly salient at the renowned Magdalenian site of La
Marche (Garcia et al. 2018; Unpublished PCR report, PALEVORPIM, Merceron and Garcia 2020), which is
famous for its finely executed and highly intricate engravings on lithic supports (Mélard 2006; Pales and
Tassin de Saint Péreuse 1969).
Located in west-central France in the Vienne Valley, La Marche Cave has been the subject of
archaeological excavation from 1937 to 1942. The site has yielded a substantial assemblage of faunal
remains, adornments, and lithic productions attributed to the Middle Magdalenian period, as well as
approximately 1,500 engraved lithic supports that have contributed to its renown. Subsequent fieldwork
involving systematic screening and sieving of excavated sediments conducted between 1960 and 1993
resulted in the recovery of additional engraved slabs, plaques, and fragments, increasing the total
number of engraved supports recovered from the site to around 3,000. Numerous descriptive and
interpretive studies have been undertaken on approximately 1,600 of these engraved lithic supports
(Bourdier 2010; Fuentes 2013; Gaussein 2017a, 2017b; Lwoff 1941, 1943, 1957, 1959, 1962, 1965; ManEstier 2009; Mélard 2006; Pales and Tassin de Saint Péreuse 1969, 1976, 1981, 1989; Péricard and Lwoff
1940). Although this body of research has been extensive, many of the engravings remain unstudied due
to the extreme subtlety and visual complexity of their incised designs, which render them challenging to
document accurately. In this context, the choice of the visual medium used for analytical recording, one
that effectively links the engravings to their stone support, is a decisive factor in producing a clear,
legible, and interpretable representation upon which accurate surveys can be based.
Although investigation methods derived from the natural sciences, such as 3D digitization, are
increasingly employed in the study of rock and cave art and its context (e.g. Burrens-Carozza et al., 2011,
Jaillet et al., 2014, Robert et al., 2014, Barbuti et al., 2023), their application to portable art remains
comparatively limited, despite a few exceptions (Dutkiewicz 2025). This underutilization is often
attributed to the extreme fineness and complexity of engraved surfaces, which are sometimes
considered difficult to capture accurately using digitization techniques. However, 3D recording tools offer
significant potential due to their capacity to capture high-resolution surface relief, a feature that is
essential for the detailed analysis of engravings. Moreover, while access to artefacts housed in museum
Page 3/40

collections is often feasible for direct observation, the loan of such objects is frequently restricted for
conservation reasons. This constraint of immobility limits the possibility of conducting analyses using
fixed or heavy equipment, such as micro-measurement stations or CT scanning devices (Mélard 2010),
thereby reinforcing the need for mobile and non-invasive recording solutions.
Within this methodological framework, the present study proposes a comparative evaluation of several
3D digitization methods applied to three engraved stones from La Marche, using portable imaging tools.
This research does not intend to propose a substitute for the direct examination of archaeological
material, which is an essential and irreplaceable part of the study of Paleolithic artistic productions.
Rather, it aims to highlight the potential of 3D digitization as an analytical tool to support documentation,
and not as an ultimate result liable to replace direct examination of artistic manifestations. The selected
devices include the Artec Space Spider surface scanner and the Medit i700 intraoral scanner. For
comparative purposes, photogrammetric models of the same artefacts were also produced using a
Nikon D7200 camera. Widely used among archaeologists, this method also permits a digitization while
being affordable and transportable in collections. To assess the accuracy, limitations, and suitability of
each digitization method, the three engraved stones selected for analysis were chosen for their differing
engraving profiles: fine, moderate, and deep. In a second stage, the 3D modeling software Blender, which
has already proven effective in cave art studies (Fuentes et al. 2019; Veysset et al. 2024) was used to
enhance the legibility of the engravings on the resulting 3D models. The overall objective of this study is
to develop the most efficient, rapid, and practical workflow for producing a two-dimensional image that
can be used as a study medium, offering a clear analytical advantage over conventional photography. By
exploring the processing features offered by Blender, we seek to produce an enhancement protocol that
best meets the specific requirements of Paleolithic engraving analysis, both in terms of ease of use and
interpretive value.

MATERIAL
The engravings from La Marche are primarily characterized by a manufacturing sequence structured
around two successive stages. The first consists of a preparation of the lithic substrate, achieved
through surface scraping to obtain a homogeneous working surface (Mélard 2006). This preparatory
phase is followed by the engraving proper, during which multiple figures may be executed in a complex
manner, frequently intertwining or overlapping one another. Such intensive and multifaceted use of the
support considerably hinders the visual identification and analytical interpretation of the motifs,
especially when the engravings are particularly fine and shallow. The present study focuses on three
engraved lithic supports selected to reflect a range of incision depths, from deep to moderate and fine,
allowing for a critical assessment of the performance and limitations of the analytical approaches
employed. The supports, all made of limestone, measure approximately 5 × 5 cm. They derive from two
distinct collections at the La Marche site: the Péricard collection (nos. 979.5.2599 and 979.5.2498) and
the Bastière collection (no. 999.3.1.2).

Page 4/40

Among these three engraved plaquettes selected for study, the first has previously been identified as
bearing a leporid hindquarters (Pales and Tassin de Saint Péreuse 1976). The second one carries a
representation attributed to a canid (Mélard 2006), while the third plaquette (no. 979.5.2599) had not
been the subject of analysis prior to the present investigation. These engraved supports were selected
not only for their variable incision depth, but also for the graphic elements they bear and that may
correspond to carnivore representations. Given the relative rarity and interpretative significance of such
motifs within Upper Paleolithic iconographic assemblages, these pieces constitute particularly relevant
case studies for testing the potential of enhanced digital visualization techniques in the identification
and documentation of engraved figures. The attribution of these engraved figures to specific genus will
be presented and discussed in the result section of this study.
METHODS

I. Methodological framework
The recording of Paleolithic engravings constitutes a fundamental methodological issue in the study of
prehistoric art, as the quality of documentation directly conditions interpretative analyses. Engraved
productions are often characterized by low visual contrast, surface alteration, and complex interactions
with the natural relief of the support, making its perception highly dependent on lighting conditions,
viewing angle, and consequently, observer subjectivity. Effective recording must therefore aim to
produce readable, accurate, and reproducible representations of engraved figures in relation to their
medium while minimizing interpretative bias and physical intervention on surfaces for conservation
purpose. This requirement of producing readable “graphic” and “technic” records has long structured
recording practices in prehistoric art research (Fritz and Tosello 2007; Fuentes et al. 2019). Traditional
methods based on manual tracing and photographic documentation have proven effective for
synthesizing engraved motifs and their support (Aujoulat 1987; Groupe de Réflexion sur l’Art pariétal
Paléolithique (GRAPP) 1993; Lorblanchet 1995; Whitley 2001).
Since almost twenty years, recording strategies for Paleolithic art have progressively started to explore
the potential of digital technologies such as digitally enhanced imaging, photogrammetry, microscopy,
and three-dimensional modeling (e.g., Robert et al., 2016). Among its first uses, we should mention the
stereophotogrammetry of the Lascaux cave in 1966, the digitization of the Cosquer cave in 1994, that of
the Taillebourg cave on the Roc-aux-Sorciers site in 1999 and that of the Chauvet-Pont d'Arc Cave
entrance in 2010 (Auzanne et al. 2002; Delannoy et al. 2010; IGN 1966; Paramythioti 1996). The
development of these digital recording techniques has introduced a wide range of procedures for
documenting Upper Paleolithic art in caves and rock shelters. In particular, the digitization of cave and
shelter walls has increasingly been used as an alternative or complement to conventional photographic
recording for the documentation of engravings and paintings, especially in the case of sculpted rockshelter panels (e.g., Fuentes et al., 2019).
I.1.1. Recording in cave contexts
Page 5/40

In cave environments, digital documentation typically combines methods operating at different spatial
scales. The topographic recording of underground spaces is commonly achieved using 3D scanning
such as total station. 3D scanning techniques were introduced relatively early in cave research and
remain widely used today. These methods allow the production of accurate three-dimensional models of
cave morphology and are more convenient for topographic purposes. Conversely, at the scale of
decorated walls, photogrammetry is the most frequently employed techniques. This method has been
adopted early in rock art studies and remains widely used due to its flexibility, accessibility, and most of
all its ability to produce extremely accurate textures for its 3D models (e.g., Cantó et al., 2022; Laurent,
2024; Lorblanchet, 1979; Monney, 2018; Rivero et al., 2019; Robert et al., 2016). In most cases, 3D
scanning is primarily used to document cave geometry rather than engraved panels themselves (Jaillet
et al. 2014). However, some studies have demonstrated its potential for recording engravings in this
particular context, such as the three-dimensional documentation of Palaeolithic engravings in the Abri
du Colombier (Azéma et al. 2010) or in the Cussac cave (Feruglio et al. 2013). Although such
applications remain relatively uncommon, they illustrate that 3D scanning can occasionally contribute to
the recording of engraved surfaces when acquisition parameters allow sufficiently high spatial
resolution.
I.1.2. Recording portable art
The recording of portable art objects raises different methodological constraints, as these artefacts are
generally smaller and often require very high-resolution approaches to document fine engraved traces.
Among the most precise analytical methods available, techniques such as Focus Variation Microscopy,
micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), and microtopographic scanning allow extremely detailed
characterization of engraved relief. These approaches provide very high spatial resolution and enable
quantitative analyses of micro-morphological features, including tool marks and engraving sequences,
which have demonstrated their relevance for the study of engraved traces (S. M. Bello et al. 2013; Silvia
M. Bello et al. 2020; Gaussein 2012; Güth 2012; Mélard 2010). However, despite their analytical potential,
their use remains relatively limited in archaeological practice. These techniques typically require
specialized laboratory equipment that is difficult to transport, which may pose significant constraints
when studying artefacts held in museum collections or conservation facilities. Furthermore, the
acquisition and processing of such high-resolution datasets can involve complex and time-consuming
analytical procedures.
Reflectance Transformation Imaging (RTI) also constitutes a useful tool for the study of portable
engraved materials. By manipulating virtual lighting conditions, RTI enhances subtle surface variations
and can reveal engravings that are difficult to observe under standard illumination. This method has
been widely recognized as an efficient approach for improving the visibility of surface details and
engravings (e.g., Abergel et al., 2023; Díaz-Guardamino et al., 2015; Díaz-Guardamino and Wheatley,
2013; Porter et al., 2016). In practice, however, RTI implementation requires time and equipment.
Moreover, it does not produce a true three-dimensional model; rather, it generates two-dimensional
outputs that are not of optimal quality and precision when it comes to capturing fine engravings on
complex media (Porter et al. 2016).
Page 6/40

Photogrammetry represents another widely used method for documenting portable art. Its principal
advantage lies in its capacity to capture high-resolution surface textures, which can greatly assist in the
visualization and interpretation of engraved surfaces (Cabrelles et al. 2020; Sánchez-Hernández et al.
2026). However, the technique also presents certain limitations. Scaling accuracy is less reliable than
with other methods, and minor geometric distortions can occur depending on acquisition conditions and
reconstruction parameters. Consequently, photogrammetry is often more effective for documenting the
texture and visual appearance of engraved surfaces than for precise geometric measurements.
Laser scanning offers complementary advantages, particularly in terms of metric reliability and
geometric precision. This method enables the production of three-dimensional surface models that
integrate engraved figures within their spatial and topographic context. It allows accurate scaling and
detailed reconstruction of the overall morphology of objects, making it particularly suitable for analyzing
their geometric structure. However, compared to photogrammetry, when the laser scanner is capable of
producing a textured model, it often provides less detailed surface texture information.

I.2. Using Blender to investigate Paleolithic art
While 3D digitization offers significant opportunities regarding data recording, including morphological,
volumetric, colorimetric, and textural information, it also provides valuable possibilities for analytical
survey. Indeed, 3D models allow observations that are difficult or impossible to perform on the original
material object, such as highly flexible manipulation, scale modification, and texture enhancement. In the
case of portable Paleolithic art presenting fine engravings such as La Marche plaques, these digital
capabilities are particularly useful for revealing shallow grooves, and improving the accuracy of graphic
tracings.
Two main approaches can be used when producing surveys based on 3D models. The first consists of
performing the tracing directly on the 3D model, while the second involves generating a 2D render from
the model which is then used as a survey medium. Both of these approaches have been explored in the
context of Paleolithic art study, primarily using the software Blender. Free, open source and fully
customizable, this 3D modeling software originally intended for 3D sculpture and animation has
increasingly been adopted by archaeologists as a survey tool, amongst other uses in heritage
documentation and promotion (Casadei and Di Modica 2025; Delomosne 2022; Ministère de la Culture,
2022).
For tracing directly on 3D models, two main functionalities are commonly employed: the Grease Pencil
Tool and the Texture Paint. The Grease Pencil tool enables three-dimensional drawing directly on the
geometry and supports vector-like cartographic recording of engravings, material removals, and wall
morphology through layered thematic annotations (Barbuti et al. 2023; Fuentes et al. 2019; Unpublished
M.Sc. Thesis, Université Paris 1, Veysset 2025). On the other hand, Texture Painting relies on the creation
of texture layers on which various digital brushes can be used to draw, with the result being mapped in
real time onto the 3D mesh (Ministère de la Culture, 2022). Although these approaches have the
advantage of preserving the tridimensionality of the object, a crucial aspect in the study of Paleolithic
Page 7/40

art, they are not the most efficient tools for producing tracings. In practice, these tools can be relatively
non-ergonomic, the workspace may be difficult to navigate, and specialized brushes often need to be
created in order to adequately capture the diversity of lines and gestures produced by Paleolithic artists
(Barbuti et al. 2023; Veysset et al. 2024).
However, Blender’s capacity to enhance a 3D model’s topography by manipulating its texture, or even its
geometry, appears particularly promising for survey-assisting analysis. Surface reliefs can be visually
enhanced to increase legibility, after which an orthophotographic image can be generated and used as a
survey medium in another software, such as a digital drawing one (e.g., Krita software) or even in printed
form. Several experimental approaches have already been explored in that perspective, including the
application of the DStretch® plug-in to the model’s original texture to increase the visibility of faint
figures, the use of a “Pointiness” treatment to accumulate colors within surface depressions and thereby
highlight grooves, and the removal of textures combined with the visualization of surface normales to
emphasize topographic features. These techniques have been explored by the Centre National de
Préhistoire (CNP, research division of the French Culture Ministry), which described them in a dedicated
guide on the use of Blender for Paleolithic art documentation (Ministère de la Culture, 2022). Although
these methods are effective, they remain complex to use without a rather extensive knowledge of the
software, particularly regarding texture manipulation. For this reason, the present study aimed to develop
a simpler protocol capable of producing similarly convincing results while remaining more accessible to
users with limited experience in Blender.

II. Digitization of the plaques
While access to specimens preserved in museums is relatively feasible, it is generally complicated to
obtain loans of objects for conservation and security reasons. This constraint of immobility rules out the
possibility of analyzing artifacts using some of the most powerful equipment available, such as micro-CT
scans.
Thanks to photogrammetry and 3D scanning, it is possible to create high-precision 3D models, with
texturing if required. Their practicality and ease of use in museums enable the creation of virtual
collections that contribute to scientific research, as well as the protection and conservation of
collections and the sharing of data. In order to produce digital copies of the La Marche plaques, we used
two separate digitization technologies: photogrammetry and structured-light 3D scanning.

II.1. Digitization with photogrammetry
Photogrammetry is a well-known technology among archeologists and paleontologists (Ammirati et al.
2025; Antinozzi et al. 2022; Brady et al. 2017; Díez Díaz et al. 2021; Peter L. Falkingham et al. 2018; Peter
Lewis Falkingham 2012; Garashchenko et al. 2022; Mallison and Wings 2014; Otero et al. 2020; SánchezHernández et al. 2026). This method consists of capturing a large number of overlapping photographs of
the same object from multiple angles. The images are then processed on a dedicated software to
Page 8/40

digitally reconstruct their subject, through optical calculations that determine the spatial coordinates of
each pixel within an orthogonal spatial system. The resulting dataset forms a ‘point cloud’, which can
subsequently be converted into a ‘mesh’ (3D model composed of polygons) through additional
processing.
Photographic acquisition was performed using a reflex Nikon D7200 camera, equipped with a 24.1
megapixels sensor, and a fixed-focus Nikon 60 mm lens. Lighting was provided by a photo box with
indirect LED illumination. The engraved surfaces were photographed at three different camera angles:
30°, 70° and 90°. Using a rotating platform, images were taken from all sides of each object, resulting in
approximately 50 to 60 photographs per plaque. The photographs were then processed using the
photogrammetry software Agisoft Metashape Standard Edition. During the 3D reconstruction workflow, all
settings were set to “High" in order to obtain the highest possible level of geometric accuracy.

II.2. Digitization with structured-light scanners
The 3D scanners employed in this study are based on structured light technology, which operates by
projecting patterned light onto the surface of an object. The resulting deformation of these projected
patterns is recorded by the scanner in the form of distance measurements. These measurements are
then processed to reconstruct the external shape of the subject in the form of a 3D mesh.
Two different devices were used for the digitization process. First, a conventional 3D scanner model, the
Artec Space Spider 1 was employed. This scanner is widely used in archaeology and paleontology
(Ammirati et al. 2025; Diara 2023; Göldner et al. 2022). In addition, an intraoral scanner, the Medit i700
was tested. Intraoral scanners, primarily designed for dental applications, are increasingly being adopted
in other scientific fields, particularly paleontology (Surault et al. 2025; Towle et al. 2024). Their reported
performance and ease of use demonstrates strong potential and reliability for high-resolution scanning,
which motivated their evaluation for the digitization of La Marche plaques.
ARTEC SPACE SPIDER 1 (2015, Artec3D, Luxembourg)
The Artec Space Spider 1 is a portable 3D scanner designed to digitize objects ranging from a few
centimeters to several decimeters in size. Thanks to its blue LED light source, it can capture fine details
with a resolution of 0.1 mm and an accuracy of 0.05 mm. Its acquisition speed is moderate, reaching up
to 7.5 frames per second (fps). One advantage of this type of scanner is that the procedure does not
require the use of reflective surface targets placed on or around the object. This scanner is also capable
of capturing and rendering the texture of the object. The reconstruction software Artec Studio 17
Professional was used to generate the 3D models of the plaques with a maximum resolution of 100 µm.
Regarding the scanning conditions with the Artec Space Spider, no preparation of the plaques was
required. The engraved surface was stabilized using a rotating platform and scanned under ambient
lighting conditions.
MEDIT i700 (2021, Medit, South Korea)
Page 9/40

The Medit i700 is an intraoral scanner used in digital dentistry, where is has demonstrated high
performance (Amornvit et al. 2021; Borbola et al. 2023; Ciocan et al. 2024; El-Refay et al. 2025; Falih and
Majeed 2023; Rotar et al. 2022; Towle et al. 2024; Winkler and Gkantidis 2020). It is designed for mobility
and ease of handling, with compact dimensions (248 x 44 x 47.4 mm) and a lightweight material (245 g).
The device is equipped with a mirror scanning tip; in this study, an adult size tip with a 2x2 cm mirror was
used. The Medit i700 operates at a high acquisition rate of up to 70 fps, and the reconstruction process
is streamlined through the scanner software MeditLink v. 3.4.4, with an accuracy of up to 11 µm.
Two primary scanning modes are available. The first is dental mode, optimized for capturing highly
detailed meshes of individual teeth or dental arches The second is face mode, designed for scanning
larger surfaces, but producing less detailed and complete meshes than dental mode. Given the
objectives of this study the plaques were scanned in dental mode in order to reproduce the fine details of
the engravings as accurately as possible.
Lighting conditions were identical to those used for the Artec Space Spider, no preparation of the
plaques was required, and each scan was performed in a single acquisition session while the plaque
was held by hand.

III.3. Digitization and comparison
The scanning process focused on the area of interest for the study, namely the upper surface of the
plaques featuring the engraved lines. The 3D surfaces generated by the scanners were saved in their raw
form in .PLY and .OBJ formats. For the photogrammetric method, only the .OBJ format was exported.
The resulting 3D surfaces were subsequently standardized to facilitate comparison between methods
and ensure the consistency of the results obtained. Several mesh homogenization manipulations were
therefore performed for each surface using Geomagic Wrap 2021 (3D Systems). First, the raw meshes
were corrected to remove potential artifacts. This was achieved using the Mesh Doctor function to
remove and correct any scanning artifacts (non-manifold edges, self-intersections, small components,
small tunnels, small holes). Comparative analysis was conducted in two steps. First, the geometric
accuracy of the 3D surfaces was assessed using Geomagic Wrap, a software commonly employed to
perform comparative 3D analyses (Adams et al. 2015; Garashchenko et al. 2022). For each plaque, the
meshes obtained using the three scanning techniques were superimposed using manual marker-based
registration, followed by automated global registration to refine the alignment. The engraved surfaces
were then cropped identically using the Polyline Selection Tool. Because no reference model was
available, a true accuracy assessment could not be performed. Ideally, the plaque would have been
scanned using micro-CT to provide a reference dataset. In the absence of such a reference model, the
meshes were therefore compared pairwise. The resulting models were then compared using Geomagic
Wrap with 3D analysis tools. Surface deviation between the meshes obtained by the three scanning
techniques were calculated according to maximum deviations of 50 µm for plaque no. 999.3.1.2 and 100
µm for plaques nos. 979.5.2498 and 979.5.2599. This approach allowed the accuracy and precision of
the meshes to be evaluated in view of their suitability for detailed visual analysis of the engravings.
Page 10/40

A qualitative comparison of the meshes was also conducted. Visual inspection of the surfaces enabled
verification of the depth of the engravings and the smoothness of the 3D reconstructions. This analysis
was supplemented by an examination of the differences between the polygon surfaces and the number
of triangles composing the meshes. Finally, curvature maps were generated to assess the quality of the
reconstructed relief and to evaluate the capacity of each digitization technique to reproduce the fine
morphology of the engraved grooves.

III. Visual enhancement with Blender

III.1. Playing with texture and light: exploring the viewport
display properties
In the second phase of our project, we aimed to improve the readability of the fine engravings of La
Marche plaques by manipulating the 3D models texture and shape. After generating the 3D models,
using the different digitizing technologies, the resulting meshes were imported into a single Blender file
in order to conduct a series of visualization experiments. Our initial approach focused on modifying the
models’ texture to visually accentuate their surfaces’ variations, and consequently increase the visibility
of the engraved lines. Because photographic textures are relatively difficult to modify, these were
removed, allowing us to concentrate on the possibilities offered by computer-generated textures.
To achieve this, we used the options presented by the Viewport Shading panel (Fig. 2), changing the
shading mode from Material Preview to Solid view . The objective was to test the different parameters
available in this mode: rather than creating a fully customized material with the desired properties, which
is a complex and time-consuming process requiring specialized expertise, we sought to determine
whether appropriate combination of existing settings could increase the readability of engravings
through simple adjustments.
The first parameter proposed of the Solid view shading mode is Lighting, which determines how
illumination is computed by the viewport and thus, how the surface of the 3D models responds to light
within the workspace). Among the three settings available, particular attention was given to the
application of MatCaps, or Material Capture. These are a type of predefined surface material that quickly
simulates various physical properties and lighting directly in Solid view, without requiring the application
of a material onto the mesh or the creation of a lighting set-up. Mostly used in 3D sculpting, MatCaps
provide a rapid and simple method for evaluating how a shape responds to material properties and light
conditions prior to rendering. Blender includes more than twenty MatCaps ready to use in the viewport
shading options panel, and also allows users to import additional ones. For this study, several MatCaps
with different physical properties were tested in order to determine whether some of them increased the
visibility of engravings.
Two additional parameters in the Options settings were also explored:
Page 11/40

The Shadow option, making the meshes cast sharp shadows instead of diffused ones. It is possible
to adjust the shadow’s intensity, its direction, angle and focus.
The Cavity option, highlighting the Ridges (convex areas) and Valleys (concave areas) of the meshes’
geometry. Blender provides three computational methods for this effect, and the Ridges and Valleys’
intensity can be adjusted independently.

III.2. Accentuating topography with constrained
transformations
Being a 3D modeling software, Blender also allows direct manipulation of mesh shape and position in
space. To achieve this, three main transformations can be operated: translation (Move), rotation (Rotate)
and scaling (Scale). These operations can be conducted simultaneously along the three axes of the
workspace, or constrained to one or two selected axes. In this study, we used constrained scaling
transformations to enhance the engravings of the plaques. The principle consisted of exaggerating the
apparent depth of the engravings by stretching the 3D models on a lateral axis using the Scale tool
(Fig. 3), and subsequently observing them from a frontal view without perspective deformation. This
procedure produces images in which the overall proportions of the plaques appear unchanged, while
grooves appear deeper and therefore easier to interpret.
This deforming operation can be applied without significantly altering the perceived geometry of the
object only because the engravings are located on a relatively flat surface. It is thanks to that
topographic condition, that when the model is observed from the front without perspective distortion, the
object keep its initial geometry. Such an approach would not be applicable on a curved surface, where
geometric distortion could significantly alter the perception of the original morphology.
This operation required to position the mesh orthogonally according to the axes of the workspace, using
the Move and Rotate tools. The engraved surface was positioned within the (X, Z) plane, with the y axis
corresponding to the plaque thickness. All scaling deformations were thus conducted strictly along the y
axis. The 3D model was then observed from the (X, Z) plane locked in orthogonal view.

I.1. 3D surfaces accuracy control

This evaluation was carried out using distance maps and measurements of the deviation between the
3D surfaces through a pairwise comparison of the digitizing methodologies (Fig. 4; Table. 1). These
deviations are represented by a color map of deviation signed values on the reference surface
(deviations’ positive and negative maximum points) and two modes of calculations were applied; the
Page 12/40

standard deviation and the Root Mean Square (RMS) operated by the software Geomagic Wrap 2021. The
3D meshes of plaque no. 999.3.1.2 obtained with the three scanning methods are particularly consistent
in terms of accuracy with less than 0.020 mm distance deviation for each inter-technique comparison
analysis. This high level of agreement can be partly explained by the pronounced flatness of the plaque.
The meshes generated by the Artec Space Spider and Medit i700 display very similar surface reliefs, with
clearly defined grooves. In contrast, the surfaces generated by photogrammetry exhibit greater
deviations compared to the other two techniques and show more pronounced differences in mesh
structure, especially within the engraved areas, indicating low accuracy of the original relief
reconstruction.
The accuracy of the surfaces obtained for plaques nos. 979.5.2498 and 979.5.2599 remains particularly
satisfactory, with respective distance deviations ranging from 0.034 to 0.041 mm and 0.026 to 0.042
mm. These results confirm the overall accuracy of the 3D surfaces. However, the same pattern can be
observed for plaque no. 999.3.1.2, namely meshes produced by the Artec Space Spider and the Medit
i700 show very limited deviations, whereas photogrammetric meshes display larger spaces within
recessed structures. This indicates a less precise topographic reproduction of the engravings compared
to structured light scanners.

I.2. Mesh comparison
The raw meshes of the plaques generated by the Medit i700 and Artec Space Spider scanners are
broadly similar in terms of number of polygons, averaging several hundred thousand triangles (Fig. 5);
the surfaces obtained by these technologies clearly show the engravings with great finesse of
representation. On the other hand, the mesh generated by photogrammetry is significantly less dense,
consistently containing less than 100,000 triangles. This implies a surface made up of large triangles,
thus greatly smoothing the relief. For the Artec Space Spider, the raw mesh shows fairly marked
engraved lines on each plaque, including the finest ones. The flat surfaces are also very detailed,
demonstrating the high resolution of the topographic relief details. However, they interfere with the
overall readability of the structures of interest. This interpretation is amplified by the analysis of the
curvature maps produced on Geomagic Wrap 2021 (Fig. 6). The relatively flat areas of the plaques
display numerous curvatures which significantly reduces the visibility of the engravings. For example, the
Page 14/40

engraved shape of the canid (plaque no. 999.3.1.2) is not visible. The upper part of the engraving on
plaque no. 979.5.2498 is equally difficult to read. In contrast, the surfaces generated by the Medit i700
display more visible grooves on these two plaques. This is accentuated by the slight automatic
smoothing of flat surfaces performed by MeditLink. This effect tends to round off the peaks and smooth
the flat areas, making the surface less rough and therefore more visually homogeneous. The contours of
the engraving are better highlighted, and can thus be seen more clearly. For the photogrammetric
models, the relief has the widest striations, which are relatively visible, but the finest engravings are not
always represented as they may appear completely smoothed out. Adjacent surfaces are also very
flattened. These observations are also found on plaque no. 979.5.2599, but due to its particularly fine
and shallow structures, engravings remain difficult to interpret even on the surface generated by the
Medit i700.

II. Visual enhancement results

II.1. Experimenting with MatCaps
MatCaps simulate different physical properties (or shaders) that can be applied to a 3D surface to
imitate the behavior of real material. Three principal parameters characterize these properties:
-The base color: which defines the overall color of the material;
-The roughness: which determines whether a material’s surface is matte or reflective;
-The metallic: which controls the intensity of the specular reflection of the material, tinted by the
base color.
In addition to these material characteristics, MatCaps also simulate the properties of a lighting set-up,
including the color of the light(s), their power, diffusion, radius, direction, etc. As a result, Blender’s
MatCaps can provide a wide range of combinations of material and lighting properties.
Our initial approach consisted of testing highly reflective MatCaps (i.e., with a low roughness and/or high
metallic), in order to use the reflections to emphasize surface relief while rotating the models and
varying light orientation. However, this approach proved to be both inefficient and impractical because of
scattered highlights that interfere with the visual interpretation of the engravings. In contrast, matte
MatCaps appear more suitable. By diffusing light more evenly across the surface, they prevent
reflections from accumulating on minor surface variations. This improves the readability of the
engravings which is thus less disturbed by bright reflections.
Regarding color selection, light values are the most appropriate for visual interpretation, as they naturally
enhance grooves visibility by contrasting with the shadows concentrating in them. Neutral hues with low
saturations improve readability as well, as they attract less visual attention than more vivid colors. On the
basis of these observations, light grey or beige MatCaps, with high roughness and low metallic values
provide particularly suitable visualization conditions (Fig. 7).

II.2. Relevant shading options: Shadow and Cavity
In addition to the MatCaps tests, other visualization options offered by the viewport shading panel were
explored. Among these, two parameters proved particularly relevant for the objectives of this study: the
Shadow and Cavity options (Fig. 8).
The sharp shadows cast by the Shadow option emphasize models’ Ridges while darkening their grooves,
in a manner comparable to the effect produced by a low-angled light that would accentuate an object’s
relief. Once the option is activated, the intensity of the effect can be adjusted using a slider ranging from
0 to 1. This setting enhances the visual perception on an object’s topography; however, excessively high
values tend to produce overly pronounced shadows that may obscure fine details rather than reveal
them.
Among the tested parameters, the Cavity option proved to be the most effective for improving the
readability of engraved features. Indeed, this option modifies the texture’s value according to its
topography: the convex areas (Ridges) appear lighter, while the concave arears (Valleys) appear darker.
Two range sliders (Ridges and Valleys) allow the intensity of each effect to be adjusted within a range of
0 to 2, making it possible to differentially emphasize convexities and depressions depending on the
desired visualization outcome.
To compute its effect, the option provides three methods: World for slower yet more realistic results,
Screen for a faster solution that doesn’t take into account the size of the Ridges and Valleys, and Both
which combines the two computing methods. In practice, the Screen mode produces a sharper visual
contrast that enhances the legibility of fine surfaces features.
In order to enhance the readability of fine surface topography, the Cavity option available in Solid Mode
provides the most effective option. Using the Screen computational method and setting both the Ridges
and Valleys parameters to their maximum values produces the strongest contrast between convex and
concave features. For additional enhancement of the mesh’s topography, the Shadow option can also be
activated with a low to moderate intensity.

II.3. Constrained scaling, an operation to use with
moderation
Constrained scaling is an effective operation for accentuating engraved grooves (Fig. 9), provided that
the transformation is applied with moderation. Indeed, excessive amplification of details can rapidly
exaggerate minor surface variations, and thereby disrupting the overall reading of the surface.
Consequently, such mesh deformation must be applied cautiously and only to slightly enhance the visual
effect produced by the Cavity option.
Page 16/40

III. Towards new identifications
A comparison of the results obtained through photogrammetry, Artec and Medit scanning enabled the
identification of the most suitable method for producing recording bases according to the requirements
of this study. For each of the three engraved plaquettes, a high-resolution 3D model generated using the
Medit i700 intraoral scanner, which provided the most satisfactory results in terms of surface detail and
engraving visibility, was selected as the reference model. These models were subsequently processed in
Blender to enhance the legibility of the engraved lines, following the visualization protocol described
above. The enhanced 3D surfaces were then used to derive two-dimensional recording supports that
served as the basis for the production of analytical and interpretative tracings of the engraved figures
conducted on the software Krita (v. 5.2.3). This free and open-source digital drawing software is
designed for illustration and graphic editing. Widely used for the creation and refinement of digital
drawings thanks to its advanced brush engine and layer-based workflow, we produced the analytical
surveys on this particular software also for its compatibility with graphic tablets The tracings presented
here therefore constitute a selective graphic record produced on Krita and derived from this combined
workflow of high-resolution 3D digitization and visualization enhancement, enabling a clearer
identification and documentation of the engraved motifs (Fig. 10).
The representation of the wolf at La Marche exhibits the principal morphological traits commonly
associated with Paleolithic depictions of canids, including a rectangular head, an elongated muzzle, a
marked frontal stop, long pointed ears, visible claws, and the suggestion of a dense coat (Mélard and
Cogné 2015). These diagnostic features are particularly evident on slab no. 999.3.1.2, previously
analyzed by N. Mélard (Mélard 2006). In this study, the author further proposed that the animal’s seated
posture, combined with a raised head, might evoke behavioral attitudes such as submission or an
invitation to play. Such an interpretation potentially situates the figure within a broader context of
behavioral signaling, thus opening up to a graphic sign of wolf domestication recognized in the fauna
register since ca. 14,000 cal. BP (Street et al. 2015; Thalmann et al. 2013).
Our re-examination of plaque no. 979.5.2498 has led to a reassessment of another figure previously
interpreted as the hindquarters of a leporid located to the left of a possible human head (Pales and
Tassin de Saint Péreuse 1976). The figure, almost carved in low bas-relief on the left side of the plaque,
presents several morphological similarities to the wolf represented on slab no. 999.3.1.2. These
similarities raise the possibility that the figure instead corresponds to the forequarters of a canid
depicted in right profile. The figure includes a thoracic line followed by two elongated and closely joined
forelimbs. A clear flexion at the humero-ulnar joint is indicated, and the distal extremity forms a rounded
paw. On the right limb in particular, five potential digits appear to be suggested by short curved incisions
that segment the extremity of the paw. This anatomical treatment closely parallels that observed in the
wolf representation mentioned above. Taken together, these morphological features support the
hypothesis that the figure may represent a second canid in a sitting position within the artistic corpus of
La Marche.

Page 17/40

Regarding plate no. 979.5.2599 (Fig. 10), the engraved figure can be interpreted as a felid on the basis of
the diagnostic criteria established for the identification of this group of carnivores in parietal and
portable art (Gillard 2018). The contours of the animal are rendered through the superimposition of
several engraved lines, a graphic convention frequently observed in the artistic corpus of La Marche. The
figure depicts a head in right profile, as well as a dorsal and a thoracic line. These lines continue toward
the left and lower-left edges of the slab, where they disappear beyond the preserved edges. The
morphology of the head is characterized by a relatively quadrangular outline, with a short, squared
muzzle and a robust neck, features consistent with felid representations in Upper Paleolithic graphic
assemblages. Several sensory and anatomical details further support this attribution. The eye is
depicted as a finely engraved oval, from which a slightly curved line extends posteriorly at the level of the
mandible angle. The ears are also clearly indicated: they are small and rounded, with the left ear
positioned slightly behind the right, suggesting an attempt to convey depth through a perspective effect.
A particularly noteworthy anatomical feature concerns the treatment of the right ear, which is
surmounted by three slightly curved lines that increase progressively in length from posterior to anterior.
These elements may correspond to the depiction of ear tufts, a characteristic that could suggest a
taxonomic attribution to the lynx. This interpretation is further reinforced by the graphic convention of
the eye from which a backward-extending line originates, a feature potentially associated with the
pattern observed on the fur of Eurasian lynxes.

DISCUSSION
I. 3D digitization tools comparisons
It is well established that both the morphological characteristics of an object and the environmental
conditions during acquisition influence the quality of 3D scanning (Ammirati, 2025). These effects are
further conditioned by the type of scanner employed, as each device is designed and optimized for
specific categories of objects. The Medit i700 intraoral scanner, for example, was developed primarily for
the in vivo scanning of dental arches. Consequently, it provides simplified ergonomics for clinicians and
high acquisition speeds to maximize patient comfort.
In addition, the ultimate objective of a study plays a decisive role in the evaluation of the resulting 3D
surface. In the present research, which aims to use 3D technologies to improve the interpretation of
engravings, the priority is to enhance the visual perception of engraved lines rather than to maximize
geometric accuracy. The use of different scanning techniques in this study therefore makes it possible
to assess their respective advantages and limitations for this specific type of archaeological material.
Photogrammetry is often the most widely used technique in archaeology, largely because of its
accessibility in terms of equipment. However, this reputation must be contextualized, as the quality of
the results strongly depends on the technical specifications of the camera employed. In particular, the
focal length of the lens and the size of the sensor play a crucial role in determining the quality of the final
model. A focal length of approximately 50 mm is generally recommended because it most closely
Page 18/40

approximates human vision, minimizes optical distortion, and provides sufficient depth of field to
produce uniformly sharp images (Delomosne 2022).
Such characteristics are essential for capturing very fine surface details. Likewise, sensor size
significantly affects the accuracy of the reconstructed model, as it directly relates to the number of
pixels recorded in each photograph. Lighting conditions also represent a critical parameter: illumination
should be uniform and diffuse, producing minimal shadows. These conditions are rarely achieved with
ambient lighting alone and often require dedicated equipment such as a photo box.
Although the Artec Space Spider scanner generated more detailed and geometrically accurate 3D
surfaces in terms of topographic relief, the Medit i700 intraoral scanner produced a more homogeneous
and visually legible surface, thereby highlighting the areas of interest more effectively.
This characteristic is particularly noteworthy given that the scanner is lightweight, highly ergonomic, and
easy to operate. Its reconstruction software incorporates numerous automated features, and acquisition
is especially rapid (approximately 50 fps on average for the plaques). While the performance of the
Medit i700 in dentistry is well documented, limitations arise when it is used outside its intended field,
depending on the morphology and size of the objects being scanned. This type of scanner is generally
recommended for small objects, ideally within the centimeter scale. From this perspective, the La
Marche plaques are well suited to this device. Moreover, it is known that the Medit i700 encounters
difficulties when scanning flat and highly smooth surfaces (Surault et al. 2025). Two of the plaques
included in this study (nos. 999.3.1.2 and 979.5.2599) display precisely these challenging characteristics
due to their thin and flat morphology. The first objective was therefore to evaluate the scanning potential
of this device for this particular category of archaeological objects. The acquisition of the upper
surfaces of these plaques, namely the engraved faces, was generally achievable. However, the scanner
occasionally lost tracking during acquisition, particularly for plaque no. 999.3.1.2, whose surface is
extremely smooth and uniform, lacking any significant roughness. The scanner’s difficulty in capturing
flat surfaces exceeding approximately 3 cm x 3 cm was mitigated by continuously scanning the more
angular edges of the plaque, gradually progressing toward the central area.

II. Visual enhancement
Page 20/40

To facilitate the interpretation of engraved surfaces, one of the primary objectives of this study was to
exploit the 3D modeling capabilities of Blender, particularly through texture manipulation. As this
software can be difficult to use, partly due to its numerous settings and functionalities, our aim was to
develop a method capable of producing convincing results without relying on complex operations. The
purpose was to ensure that the protocol could be implemented easily by users with only basic
experience in Blender. Consequently, we designed a workflow that relies exclusively on the Layout
workspace, one of the eleven workspaces available in the software, while minimizing the number of
operations required and avoiding the need to install additional add-ons.
The configuration of Viewport Shading parameters, combined with constrained scaling operations,
constitutes a visual enhancement procedure that is particularly effective for engraved supports including
those presenting fine engravings. In only a few steps, this approach significantly increases the visibility
of surface grooves. Using the Viewport Render function, high-resolution images can then be generated
rapidly and subsequently used as background references for digital tracing in drawing software.
Furthermore, due to its rapid execution, this protocol appears particularly well suited for the processing
of large datasets such as the corpus of La Marche plaques.

III. Carnivores at La Marche: Corpus review and perspectives
The application of the methodological framework developed in this study has led to the identification of
a previously unrecognized felid representation at the site of La Marche. This finding contributes to the
expansion of the corpus of carnivore figures already documented at the site and highlights the
interpretative potential of renewed analytical approaches to engraved Paleolithic supports. Carnivore
representations are comparatively rare within the broader iconographic bestiary of Upper Paleolithic art
(Cattelain et al. 2018; Pales and Tassin de Saint Péreuse 1969). Quantitative assessments of cave art
assemblages indicate that felids and ursids account for only a small proportion of known animal figures,
approximately 2.8% and 1.4% respectively, and are generally considered more characteristic of earlier
phases of the Upper Paleolithic, particularly the Aurignacian and Gravettian periods (Clottes and Azéma
2005; Lorblanchet 2010; Sauvet 2019).
In this broader context, the situation observed at La Marche appears atypical. The frequency of carnivore
depictions at the site exceeds that of bovids (12 representations) and ovicaprids (9 representations),
animal categories that are otherwise among the most recurrent themes during the Magdalenian (Sauvet
2019). This apparent inversion of the dominant iconographic hierarchy raises questions regarding the
specific role attributed to carnivores within the symbolic system expressed at the site.
With the addition of the newly identified felid figure, as well as a potential depiction of a canid
forequarter, the corpus of carnivore representations at La Marche now comprises 17 felids, 8 ursids and
2 canids (Fuentes 2020; Man-Estier 2009; Mélard 2006; Pales and Tassin de Saint Péreuse 1969). Within
this assemblage, felids appear particularly prominent, suggesting that these animals may have held a
distinctive position within the iconography of the site and may reflect a specific symbolic meaning and
Page 21/40

significance within these Magdalenian societies. While caution is required when attempting to infer
symbolic meanings from quantitative patterns alone, the recurrence of these figures nonetheless
suggests that carnivores may have played a more central role in the visual and symbolic landscape of La
Marche than is typically observed in Magdalenian art.
Beyond the iconographic implications of this discovery, the present study also underscores the analytical
value of integrating high-resolution digital acquisition and visualization techniques into the study of
Paleolithic engravings. The digitization of engraved lithic supports using the Medit i700 intraoral scanner,
combined with enhanced rendering manipulation in Blender, allows for the detailed examination of
surfaces and substantially improves the readability of extremely thin engravings. The engraved plaquette
no. 979.5.2599 bearing the newly identified felid illustrates particularly well the capacity of this approach
to reveal graphic elements that remain difficult to perceive through conventional observation alone.
Nevertheless, despite the advantages offered by digital enhancement, such approaches cannot
substitute for direct examination of the archaeological material. The physical and direct analysis of
artistic productions remains essential for assessing engraving techniques, surface preservation,
taphonomic alterations and the sequence of graphic interventions. Digital models should therefore be
regarded not as replacements for traditional analytical practices, but rather as complementary tools
capable of enhancing the documentation, visualization and interpretative potential of engraved
Paleolithic supports. When integrated within a broader analytical protocol that combines direct
observation and digital analysis, such methodologies open new perspectives for the identification,
documentation and interpretation of complex graphic productions of Paleolithic engraved assemblages.

CONCLUSION
This comparative study enabled us to identify the Medit i700 intraoral scanner as the most suitable
digitization tool for the objectives of our research, both in terms of accuracy and practical efficiency. This
portable device proved particularly effective in reproducing engraved lines, including those that are
extremely fine or difficult to discern, while incorporating a balanced auto-smoothing process that
removes minor surface irregularities likely to hinder overall legibility. Furthermore, the 3D visual
enhancement tests led us to consider the tools offered by Blender as a set of powerful and flexible
features for bringing out the contrasts of the engraved lines and generating the most reliable visual
support for analytical survey work. Combined, these digitization and visualization procedures produced
images of sufficient quality to support a revised interpretation of two canid figures as well as a
previously unrecognized felid representation from the site of La Marche.
