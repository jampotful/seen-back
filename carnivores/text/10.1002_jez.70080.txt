# Experimental Assessment of the Effects of Substrate Choice on the Duration of Chemical Signals in Fecal Scent‐Marks of Free‐Ranging Iberian Wolves

## Abstract
ABSTRACTChemical communication, typically based on feces, urine, and glandular secretions, often deposited as substrate scent marks, plays a key role in social organization and communication among many mammals, especially carnivores. This study assesses experimentally whether the type of substrate chosen for fecal deposition and the temperature reached by these substrates influence the persistence of volatile compounds in feces of the Iberian wolf (Canis lupus signatus), considering their role in chemical communication. Fresh fecal scats collected in the wild were divided into equal fragments and positioned in an experimental setup on four different types of substrates exposed to the natural environment. Thereafter, we collected small fecal samples from each fragment at seven different time intervals since the initial deposition and analyzed their lipophilic chemical composition using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). Variations in the temperature of each substrate were also monitored. The number of compounds in feces decreased with time since deposition and depended on the types of substrates. However, substrate temperature only influenced the persistence of some chemical compounds. These results suggest that when wolves select a substrate to deposit feces, enhancing visual cues (i.e., the different visual conspicuity of feces in different substrates) may play a more important role in fecal marking than trying to increase the persistence of the chemical signal. Overall, our study may help to enhance the understanding of fecal marking behavior in the Iberian wolf, especially regarding the interplay between visual and chemical signaling.Keywords: Canis lupus signatus, Carnivora, chemical communication, fecal marking, feces, volatile compounds

## Introduction
Organisms can communicate using chemical compounds, sounds, visual signals, electric and thermic signals, and movements (Bradbury and Vehrencamp 2011). They can use one sensory channel or a combination of all of these channels, which makes animal communication very rich and variable (Hickman et al. 2020). Many of these forms of communication were frequently studied in the past. However, chemical communication received less attention, likely due to the difficulty in analysis until advancements in microanalytical chemistry methods were made (Wyatt 2014).
Chemical communication relies on volatile compounds released by organisms, which are detected by others, eliciting responses (Eisenberg and Kleiman 1972; Muller‐Schwarze 2006). Vertebrates use chemical signals in various contexts (Muller‐Schwarze 2006; Wyatt 2014), making communication highly variable and crucial for social organization, inter‐ and intraspecific communication, and sexual selection (Alberts 1992; Wyatt 2014). Many chemical compounds are often present in feces, urine, and glandular secretions and have different functions (Alberts 1992; Barja et al. 2004, 2005; Wyatt 2014). For social species, particularly carnivores (Gittleman 1989), scent marking is crucial for reaffirming resource locations (Cafazzo et al. 2012; Piñeiro and Barja 2015), delimiting territories (Cafazzo et al. 2012; Piñeiro and Barja 2012), signaling social status (Barja et al. 2008), and conveying health or reproductive status (Drea et al. 2002; Barja et al. 2011, 2023). Numerous canid species, such as wolves, utilize feces as a chemical signal (Peters and Mech 1975; Asa et al. 1985a; Vilà et al. 1994; Barja et al. 2004; Barja 2008, 2009).
Wolves (Canis lupus [C. lupus]) are heavily territorial species that live in dominance hierarchies. These packs are formed by a reproductive pair (the highest‐ranked individuals), and the offspring of the breeding pair from several litters (Mech 1999; Barja 2009). This reproductive pair is the only one who shows marking behavior with urine (Peters and Mech 1975; Rothman and Mech 1979), feces (Vilà et al. 1994; Barja et al. 2008, 2023), scratches (Peters and Mech 1975; Barja 2001) and glandular secretions (Asa et al. 1985a; Raymer et al. 1985), through which they signal their social status to the rest of the group members (Gittleman 1989; Barja et al. 2023). Thus, scents marking is associated with dominance, spatial orientation, foraging efficiency, territory maintenance, and pair bounding (Peters and Mech 1975; Rothman and Mech 1979; Vilà et al. 1994; Barja et al. 2008).
Studies show that wolves often mark the same trail intersections (Vilà et al. 1994; Barja et al. 2004), being attracted to the scent of previous scats, which leads them to spend more time investigating these “high‐scent concentration” points and depositing their own scat (Vilà et al. 1994; Barja et al. 2004). Early studies suggested that feces were less important than urine for chemical communication due to the lack of a stereotyped posture during defecation (Peters and Mech 1975; Asa et al. 1985a). However, it has been proven that feces are also an important part in wolves' communication (Vilà et al. 1994; Barja 2001, 2009; Barja et al. 2004, 2008).
It is challenging to differentiate normal excretion and excretion with an intended function in communication, but we can consider fecal signals as those intentionally placed on conspicuous and elevated substrates (such as plants) to enhance their marking function (Peters and Mech 1975; Rothman and Mech 1979; Vilà et al. 1994; Barja 2009), aggregated in specific locations (such as crossroads), where feces can be easily detected (Vilà et al. 1994; Barja et al. 2004), scratched by the same wolf (depositing secretions from the interdigital glands) (Peters and Mech 1975; Vilà et al. 1994; Barja et al. 2005), and placed repeatedly on the same locations already used by the wolf to deposit scats or glandular secretions (Asa et al. 1985b).
Numerous studies have confirmed that the function of feces as chemical signals lies in the great number and diversity of volatile compounds they contain (Raymer et al. 1985; Barja et al. 2023). Wolve's feces include, among others, high proportions of aromatic organic heterocyclic compounds with benzene rings, aldehydes, low‐weight fatty acids and alcohols, all of which are strong odoriferous compounds (Martín et al. 2010). Some of these volatile compounds are secreted by the anal sacs and then incorporated into the feces during defecation (Barja et al. 2004). However, numerous highly volatile compounds present in wolf anal sac secretions are absent in feces (Raymer et al. 1984, 1985; Martín et al. 2010), with anal secretions appearing in < 10% of the feces (Asa et al. 1985a; Asa et al. 1985b). Voluntary control over expulsion results in variable volatile profiles in feces, which may explain their role in conveying different messages and individual recognition (Raymer et al. 1985).
Although nowadays we are able to identify volatile compounds in scent marks, we still do not fully understand whether the physical characteristics of the substrates chosen to deposit these scent marks may affect the persistence of compounds involved in marking behavior. The aim of this study was to understand the factors influencing the duration and effectiveness of feces as chemical signals in the Iberian wolf (Canis lupus signatus [C. lupus signatus]). We hypothesized that the time and temperature that the substrates reach affect how long the volatiles remain active and thus, these aspects should influence the choice of marking location. In other words, if the purpose is territorial marking, wolves would probably select cooler substrates to ensure scent marks last longer and reduce the need for frequent remarking. Specifically, in this study we address the following questions: (1) Do the volatile compounds in the fecal scent‐mark of adult wolves differ in presence and abundance depending on the exposure time since deposition? (2) Do the presence and abundance of compounds in feces vary depending on the type of substrate on which they were deposited? (3) Do the temperature differences in each substrate affect the presence, abundance, and longevity of volatile compounds in the feces? If we understand how these variables influence the longevity and effectiveness of scent signals, we could provide insights into species‐specific communication strategies.

## Material and Methods

## Study Area
We carried out this experiment in a siliceous metamorphic mountain area in northwest Spain (Galicia), Os Montes do Invernadeiro Natural Park (42°07′55.0″ N 7°19′18.0″ W) and surrounding areas. Montes do Invernadeiro is a protected area of ~6700 ha (Rodríguez‐Guitián et al. 2020) that has a continental climate, with hot summers and cold winters, a mean annual rainfall that exceeds 2000 mm and a very short dry period (Carril 2009).
The flora is diverse and is composed of scrubland (Erica australis, Pterospartum tridentatum [P. tridentatum], Cistus lasianthum), native Atlantic deciduous forests relicts (Quercus robur, Betula celtiberica, Ilex aquifolium, and Sorbus aucuparia), and extended plantations of Scots pines (Pinus sylvestris) (Rodríguez‐Guitián et al. 2020). This area harbors a high density of wild ungulates (Cervus elaphus, Capreolus capreolus, Sus scrofa) and carnivores (Meles meles, Genetta genetta, Felis silvestris, Vulpes vulpes, Martes martes), including one of the highest densities of wolves (C. lupus signatus) in the Iberian Peninsula (Barja 2001; Espartosa et al. 2024).

## Collection of Fecal Samples and Experiment Design
We collected fresh scats deposited along forest tracks and firebreaks by different wild adult individual wolves from three wolf‐breeding groups. Given that all samples were collected in summer (August 2010), we assumed that adverse weather conditions did not significantly affect the study.
To differentiate adult wolf feces from other sympatric carnivores, such as the red fox, or even from wolf pups, we applied size and shape criteria, excluding fecal samples smaller than 20 cm × 2.5 cm (length × diameter). In addition, ongoing camera trap studies allowed us to rule out the possibility that the feces belonged to other species. To ensure that the feces were from different groups of wolves, we had previously located the rendezvous sites, areas where the groups are clearly distinguishable and defend their territories without overlap (Mech and Boitani 2010). Although specific individuals responsible for the scats could not be identified, we minimized pseudoreplication and potential bias by monitoring three wolf breeding groups, which consisted of 6–14 individuals, as confirmed through direct observations, as well as camera trap data.
To locate very fresh scats, forest tracks and firebreaks, which serve as transit paths for wolves (Vilà et al. 1994; Barja 2001; Barja et al. 2004), were surveyed twice daily, at dusk and dawn, during the wolves' peak activity periods (Mech and Boitani 2010). During July and September, we inspected by foot and using an off‐road vehicle (at a speed of 10 km/h), these transects. To prevent a previous potential loss of volatile compounds in feces exposed to the environment (Alberts 1992), we only collected fresh feces with mucosal cuticle on the exterior, moist, with a shiny appearance, and a strong, characteristic odor (Martín et al. 2010; Barja et al. 2023). These fresh feces could also be crushed due to their high organic matter content, whereas older feces mostly consisted of indigestible prey remains, such as hair or hooves. Sometimes, very fresh feces were accompanied by urine marks or ground scratches, as additional wolf marking signals (Barja 2001). The presence of urine and noncompacted scratches also indicated the recency of the feces, as urine evaporates quickly, and scratches dry and compact over time (Barja 2009).
We only used feces that presumably served a role in chemical communication, considering the visual component of the signals (Barja et al. 2004, 2005; Barja 2009). Thus, we collected scats deposited on conspicuous substrates (e.g., plants, rocks, trunks), defined as those most prominent within a 2‐m radius around the scat (Barja 2009), elevated more than 4 cm above ground level (Bárcena and Barja 2005) and/or located at the junction of two or more trails (Barja et al. 2004). Each fresh scat showing the aforementioned characteristics was collected and stored in a container at −20°C until the experiment was conducted.
At the beginning of the experiment, the fecal scats were thawed and immediately placed in a natural experimental setup area, located near to Os Montes do Invernadeiro (42°05′46.6″ N, 7°19′52.3″ W; 1216 m) (see Supporting Information S1: Figure 1). A small fragment of each fecal sample (2 g) was collected as “Time 0” to determine the initial chemical composition of the feces. Then, each scat (N = 5) was divided into four equal‐sized fragments (5 cm in length and 2.5 cm in diameter), which were placed on different types of substrates equal to those used naturally by wolves in this area: (a) “Sand” (S) was plain soil, with the scat fragment positioned at ground level (0 cm height); (b) “Rocks” (R) was an aggregation of rocks collected nearby, with the scat fragment placed at 12 cm height; (c) “Sand prominence” (SP) consisted of an assemblage of stones, which was subsequently covered with a thick layer of soil from the nearby area, with the scat fragment placed at 12 cm height; and (d) “Plant” (P) was a natural prickled broom bush (P. tridentatum), with the scat fragment positioned at 35 cm height.
Small samples of feces from each substrate (2 g) were collected with forceps and from the end of the fragment at the following times after the initial deposition: 3, 6, 24, 48, 168 h (7 days), and 936 h (39 days). These samples were stored in a vial at −20°C in the lab until analyses. During the experiment, a total of 125 fecal samples were collected. The five initial samples collected at Time 0 served as baseline controls of the initial chemical composition of the fecal scats. Additionally, 120 samples came from fecal fragments that had been placed across four substrates, with each substrate providing five samples for each of the six time periods. The size of the entire fecal samples exposed was sufficient to prevent the rapid degradation of the compounds, because in larger feces, the compounds within them are better protected, leading to prolonged persistence, while in very small fragments, they degrade more quickly (Magondu et al. 2023). Therefore, the fragments we collected can be considered representative of the entire feces.
The temperature of each substrate was measured using probe temperature gauges connected to datalogger (HOBO 4‐Channel Thermocouple Data Logger), with the initial reading taken immediately after deposition of the scats. Different probes recorded the temperature every 5 min in each substrate during the experiment. For further statistical analysis, we calculated the mean temperatures recorded by each probe during each time interval for each substrate. Specifically, for each time period, the mean temperature was calculated from the moment that feces were placed until just before the collection of each fecal fragment, considering the specific duration of the interval of exposure (e.g., 0–3 h, 0–168 h, etc.).

## Analyses of Volatile Compounds in Feces
We used gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) to analyze volatile lipophilic compounds found in hexane extracts of the fecal samples following procedures described in detail in Martín et al. (2010) and Barja et al. (2023). Compounds were tentatively identified by comparing their mass spectra with those available in the NIST/EPA/NIH 2002 mass spectral library. We also used previously published information on chemical composition of feces of wolves from the study area (Martín et al. 2010; Barja et al. 2023). We used the peak area integration feature available in the Xcalibur software to calculate the relative abundance of each compound (i.e., percentage of its peak area in the chromatogram relative to the total ion current, TIC, area covered by all peaks, excluding contaminants). These relative proportions were transformed using the formula: Z ij = asin (√(Y ij /100)), where Z ij is the standardized peak area i for individual j and Y ij is the peak area i for individual j (Hirose et al. 2023). We used this to fix the nonindependence and deviations from normality commonly observed in proportion data (Aebischer et al. 1993). We also considered in each fecal sample the total number of chemical compounds and calculated the Shannon diversity index to assess the diversity of compounds (Morris et al. 2014).

## Data Analysis
To test whether the overall chemical profiles of the fecal samples (transformed relative proportions of all compounds in each sample) differed between substrates, temperatures and times we calculated separated Permutational Analyses of Variance (PERMANOVA) (Anderson and Braak 2003). We used the Euclidean distance between each pair of individual samples to generate a resemblance matrix, which was used for the later analyses with 999 permutations. Pairwise post hoc comparisons between groups were made using permutation tests (Anderson 2017). Then, we performed an ANOVA to assess whether each explanatory variable had sufficient variation to justify its inclusion as fixed factor in the generalized linear mixed models (GLMM). We also conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) as a descriptive tool to explore the relationships and patterns within the data. The PCA was performed by considering the summed relative proportions of all compounds within each functional group (e.g., steroids, fatty acids) present in the samples.
To address the issue of multicollinearity that arises when all compounds are analyzed simultaneously, the compounds were grouped based on their functional chemical properties. Clustering analyses were then performed to identify natural patterns and groupings within the data prior to implementing supervised classification models (Usefi 2022). The compound with the highest contribution weight in each functional group was selected as a representative variable. These representative compounds were then used to compare the effects of substrate, time, and temperature categories using GLMM (Bernaisch 2022).
We performed a GLMM to analyze the differences between the chemical profiles of the fecal samples. Our response variables were the transformed proportions of the selected compounds, and we also included proportions of cholesterol (one of the most important components in the reproductive period based on Barja et al. 2023) and (Z)‐9‐octadecenoic acid, ethyl ester (which might indicate diet quality based on Martín et al. 2007). We also made separate GLMMs for Shannon's diversity index, the total number of chemical compounds, and the PC1 or PC2 extracted in the PCA for the functional groups in each fecal sample. We included in all the models the type of “substrate” and “time” since deposition (fixed factors), “temperature” (continuous variable), and the original individual scat as random factor (since the samples came from five initial fecal scats). We performed models incorporating additive and interaction effects of these variables. We chose the most informative model based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) (Arnold 2010). Specifically, we selected the model with the minimum AIC and the greatest Akaike weight, following the principle of simplicity to determine the number of explanatory variables. The selected model was required to have a residual deviance‐to‐degrees of freedom ratio below 1.3 to ensure the absence of overdispersion (McCullagh 2019). The selected model was validated using the significance levels obtained from the model using the likelihood ratio test as the omnibus test. We conducted Wald's χ 2 for mixed models to estimate the effects of the contributing variables (Lee 2012).
All analyses were conducted in R software (R Core Team 2023), using tidyverse (Wickham et al. 2019), tidyr (Wickham et al. 2024) and dplyr (Wickham et al. 2021) packages for data edition and organization, ggplot2 (Wickham 2016), ggvis (Wickham and Chang 2016) and ggcorrplot (Kassambara 2023) packages for plotting, cluster (Maechler et al. 2023) and factoextra (Kassambara and Mundt 2020) for the clustering hierarchy, caret (Kuhn 2008) for the balancing of the number of observations across the classes, permute (Simpson 2022), lattice (Sarkar 2008) and pairwiseAdonis (Martinez 2017) packages for the PERMANOVA, and lme4 (Bates et al. 2015), lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al. 2017), car (Fox and Weisberg 2018) and MuMIn (Barton 2022) packages for the GLMM.

## Results
We found a total of 114 lipophilic compounds in 125 fecal samples (Table 1). The main compounds were 33 steroids (mean ± SE % of the TIC area = 51.0% ± 1.8%), followed by 15 alkanes (15.3% ± 1.6%), 26 fatty acids (9.0% ± 1.1%), 8 aldehydes (6.8% ± 0.8%), 2 terpenoids (5.8% ± 0.5%), 12 alcohols (3.9% ± 0.8%), 7 waxy esters (3.0% ± 0.5%), 7 aromatic compounds (3.0% ± 0.5%), 1 alkene (0.3% ± 0.1%), 2 ketones (0.2% ± 0.1%), and 1 inorganic compound (0.1% ± 0.01%). The five most abundant compounds were cholesterol (18.8% ± 1.2%), cholest‐4‐en‐3‐one (5.0% ± 0.9%), cholesta‐3,5‐diene (3.8% ± 0.9%), hentriacontane (3.4% ± 0.3%), and squalene (3.1% ± 0.3%).

The number of compounds detected in each fecal sample varied from 14 to 67 (mean ± SE = 37 ± 12 compounds per fecal sample). The number of chemical functional groups detected in each fecal sample varied from 5 to 18 (12 ± 3 chemical functional groups per fecal sample). Only two compounds appeared in all samples (squalene and cholesterol), while most compounds appeared in lower frequencies and produced variability between samples. Shannon's diversity index indicated a relatively high overall diversity of compounds in the fecal samples, but with a high variability between samples (H′ = 2.73 ± 0.78, range = 0.04–3.61). The GLMM analysis suggested that “time” and “substrate” were crucial predictors of the number of compounds in each sample (see Supporting Information S5: Table 1). The number of compounds was significantly higher at early time points, such as 3 h and decreased at later time points, such as 936 h. An elevated value was observed at 48 h (Figure 1). The number of compounds was significantly different for rock, sand prominence, and plant, being higher in sand followed by plant, sand prominence, and rock (LTR: χ 2 8 = 63.35, p < 0.001; see Supporting Information S6: Table 2). Yet none of the GLMM models carried out to explain Shannon's diversity index were significant.
Mean (±SE) number of compounds per sample depending on the type of substrate and the time‐period since initial exposure (in hours). Each line type indicates a different substrate (P = plant, R = rocks, S = sand, and SP = sand prominence).
The overall chemical profiles of the fecal samples did not significantly differ between the different types of substrates (PERMANOVA, pseudo F 4,119 = 0.68, p = 0.995) nor temperatures (PERMANOVA, pseudo F 1,122 = 1.86, p = 0.30) but did significantly differ between exposure times (PERMANOVA, pseudo F 6,117 = 1.48, p = 0.01). Post hoc comparisons showed that the composition at 3 h differed significantly (or at least marginally) from the subsequent later times, and while composition at 936 h differed significantly from the other shorter exposure times (see Supporting Information S7: Table 3).
Moreover, the temperature varied significantly depending on the type of substrate (ANOVA: F 4,119 = 3.85, p = 0.006) (see Supporting Information S2 and S8: Figure 2 and Table 4). Post hoc Tukey tests revealed that the substrate with the highest mean temperature was sand prominence, which showed a significantly higher temperature compared to plant and sand (p < 0.05). Plants attained a significantly lower temperature compared to sand prominence (p = 0.04) and rock (p = 0.05), while rock only showed significant differences compared to sand (p = 0.06). Overall, sand recorded the lowest temperature, though it was not significantly different from rock (p = 0.09). The temperature also varied significantly across different exposure times (ANOVA: F 6,117 = 57.85, p < 0.001) (see Supporting Information S3 and S8: Figure 3 and Table 4). Post hoc Tukey tests revealed that 3 and 6 h had the highest temperatures, significantly higher than 24, 48, and 168 h (all p < 0.05). The temperatures of 3 and 6 h were not significantly different from each other (p = 0.83). 24, 48, and 168 h showed intermediate temperatures, significantly higher than 936 h (all p < 0.05), but did not differ from each other (all p > 0.05). Lastly, 936 h recorded the lowest temperatures, significantly lower than all other time intervals (p < 0.05).
Furthermore, when considering the major chemical functional groups, two principal components (PCs) resulting from the PCA, accounted for 48% of the variance (see Supporting Information S4 and S9: Figure 4 and Table 5). In this case, high proportions of ketones and alkanes distinguished the fecal samples on the positive axis of PC1 (eigenvalue = 1.33, explained variance = 28%), while high proportions of waxy esters and aromatics loaded on the negative axis. On PC2 (eigenvalue = 1.23, explained variance = 20%), high proportions of aldehydes and alkanes separated the samples on the positive axis, whereas samples with high proportions of steroids were on the negative axis. For the PCA describing the chemical functional groups, variation in the PC1 scores was not significantly explained by any of the variables, while the variation in the PC2 scores was significantly explained only by “time” (GLMM: df = 5, AIC = 391.76, d = 0.00, w = 0.82; LTR: χ 2 6 = 26.48, p < 0.001). The PC2 scores were significantly lower at 168 h (estimate ± SE = 1.19 ± 0.53, Wald's test, χ 2 114 = 2.27, p = 0.025) and at 936 h (estimate ± SE = 1.20 ± 0.53, Wald's test, χ 2 114 = 2.28, p = 0.025). This indicated that samples taken after a long exposure time period had a higher proportion of steroids and terpenoids, and a lower proportion of aldehydes and alkanes compared to samples collected at other earlier time points.
The compounds selected in the clustering analyses included hexadecanol (estimate = 1.92) from the alcohol functional group, octadecanal (estimate = 1.89) from the aldehyde functional group, hentriacontane (estimate = 1.21) from the alkane functional group, indole (estimate = 0.60) from the aromatic functional group, pentadecanone (estimate = 0.42) from the ketone functional group, tetradecanoic acid, ethyl ester (branched or isomer) (estimate = 1.25) from the fatty acid functional group, β‐cholestanol (estimate = 6.07) from the steroid functional group, α‐tocopherol from the tocopherol functional group and butyric acid, hexadecyl ester (estimate = 1.73) from the waxy ester functional group. We also selected tetramethyl‐2‐hexadecene from the alkene functional group, squalene from the terpenoid functional group, and the cyclic octaatomic sulfur from the inorganic functional group, as these were the sole representatives of their respective functional groups.
Regarding the models based on the compounds selected in the clustering analyses, none of the GLMMs conducted to explain the variation in tetramethyl‐2‐hexadecene, pentadecanone, cyclic octaatomic sulfur, butyric acid, hexadecyl ester or (Z)‐9‐octadecenoic acid, ethyl ester showed significant results. However, when evaluating hexadecanol, octadecanal, hentriacontane, indole, tetradecanoic acid, ethyl ester (branched or isomer), β‐cholestanol, squalene, and α‐tocopherol in the fecal samples, the model with “time” as predictor appeared to be the best fitted model (see Supporting Information S10: Table 6). The abundance of hexadecanol (Figure 2A) was significantly lower at 168 and 936 h, and significantly higher at 3, 6, 24, and 48 h (LTR: χ 2 6 = 15.38, p = 0.02; see Supporting Information S11: Table 7). Octadecanal levels (Figure 2A) were found to vary significantly, with notably higher levels at 3, 6, 24, and 48 h, and lower abundances observed at 168 and 936 h (LTR: χ 2 6 = 23.91, p < 0.001; see Supporting Information S11: Table 7). Regarding indole (Figure 2A), samples collected at 3 and 24 h had significantly higher proportions of indole, while samples collected at 6, 48, 168, and 936 h had significantly lower proportions of indole (LTR: χ 2 6 = 28.94, p < 0.001; see Supporting Information S11: Table 7). For tetradecanoic acid, ethyl ester (branched or isomer), significant differences were found only in some time intervals (Figure 2A), being the abundance of this compound significantly higher at 3 h and significantly lower at 39 days (LTR: χ 2 6 = 12.39, p = 0.07; see Supporting Information S11: Table 7). As for β‐cholestanol (Figure 2B), the only category that differed significantly was 24 h, in which the levels of β‐cholestanol tend to increase (LTR: χ 2 6 = 4.32, p = 0.05; see Supporting Information S11: Table 7). Variations in hentriacontane abundance (Figure 2B) were evident across time points levels were significantly higher at 6, 168, and 936 h, while they were significantly lower at 3, 24, and 48 h (LTR: χ 2 6 = 12.98, p = 0.04; see Supporting Information S11: Table 7). Regarding squalene (Figure 2B), only the samples collected at 6 h had significantly higher proportions (LTR: χ 2 6 = 6.92, p = 0.08; see Supporting Information S11: Table 7). The abundance of α‐tocopherol (Figure 2B) was significantly lower at 3 h followed by 6, 24, 48, and 168 h. The lowest abundance of α‐tocopherol occurred at 39 days (LTR: χ 2 6 = 56.79, p < 0.001; see Supporting Information S11: Table 7). Regarding cholesterol, the analysis identified the model with “temperature” alone as the primary predictor demonstrated the best fit (see Supporting Information S10: Table 6). Cholesterol relative proportions (Figure 3) were varied significantly with temperature, with a positive correlation observed; as temperature increased, cholesterol proportions also increased (LTR: χ 2 1 = 8.21, p = 0.004; estimate ± SE = 0.04 ± 0.01, χ 2 119 = 2.91, p = 0.004).

## Discussion
We identified a wide range of chemical compounds in the feces of Iberian wolves. This mixture, at least the one found in the fresh samples immediately after deposition, included high proportions of heterocyclic aromatic organic compounds with benzene rings, aldehydes, low‐weight fatty acids, and alcohols, which are strongly odoriferous compounds, similar to those identified in previous studies (Martín et al. 2010). Squalene, α‐tocopherol, and cholesterol usually appear in high proportions in feces (Raymer et al. 1985). In contrast, amides and sulfur compounds are in low abundance in wolves' feces (Martín et al. 2010) and anal sac secretions (Raymer et al. 1985), since these compounds are sensitive to oxidation and unlikely to function as persistent signalers of the occupation of an area (Apps et al. 2012). This may confirm that feces have a scent‐marking purpose and play an important role in chemical communication (Barja et al. 2023).
The overall chemical composition of wolves' feces did not differ between the different substrates or temperatures but strongly depended on the time of exposition. Wolves probably would not respond to fecal marks that had been environmentally exposed for 39 days, the longest exposure time in our experiment, since the chemical composition of feces changed significantly with respect to the original composition. However, some information might still have been perceived at that point (Walker et al. 2024), as fade‐out times seem to be appropriately adjusted to specific social contexts (Alberts 1992). This suggests that chemical compounds are progressively lost since the moment of deposition (Alberts 1992; Barja 2001) and that the effectiveness of fecal marks would be almost entirely lost 1 month after the deposition of the scat.
Interestingly, although there is a general trend of decreasing numbers of chemical compounds, a peak is observed at 48 h. This may be attributed to a slight increase in new compounds produced by bacteria involved in the degradation of feces (Raymer et al. 1985). Some such compounds are certain alcohols and aromatic heterocyclic compounds that can be found in wolf feces, like indole and phenol. These compounds result from bacterial transformation of other compounds originally produced in the anal glands, such as the amino acids tryptophan and tyrosine, respectively (Raymer et al. 1985; Martín et al. 2010). Since this transformation is driven by enzymes primarily produced by intestinal bacteria with pathogenic potential, it is possible that the intestinal microbiota from the reproductive pair had a high nonpathogenic bacterial population (Garner et al. 2007; Bastos et al. 2020).
Given that compounds that dissipate quickly are considered less likely to play a significant role in long‐term territorial signaling (Apps et al. 2012), multivariate analyses showed time differences in the chemical profiles of feces. In the collected samples, we observed a more pronounced loss of hexadecanol (alcohol), octadecanal (aldehyde), indole (aromatic compound), hentriacontane (alkane), and tetradecanoic acid, ethyl ester (branched or isomer) (fatty acid). In contrast, compounds such as squalene (terpenoid) and α‐tocopherol showed greater persistence or a slower temporal decline over the periods of 7 and 39 days.
Alkanes, such as hentriacontane, are potentially metabolized compounds from exogenous compounds, specifically from skin bacteria (Schirmer et al. 2010; Kücklich et al. 2017). Aromatic compounds such as indole, as we already discussed, are bacterial fermentation products (Scheline 1973; Raymer et al. 1985), which could also serve as short‐term signaling function (Barja et al. 2023). It is likely that these compounds are intended to communicate with individuals present at the time of defecation, similar to how certain compounds in the anal gland secretions of canids, such as foxes, function (Comizzoli et al. 2021). Aldehydes, such as octadecanal, are highly odoriferous compounds that are more abundant during the reproductive season, potentially enhancing the conspicuousness of scent signals to conspecifics during this period (Barja et al. 2023).
In contrast, steroids, such as β‐cholestanol or cholesterol, have high molecular weights and low pressures, which makes them quite stable at high temperatures and harsh environmental conditions (Apps et al. 2012; Lewis 2016). Steroids are believed to work as semiochemicals and may also play a role in protecting other semiochemicals within secretions (Martín and López 2013). Terpenoids, such as squalene, are highly robust compounds with similar stability properties as steroids that appear in the anal gland secretion (Martín et al. 2010). It is believed that their primary function may be to serve as fixatives for smaller molecules (Apps et al. 2012) and lipophilic antioxidants (Brigelius‐Flohé and Traber 1999), enhancing the chemical stability of feces on moist surfaces (Alberts 1992), rather than as direct signaling agents (Apps et al. 2012). Either way, both of these functional groups might allow scent marks to withstand relatively high temperatures, preserving their capacity to signal the presence of a reproductive pair, specifically a male (Martín et al. 2024). Lastly, α‐tocopherol (=vitamin E) is an antioxidant of dietary sources and is associated with individuals with a higher quality diet, since these individuals secrete greater amounts of vitamin E in their chemical signals (Martín et al. 2010; Barja et al. 2023), which could be directly related with the fact that the reproductive pair feeds first and has a better diet (Kopena et al. 2011).
Substrate appeared only in the best‐fitted model for the total number of chemical compounds per sample but not in the models for selected chemical compounds. This suggests that substrate type may affect the overall chemical composition of fecal scent marks, but not their specific chemical composition. One possible explanation is the physical or chemical properties of the substrates, beyond temperature, that may influence compound stability, alter interactions among molecules, or induce phase changes, ultimately impacting in the total number of compounds detectable (Luo 2007).
Temperature varied significantly among substrates, with rocks reaching the highest temperatures and sand the lowest. This pattern aligns with the geological characteristics of the study area, dominated by siliceous metamorphic mountains rich in quartz. Quartz is known for its high thermal conductivity, allowing rocks to absorb and retain heat more efficiently (Schön 2011; Tiskatine et al. 2017). In contrast, plants have evolved molecular, cellular, and physiological mechanisms to adapt to and tolerate heat stress (Li 2020), resulting in lower temperatures in plants compared to rocks. Our findings are consistent with this, as plants showed the lowest mean temperatures and may allow feces release volatile compounds more gradually.
While substrate type influenced the number of chemical compounds quantitatively, it did not significantly alter the qualitative composition of the feces. Our results showed a lower number of chemical compounds per fecal sample in substrates such as rocks and sand prominence, whereas substrates like plants and sand maintained a higher number of compounds. This may reflect functional differences in scent‐marking strategies. Marks on warmer substrates inducing higher and quicker volatility of compounds (rocks and sand prominence) would be suited for rapid intragroup communication, especially during the reproductive period. In contrast, marks on cooler substrates that allow a lower volatility (plants and sand) would be better for territorial marking, as they may optimize signal longevity and are more energy‐efficient for boundary marking.
Although substrates reaching higher temperatures tended to be associated with a lower number of compounds, temperature alone did not significantly explain the variation in compound presence across models. We believe this absence of significance was due to the attenuating effect of daily temperature cycles (Aires et al. 2004), which moderates thermal peaks and fluctuations and reduces the impact on compound degradation. The experimental design aimed to replicate natural field conditions and thus did not impose artificial cumulative heat stress over time. Yet, rising temperatures due to climate change might likely result in higher daily maximum and minimum temperatures, potentially accelerating the loss of volatile compounds from fecal marks and eventually affecting the effectiveness of chemical signals. This phenomenon has already been documented in substrate scent‐marks of other vertebrate species (Martín and López 2013; Conrad et al. 2017; Martín et al. 2024) and should be further investigated in the future.
Wolves are known to exhibit selective behavior when choosing substrates for marking (Barja 2001, 2009). Specifically, Iberian wolves show a preference for marking plants with greater height and larger trunks diameters (Barja and de Miguel 2004; Barja 2009). However, our results did not fully support the hypothesis that substrate selection is driven by the optimization of the olfactory signal persistence. While the number of chemical compounds varied among substrates, neither compound persistence nor volatility were clearly influenced by substrate type. This contrasts with findings in other carnivores, such as the red fox and European wildcat (Barja 2003), and may suggest that the visual component plays a more important role than the olfactory component when selecting a substrate. Taller substrates like plants may facilitate better visual detectability (Peters and Mech 1975), thereby contributing to the visual conspicuousness of the signal. Given the territorial nature of wolves, however, scent marks must persist long enough to be detected by potential intruders (Barja 2009; Apps et al. 2012). This may explain the repeated deposition of feces on the same marking sites, a behavior known as remarking (Peters and Mech 1975; Barja et al. 2004, 2008).
Our results help to understand the factors that may affect the duration and effectiveness of fecal scent marks as chemical signals in the Iberian wolf. As expected, the number and abundance of volatile compounds decreased with time, although some compounds showed greater stability than others. Lower environmental temperatures may help prolonging the chemical signal in some cases, but this effect is not uniform across all compounds. While neither the type of substrate nor its temperature significantly influenced the persistence of individual chemical compounds, substrate type did affect the total number of compounds detected in each fecal sample. This suggests that the substrate may modulate quantitatively the overall chemical richness of the scent mark. We found that substrates with lower temperatures, such as plants, retained a higher number of compounds compared to hotter substrates like rocks, although temperature alone was not a significant predictor in most models. Most importantly, we suggest that the visual component seems to play a more important role in the substrate preferences than the olfactory one. If the purpose is territorial marking, these signals should be deposited on substrates where they can be easily detected by conspecifics. In summary, our results suggest that the type of substrate itself may mainly amplify the visual component of the signal, although it also influences the olfactory component of the scent markings in the number of chemical compounds. Notably, our multivariate approach may provide a deeper understanding of the variations in the chemical composition of scent marks and contribute to the understanding of fecal marking of the Iberian wolf. However, to understand the function of specific compounds in feces and the effect of variables related to their location within the territory, further research is required to investigate the chemosensory and behavioral responses of wolves to feces, naturally or experimentally manipulated to differ in chemical compositions.
