# Clinical Validation of a Comprehensive Vision Screener for Assessing Visual Fitness for Drivers in Mumbai, India

## Abstract
AbstractPurposeTo determine the agreement of an all-in-one portable vision screener (VS) with a standard battery of optometrist-administered tests. The purpose is to understand the feasibility of rapid screening for visual functions before providing occupational visual fitness.Patients and MethodsA Prospective cross-sectional study was performed among licensed public transport drivers at community eye camps in Mumbai. Distance and near vision, contrast sensitivity (CS), stereopsis, colour vision (CV), and peripheral visual field were assessed using standard tests, including the LogMAR, Titmus Fly, Ishihara, and confrontation test, respectively. The same assessments were repeated using an all-in-one portable screener (VS). The total testing time was recorded for each test. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and Bland-Altman plots were used for statistical analyses.ResultsA total of 384 drivers participated, with a mean age of 47.1 ± 9.14 years. The mean presenting distance visual acuity was 0.22 ± 0.16 and 0.20 ± 0.18 LogMAR (p>0.05), the mean values for CS were 2.98 ± 2% and 5.7 ± 10.17% (p<0.05), the values for stereopsis were 863.08 ± 1402.92 and 656.54 ± 933.29 sec of arc (p<0.05) with standard charts and VS, respectively. The integrated colour vision test demonstrated low sensitivity (22.2%) but high specificity (89.2%) for detecting colour vision deficiency. Screening time decreased from 20 ± 5 minutes to 7 ± 3 minutes. ROC analysis showed excellent diagnostic accuracy for distance visual acuity (AUC = 0.861) and modest discriminative ability for stereopsis (AUC = 0.653). Bland–Altman analysis indicated good agreement between the standard test and screener for vision, CS, and stereopsis, with no significant systematic bias.ConclusionThe portable vision screener demonstrated high accuracy and strong agreement with standard clinical tests for visual functional tests. It enables rapid, comprehensive assessment of visual functions, making it a practical tool for large-scale vision screening in OHCs or mobile eye screening camps.Keywords: vision screening, occupational health, visiolite vision screener, visual functions

## Introduction
Assessment of visual fitness for occupational drivers typically requires a comprehensive battery of visual function tests performed by optometrists, including distance and near visual acuity, colour vision, stereopsis, contrast sensitivity, glare recovery, and peripheral visual field evaluation. Road traffic accidents (RTAs) involving both public and private transport drivers represent a significant public health concern in India. According to the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways (2019), India accounts for approximately 11% of global road traffic fatalities, as reported in the Global Road Safety Report.1 Despite the multifactorial nature of driving, human error remains a predominant contributor to RTAs. Among these errors, deficiencies in visual function have been consistently identified as a leading cause.2,3 The visual functions considered essential for safe driving encompass distance and near visual acuity, visual field assessment, stereopsis, colour vision, and contrast sensitivity or glare recovery, as these parameters are critical for hazard detection, distance judgment, and night/low contrast performance.2,4 Recent occupational studies have shown that reduced peripheral vision, and visual field restriction may impair hazard detection and increase the risk of accidents.5,6
While this multi-domain assessment is essential for ensuring safe driving performance, its routine implementation is time-consuming, resource-intensive, and often cumbersome for both patients and providers. Conventional testing requires multiple charts, instruments, and testing stations, making the process tedious, especially in high-volume occupational health clinics, licensing centres, and field-based screening camps. The dependence on trained eye care professionals and dedicated infrastructure further limits the scalability and consistency of testing in India. Narayan et al reported that India need is short of 100,000 Optometrists to serve the eye and vision care needs of India.7,8
To address these constraints, several comprehensive vision screeners have been developed to enable rapid, standardized screening. However, not all commercially available screeners assess the full range of visual functions critical for driving. In particular, contrast sensitivity under glare conditions and peripheral visual field assessment—both strongly associated with crash risk and night-time driving performance—are often omitted or inadequately evaluated. These omissions reduce the effectiveness of screening programs aimed at identifying visually at-risk drivers. Consequently, there is a clear need for a time-efficient, portable, and comprehensive vision screening solution that can reliably assess all key visual functions relevant to occupational driving, while remaining feasible for deployment in real-world screening environments.
Recent literature underscores the critical role of visual assessment in driver safety, with Honavar (2022) strongly advocating for the implementation of comprehensive visual function testing prior to the issuance of driving licences in India, and providing a recommended battery of such tests.4 However, the effective enforcement of vision standards faces substantial challenges. These include a shortage of trained human resources, disparities in the distribution of eye and vision care services between urban and rural regions, and the absence of uniform, evidence-based vision standards for driver licensing. Collectively, these barriers hinder the adoption of systematic visual screening protocols, thereby perpetuating the risk of vision-related RTAs.9–11 Table 1 summarizes key findings from the occupational/commercial-driver literature for the visual parameters considered in comprehensive screening.

A practical approach for driver licensing is to implement a two-step pathway in which all applicants first undergo a rapid, standardized vision screening, followed by a detailed confirmatory examination for those who fail or have borderline results. Systematic reviews have reported considerable variability in testing methods and cut-off criteria across studies,18 underscoring the need for simple, scalable screening systems that can be applied consistently at the population level. For such a model to be feasible in real-world settings, comprehensive vision screening must be time-efficient and capable of being conducted without the need for specialist staff at every testing centre.
In this context, the present study aims to propose and evaluate a feasible solution to address these gaps, to enhance road safety through improved visual function assessment among drivers in India.
The study cohort consisted of public transport drivers. Among various modes, autorickshaws and taxis are particularly popular for short distances and last-mile connectivity. Autorickshaws, also known as tuk-tuks, are three-wheeled, low-cost vehicles that are both accessible and affordable for the general population in suburban areas—not only in India but across Southeast Asia. Many drivers of these vehicles lack formal training or standardized certification, unlike drivers of four-wheelers and heavy-duty vehicles, which may contribute to concerns about road safety.
The efficacy of comprehensive vision screeners and their application within occupational environments is well substantiated in the literature.17,19 In recent years, the utilisation of smartphone-based applications for vision screening has also been systematically investigated.19 Nevertheless, most existing studies predominantly address assessments of visual acuity and colour vision, with limited emphasis on the broader spectrum of visual functions essential for driving, such as contrast sensitivity, glare recovery, and peripheral visual field assessment.20 Given that current recommendations for driver visual assessment encompass a battery of tests extending beyond visual acuity and colour vision, there remains a critical need to identify an appropriate screening device and rigorously validate its performance against established gold standard screening tests.
One such device was identified, which performs the required battery of tests called Visiolite 4K. This is a device manufactured by FIM Medical, France, and distributed by Jutron Vision, India. It is a class 1 medical device and is CE certified, and its reference standards are EN60601-1, EN60601-1-2, and NF EN ISO 8596.21
It integrates the complete battery of visual function tests recommended for occupational vision screening. Unlike previously validated vision screeners, the Visiolite 4K includes assessment of peripheral visual field and glare recovery in addition to conventional visual function tests, making it particularly suitable for occupational and licensing settings. However, there has been no published data demonstrating the feasibility of using this vision screener in makeshift clinics and occupational health centres for assessing visual skills. The present study aimed to evaluate the agreement between an all-in-one portable vision screener and standard ophthalmic tests, and to assess its feasibility for use in occupational health centres and field-based screening settings. Specifically, the study compared measurements of distance visual acuity (DVA), stereopsis, colour vision, contrast sensitivity, glare recovery, and peripheral visual field.

## Objectives
To determine the diagnostic validity of the Visiolite 4K vision screener by estimating its sensitivity and specificity.
To assess agreement between screener and gold standard methods for each visual function.
To evaluate the feasibility of large-scale occupational vision screening using the comprehensive screener.

## Materials and Methods
A prospective cross-sectional validation study was conducted among public transport drivers in Mumbai. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Lotus Eye Hospital and College of Optometry prior to study initiation. Eye camps and makeshift clinics were established at strategic locations throughout the city to facilitate participant recruitment. All tests were conducted indoors under standardized ambient lighting conditions to ensure consistency across participants.
A convenience sampling technique was used formal sample size calculation was not performed, as this was a field-based validation study, and all eligible autorickshaw and taxi drivers attending the community eye camps during the study period were invited to participate. Drivers holding a valid licence who provided written informed consent to undergo assessment using both the standard clinical tests and the Visiolite 4K vision screener were consecutively enrolled.
Each participant underwent a detailed medical history and a comprehensive vision assessment. The assessment protocol included the following tests: distance visual acuity (LogMAR chart), near visual acuity (LogMAR chart, Good-Lite, USA), contrast sensitivity (Pelli-Robson chart, Good-Lite, USA), stereopsis (Titmus Fly test, Precision Vision, USA), peripheral visual field awareness (Tangent Screen, HKSB, Hong Kong), and colour vision using the Ishihara test (24-plate test by Good-Lite, USA). These tests were first administered using gold standard clinical charts, followed by the same battery of tests performed on the Visiolite 4K Vision Screener. The time taken for a subsample for all the gold standard tests was noted, and the time taken to perform the same tests was also recorded on the Visiolite 4K. Additionally to complete posterior segment screening, direct ophthalmoscopy was performed on all participants. Those who were identified with uncorrected refractive errors were provided with corrective spectacles, and those requiring further evaluation or treatment were referred to Lotus Eye Hospital for comprehensive care.
Peripheral visual field assessment was initially performed using confrontation methods during eye camp screenings. For validation and to meet occupational health and safety standards, the Visiolite 4K’s peripheral field test was further evaluated for sensitivity and specificity against the Tangent Screen test. As the Tangent Screen test requires standardized illumination and adequate testing space, it was not feasible to perform during the camps. This validation was conducted in a separate cohort of 30 healthy individuals at a tertiary-care eye hospital, where the Tangent Screen served as the reference standard.13,20 Participants who were able to complete both the Visiolite 4K and Tangent Screen assessments were included.
The diagnostic thresholds used for each visual function were selected based on previously published literature and established clinical reference standards, and were applied consistently throughout the diagnostic accuracy analyses.4

## Statistical Analysis
Data were analysed using SPSS version 31. Normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the data were not normally distributed, the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was applied to compare the results between the gold standard clinical test and the Screener. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to determine sensitivity and specificity, while Bland–Altman plots were used to evaluate the agreement between the Comprehensive Screener and the gold standard clinical tests.

## Results
384 drivers enrolled and participated in the study, with a mean age of 47.1 ± 9.14 years. The mean values for Distance and near visual acuity, Contrast sensitivity, stereopsis, and Glare recovery were then analysed and compared for any statistically significant differences, as shown in Table 2.

Distance visual acuity showed no statistically significant difference between methods (0.22 ± 0.16 vs 0.20 ± 0.18 LogMAR; p>0.05), supporting interchangeability for this core parameter. Near visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, and glare recovery showed statistically significant differences (p<0.05), indicating that these parameters should be interpreted as screening triggers for confirmatory assessment rather than direct substitutes for reference testing. Stereopsis values did not differ statistically significantly between workflows (863 ± 1403 vs 657 ± 933 seconds of arc; p>0.05), though high variability was observed in both methods.
The peripheral visual field was tested using the screener, and confrontation in eye camps showed good agreement. The Visiolite Screener’s test for peripheral field was performed on 30 normal individuals and repeated on a tangent screen at 1 m distance. The test results, when compared, showed 100% agreement, where Sensitivity was 100% (95% CI: 39.8–100%), and specificity was 100% (95% CI: 86.8–100%).
The vision screener demonstrated high sensitivity 81% (95% CI: 67.7–90%) and specificity, 91% (95% CI: 87.1–93.5%) for distance visual acuity at the selected threshold (Figure 1), indicating strong agreement with the reference standard in identifying drivers who met or failed the visual acuity criteria. For stereopsis, sensitivity was 58.5% (95% CI: 50.8–66.2%), and specificity was 83.5% (CI 95%: 75.3–90%), reflecting moderate performance in detecting reduced depth perception. Color vision assessment was performed using the Ishihara 24-plate test as the reference standard and a consolidated 12-figure pseudoisochromatic plate within the vision screener. The vision screener showed low sensitivity (22.2%) (95% CI: 6.3–54.7%) but high specificity (89.2%) (95% CI: 85.1–92.3%) for detecting color vision deficiency (Figure 1). While positive findings were largely reliable, a substantial proportion of color-deficient individuals were not detected, indicating the need for refinement of the integrated color vision component.

The assessment times for the standard tests were recorded for 288 subjects, followed by a reassessment using the Visiolite Vision Screener for the same battery of tests. The mean duration to complete the standard test battery was 20 ± 5 minutes, whereas administration with the Visiolite Vision Screener required 7 ± 3 minutes, indicating a 65% reduction in assessment time. This difference translates, theoretically, to about 8 comprehensive vision screenings can be completed per hour using the vision screener, compared to only about 3 per hour with the gold standard test.
The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve was utilized to evaluate the efficacy of measurements for acuity and stereopsis. ROC analysis demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy for distance visual acuity (AUC = 0.861, SE = 0.037, 95% CI = 0.788–0.933, p < 0.001), while only a modest discriminative ability was observed for stereopsis (AUC = 0.653, 95% CI = 0.589–0.718, p < 0.001), as illustrated in Figures 2 and 3.

## Discussion
This study demonstrates that a time-efficient, multi-domain vision screening approach can be effectively implemented for assessing driver visual competence and in licensing-oriented settings. The portable vision screener enabled completion of a comprehensive set of visual tests in approximately 7 minutes, compared to about 20 minutes using the conventional multi-station method. This reduction in testing time is particularly valuable in high-volume environments such as occupational health clinics and remote worksites, where space and manpower may be limited.
The device can be operated by trained healthcare personnel in occupational or field settings, as the testing process is standardized and system-guided, generating structured digital reports. These reports can be securely shared with optometrists or ophthalmologists for remote review, enabling Teleoptometry or teleophthalmology support in locations where dedicated eye care professionals are not available.17,20 This facilitates accurate interpretation and clinical oversight even in remote or resource-constrained settings. In addition to improving efficiency, the study showed that a single portable instrument could assess all essential visual functions required for safe driving, including visual acuity, stereopsis, color vision, contrast sensitivity, glare recovery, and peripheral visual field.
Taank P. et al, in their study, further stated that such screeners are particularly valuable in occupational environments and can be effectively utilized by occupational and other healthcare professionals with appropriate training, even in the absence of optometrists or ophthalmologists.17 The present study reported not just efficiency in terms of time saved, but also that one single portable instrument could capture all essential visual skills. Furthermore, the output report generated by the Visiolite Vision Screener is straightforward to interpret, enabling prompt referral for comprehensive eye examinations for individuals who do not pass the screening. In remote and industrial locations in the absence of easy access to tertiary care systems, the reports could be further shared using telemedicine platforms for expert consultation.20
The Visiolite 4K vision screener incorporates all essential tests required to assess the visual skills of drivers and occupational health professionals during workplace functional evaluations. The device encompasses a comprehensive range of visual function tests, as recommended by Honavar and Kobal N. et al, with particular emphasis on the assessment of visual fields.4,8 The results of the present study demonstrate that the screener maintains strong agreement across all evaluated visual functional tests.
Distant visual acuity by far remains the most significant visual skill for not just drivers but also for various other occupations. Traffic accidents and safety issues have been linked to poor visual acuity by Ekpenyong et al.2,3 The present study’s validation results for the Visiolite Vision screener’s specificity and sensitivity reported 91% and 81%, respectively, indicating that the screener is most suitable for visual acuity assessment. A study by Torres et al compared two different screeners and reported a sensitivity and specificity for monocular visual acuity of 80% for both screeners.19 Taank et al also reported that a Titmus Vision screener also showed high sensitivity and specificity for distant visual acuity of 99.5% and 90.6%, respectively.17
Torres et al measured stereoacuity with Visiotest and Optec 6500 and reported lower sensitivity and specificity (55–70%) for both devices,19 while the present study reported 58.5% and 83.5%, making it a clinically acceptable device.
A notable feature of the Visiolite 4K screener is its capacity to assess peripheral vision, demonstrating strong concordance with tangent screen visual tests typically regarded as appropriate for screening applications.15,22 Various vision screeners are available, including the Titmus V4, Optec 6500, Vutest, Ergovision, Visiotest, and Keystone VS-V, almost all of which evaluate visual acuity, colour perception, and stereovision. Among these, only the Keystone VS-V measures peripheral visual fields, while the Visiotest evaluates horizontal field; however, limited research has addressed the validity of these assessments. The device utilized in this study assesses both peripheral and central visual fields and has been at the forefront of this technology since 2013, with an associated patent held by the company.21
Owens et al emphasized the importance of evaluating both photopic and mesopic visual acuity, especially in relation to night driving conditions. The present study demonstrated that contrast sensitivity assessments conducted under glare and non-glare conditions exhibited strong clinical concordance with the gold standard Pelli-Robson test for contrast sensitivity.16
Taank, P. et al reported high sensitivity for color vision in their validation of the Titmus Vision Screener V4, whereas Toress et al observed low sensitivity but high specificity with other screeners. They further mentioned in their study that Optec 6500 does not assess color vision and Visiotest screener had a sensitivity of 50% and specificity of 91% and both devices are marked as approved for occupational standards.17,19 Similarly, the present study also found low sensitivity with clinically acceptable high specificity, suggesting that all false positives should be re-evaluated using standard Ishihara Colour pseudoisochromatic plates or alternative tests to avoid unjust denial of valid driving licenses. It is often a case of color deficiency rather than an inability to recognize red and green traffic lights. Kobal N. also reported that lower accidents have been attributed to poor color vision ability of drivers.8
A structured, tiered screening approach is recommended for occupational and commercial drivers. This supports a two-step pathway in which all applicants first undergo a rapid, standardized screening, followed by a detailed optometric or ophthalmic evaluation for those who fail the initial test or show borderline results. The basic screening should include distance visual acuity (binocular/monocular), as this remains the primary requirement for licensing decisions. The next important tier should focus on contrast sensitivity with glare testing and a peripheral awareness or visual field assessment, as these visual functions have been strongly associated with crash involvement. Studies done by Isik et al have shown that reduced peripheral vision and work-related visual field restrictions can impair hazard detection and situational awareness, reinforcing the importance of incorporating peripheral visual field assessment into vision screening.5,6
Previous evidence has shown notably higher crash rates among drivers with poor depth perception, restricted vertical visual fields, reduced contrast sensitivity, and delayed glare recovery (eg, depth perception 95% vs 56%, vertical field 91% vs 61%, contrast sensitivity 106% vs 66%, glare recovery 92% vs 66% in unacceptable vs acceptable groups).2 Stereopsis (depth perception) should mainly be used to identify individuals who require further evaluation rather than serving as a direct reason for license rejection. Color vision testing is of lower priority for crash prevention, and any abnormal findings should be confirmed with a standard test before making licensing decisions.
Overall, this tiered approach enhances detection of visual problems beyond distance acuity alone, while remaining practical and time-efficient for large-scale screening programs. The screening protocol can be readily replicated across different occupational health settings by maintaining standardized testing conditions, ensuring operator training, and following the manufacturer’s recommended operating procedures, thereby supporting consistent diagnostic performance. Further to address the lack of human resources, the device can also be used for task shifting by training occupational healthcare professionals.

## Limitations
The cross-sectional design of this study did not permit evaluation of prospective crash outcomes or prediction of future driving risk. In addition, reference testing was conducted prior to the portable screening workflow, which may have introduced potential learning or fatigue effects; future studies should consider randomizing the order of assessments and incorporating operator masking where feasible. The feasibility of peripheral visual field assessment was examined only among participants with normal visual fields, and it was validated in a small sample of participants; therefore, the observed 100% agreement should be interpreted with caution, and further studies involving larger and more diverse populations are warranted. Further implementation research is warranted to evaluate the device’s performance when operated by trained non-specialists, as would typically occur in licensing centres and field-based settings.

## Conclusion
The Visiolite Vision Screener showed strong agreement with gold standard clinical tests, particularly for distance and near visual acuity, while enabling assessment of multiple visual functions within a short testing time. Its ability to deliver comprehensive screening in a single workflow makes it suitable for occupational and licensing settings. However, the lower sensitivity observed for colour vision assessment and the limited validation sample for peripheral vision assessment call for cautious interpretation. Hence, the vision screener is intended for screening, but not diagnostic purposes. Individuals who fail or receive borderline results should be subjected to thorough diagnostics using standard ophthalmic clinical tests.
