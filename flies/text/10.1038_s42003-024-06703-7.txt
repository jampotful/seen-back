Dual color optogenetic tool enables heart arrest, bradycardic, and tachycardic pacing in Drosophila melanogaster.
Gracheva E, Wang Y, Zhu J, Wang F, Matt A, Fishman M, Liang H, Zhou C. (2024). Communications biology. . doi:10.1038/s42003-024-06703-7. Licence: cc-by-nc-nd. Text from europepmc-xml.

# Dual color optogenetic tool enables heart arrest, bradycardic, and tachycardic pacing in Drosophila melanogaster

## Abstract
AbstractIn order to facilitate cardiovascular research to develop non-invasive optical heart pacing methods, we have generated a double-transgenic Drosophila melanogaster (fruit fly) model suitable for optogenetic pacing. We created a fly stock with both excitatory H134R-ChR2 and inhibitory eNpHR2.0 opsin transgenes. Opsins were expressed in the fly heart using the Hand-GAL4 driver. Here we describe Hand > H134R-ChR2; eNpHR2.0 model characterization including bi-directional heart control (activation and inhibition) upon illumination of light with distinct wavelengths. Optical control and real-time visualization of the heart function were achieved non-invasively using an integrated light stimulation and optical coherence microscopy (OCM) system. OCM produced high-speed and high-resolution imaging; simultaneously, the heart function was modulated by blue (470 nm) or red (617 nm) light pulses causing tachycardia, bradycardia and restorable cardiac arrest episodes in the same animal. The irradiance power levels and illumination schedules were optimized to achieve successful non-invasive bi-directional heart pacing in Drosophila larvae and pupae.Subject terms: Optical imaging, Optogenetics

## Introduction
Heart disease is a leading cause of mortality in the United States. Grim statistics indicate that it affects all social and racial groups of society1. Heart disease can manifest itself in many forms: heart attack, heart failure, or arrhythmia. Abnormal heart rhythms frequently lead to insufficient cardiac output. Erratic electric pulses in the heart result in a slower heart rate (bradycardia) or more intense beating (tachycardia). Most heart disease risk factors are related to the lifestyle choices; medical conditions (including prescription drugs) and genetic factors also play a significant role.
To advance our understanding of heart disease mechanisms, selection of an effective model organism is very important. While primates and humans are ideal for recapitulating heart disorders, the complexity of cardiovascular regulation in higher mammals and ethical issues present significant challenges to conduct research. Using cell-based research systems (engineered heart tissue, human cardiac organoids) significantly facilitated the understanding of heart development and screening for new therapeutics2,3; however, these systems cannot replace model organisms due to incomplete representation of tissues and physiological processes. Vertebrate model organisms (from zebrafish to mammals) are indispensable for cardiovascular research but are not suitable for high throughput screens. A non-vertebrate model organism, Drosophila melanogaster (fruit fly), has been successfully used for cardiovascular research. D. melanogaster has a permanent presence in biological research since T. Morgan’s genetic experiments in 1900s4. Drosophila and mammalian genomes have significant conservation levels among the genes regulating the development, metabolism, and disease state5. Drosophila cardiovascular system is an open circulatory system, where a heart tube, spanning the dorsal side of the body, pumps nutrients, signaling proteins, and hormones to their target organs. The structural formation of the heart is regulated by Wg and Dpp signaling pathways through tinman (fly homolog of human NKX2-3, NKX2-5, and other NKX family members)6. A completely formed cardiovascular system consists of anterior ‘aorta’ and posterior ‘heart proper’ formed by contractile cardiomyocytes and non-contacting pericardial cells. Specialized cardiomyocytes form a valve-like structure inside the tube and help to pump the nutrients transferring hemolymph to the front part of the animal body7. Drosophila heart does not perform tissue oxygenation function; a separate tracheal system transfers the oxygen. Though this trait underlines a difference between flies and mammals, it allows various heart manipulations to be performed in Drosophila with reduced risk of lethal consequences8. Studies of hypoxia in flies have been reported and cardiac defects that arise from long-term hypoxia exposure have been investigated9. The utility of Drosophila as a model for cardiovascular research has been demonstrated for various types of cardiomyopathy8,10,11.
Cardiac optogenetics methods offer contactless precise control over cardiac cells activity by utilizing light-sensitive proteins, opsins12. This approach is applicable for heart function interventions and generation of heart disease simulations. Illuminating cardiac tissue expressing opsins provides the means to finely adjust heart rhythms and functionality. Excitatory opsins enable the activation of cardiac cells, inducing more frequent contractions, while inhibitory opsins mediate suppression of cardiac activities.
Optogenetics has established a strong connection with Drosophila research, mostly with neuroscience applications. The first report on using optogenetic approach in Drosophila dates to 2007, when ChR2-mediated neuronal activation helped to decipher larvae protective behavior13. Most publications on optogenetic applications that followed were focused on manipulation of neuronal circuits and behavioral studies. For example, Berni et al.14 described how activation of eNpHR2.0 in the larval brain resulted in neuronal blockade and temporal larva paralysis14. Later, excitatory and inhibitory opsins were brought into Drosophila cardiovascular research for functional studies; ChR2 and its derivative, ReaChR, were employed for the higher-than-normal heart rate optical pacing, and NpHR activation has been demonstrated to have an opposite effect causing bradycardia and restorable heart arrest15–19.
However, current optogenetic applications in Drosophila are limited to either heart inhibition or excitation; they lack an ability to control both activities in the same animal. Bi-directional control (increasing and decreasing HRs) would overcome this limitation by facilitating the coordinated manipulation of cardiac function and allow more comprehensive and insightful exploration of cardiac phenomena. In 2014, bi-directional optogenetic control of cardiac membrane potential was achieved in engineered cell cultures containing co-expressed ChR2 and Arch-T20. BiPOLES (bi-directional pair of opsins for light-induced excitation and silencing) was later proposed as an innovative optogenetic tool enabling bi-directional control of neuronal activity with two different wavelengths of light, showcasing its applicability in various organisms including worms, flies, mice, and ferrets21. Recently, BiPOLES were introduced into cardiomyocytes, providing precise modulation of cardiac physiology over engineered heart tissue22.
Taking inspiration from these concepts, we aimed to create a Drosophila model for bi-directional optogenetic control of heart function. Previously, we employed transgenic Drosophila models and successfully demonstrated tachycardia, bradycardia, and restorable heart arrest stimulations, non-invasively, using optogenetic approach15,17. However, only one type of modulation, either enhancement or reduction of the heart rate was performed in the fly where either an excitatory or inhibitory opsin was introduced. Here, we demonstrate and characterize a double-transgenic fly model with two opsins, ChR2 (mutant form H134R-ChR2) and eNpHR2.0. ChR2 coding gene originates from Chlamydomonas reinhardtii. It is a light-gated cation channel reacting to blue light (peak absorption at ~480 nm) resulting in quick cell membrane depolarization leading to reflective reactions: muscle contractions and neuronal activation with subsequent behavioral changes23–25. Modified halorhodopsin NpHR from Natronomonas pharaonic is an engineered light-gated chloride ion-pump excitable by ∼590 nm orange light; it causes cell membrane hyperpolarization. Activated eNpHR2.0 inhibits the neurons expressing it14,26,27. Individual optimization of H134R- ChR2 and eNpHR2.0 use in Drosophila heart was demonstrated previously15,17.
One key element in development of any cardiac study platform is the ability to visualize the heart function with high resolution and in a dynamic state. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) has been an important imaging modality for cardiac research in Drosophila. OCT is non-invasive, does not cause specimen destruction, and can serve as a primary method for 3D structural and functional in vivo imaging with high spatial resolution and high imaging speed28. In past studies, OCT helped to reveal the roles of Sox102F (human SOX5 ortholog), presenilin (gene linked to Alzheimer’s disease) in aging and cardiac dysfunction29,30. Our lab has developed a customized optical coherence microscopy (OCM) module for imaging the Drosophila cardiovascular system at all developmental stages15,16,31. OCM provides higher resolution than OCT through the use of an objective lens with higher numerical aperture.
Our main objective in this study was to expand our optogenetic tools by enabling bi-directional modulation of Drosophila heart function, from complete heart arrest to the maximum physiologically achievable tachypacing on the same animal. This effort was coupled with OCM imaging for a comprehensive characterization of heart performance; here, heart rate parameters were used to demonstrate the new tool’s utility. Thus, we are now able to simulate heart disease/stress episodes in live Drosophila and the procedure, from illumination to image acquisition is non-invasive and performed simultaneously. Imaging data was processed using FlyNet2.0+ software developed in the lab32. This represents significant improvement over the previously developed methods of beating heart imaging and heart pacing18,33,34. We have verified the presence of opsins and demonstrated cardiac pacing and heart arrest. Additionally, we performed a comprehensive assessment of our model, showing an ability to extend the range of reversible heart rhythm control to 0% and to 125% of the resting heart rate using relatively low light power density of several mW/mm2.

## Results

## Development of double-transgenic Drosophila model containing excitatory H134R-ChR2 and inhibitory eNpHR2.0 opsins
To achieve bi-directional control, we selected H134R-ChR2 as the excitatory opsin and eNpHR2.0 as the inhibitory opsin. We performed series of genetic crosses to establish a stable Drosophila stock carrying UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry and UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP transgenes as shown in Fig. 1a. These opsins provide control of the heart function without having overlapping spectra (Fig. 1b): H134R- ChR2 used to induce tachycardia is an excitatory opsin with peak absorption at 480 nm, while eNpHR2.0 used to stimulate bradycardia and heart arrest is an inhibitory opsin with peak absorption at 590 nm. The combination of these two opsins ensures separation of tissue inhibition/activation effects since stimulation wavelengths used (470 nm or 617 nm) only activate one opsin (Fig. 1b)35. Presence of both transgenic opsins was verified by PCR (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 2). To express UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry and UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP transgenes, we have tested several heart-specific GAL4 drivers and decided to use GMR88D05-GAL4. This driver contains a regulatory fragment from the gene Hand; Hand is a target of Tinman and is a transcription factor required for Drosophila cardiogenesis and hematopoiesis36,37. From this point, we will use Hand-GAL4 term instead of GMR88D05-GAL4 for clarity reasons. Hand > UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry; UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP animals were subjected to two-photon imaging to confirm the expression pattern (Fig. 1d). We detected heart-specific expression of both fluorescent markers, mCherry (co-expressed with H134-ChR2) and YFP (co-expressed with eNpHR2.0) in double transgenic line. Progeny from the cross between Hand-GAL4 and UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry or UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP show only mCherry or YFP signals, respectively. Therefore, both opsins are simultaneously expressed in the heart tissue of Hand > UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry; UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP animals.

## Demonstration of bi-directional heart function modulation
To show the feasibility of bi-directional heart control, we collected Hand > UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry; UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP animals (at least 5 3rd instar larvae and 5 white pupae) and determined their resting heart rate (RHR) during a ~15 s period (Fig. 2a-i–e-i) as previously described16. The Hand > UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry; UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP animals’ hearts function normally, without any significant arrhythmicity or other deviations (Figs. 2b-i, d-i). We used 470 nm blue light (~6.1 mW/mm2) to engage H134R-ChR2 for pacing experiments. We designed the illumination schedule as following: 5 s waiting time; 5 s series of light pulses; 5 s recovery time (Fig. 2a-ii). The frequency of the light pulses was determined as the measured RHR value plus 0.5 Hz. The pulse width was set at 20 ms. The representative examples of heart pacing results are shown in Fig. 2b-ii for 3rd instar larva and Fig. 2d-ii for early pupa. M-mode images (‘motion over time’ form of OCM images) show that the heart contractions faithfully follow the light pulses in larva and pupa, increasing the heart rate exactly by 0.5 Hz as prescribed (Fig. 2f). These experiments allowed us to create the heart condition known as tachycardia, where the heartbeat is increased. The same animals were used for the heart arrest demonstration. After 5 s waiting period, 617 nm red light (~5.3 mW/mm2), activating eNpHR2.0, was turned on for 5 s, followed by 5 s recovery time (Fig. 2a-iii). The heart stopped contracting during the illumination period. This effect was observed for both 3rd instar larvae and early pupae (Fig. 2b-iii and 2d-iii, Supplementary Videos 1–3). The heart beating was completely restored after the red light was turned off. Hand> yw animals not carrying the opsins did not respond to any light stimulation as shown in Fig. 2c, e.

## Determination of the applied light power levels sufficient for the heart control
The Drosophila heart control experiments described above were performed at the maximum light power levels produced by the LED sources: 6.1 mW/mm2 for blue light, and 5.3 mW/mm2 for red light sources. Our next goal was to determine the minimal light power level sufficient to achieve heart control. This step would help to reduce potentially detrimental effects from irradiation on live animals and extend variable heart manipulations; this would be particularly important for longitudinal studies. We tested the light power density at the 3rd instar larval (n = 12) and early pupal (n = 15) developmental stages.
Animals were subjected to light stimulations to pace or stop the heart starting from ~0.4 mW/mm2. The successful pacing or heart arrest event was scored according to the following criteria: the pacing event was recorded as successful only when the difference between the light pulse frequency and the resulting heart rate was less than ±0.1 Hz during the 5 s of blue light stimulation; successful heart arrest was scored only when the heart stopped for entire 5 s of red-light illumination.
For the blue light LED, we applied the excitation power densities starting from 0.4 mW/mm2 (~6% of maximum power) and gradually increasing it until reaching the successful (100% probability) heart pacing. The pacing frequencies were defined as RHR + 0.5 Hz for each animal. The pulse width was set as 20 ms in all pacing experiments. We have determined that efficient pacing occurs starting at ~1.5 mW/mm2 for larvae and ~2.3 mW/mm2 for pupae (Fig. 4a). We also performed experiments to determine the optimal red light (617 nm) power levels to induce the heart arrest. We initially applied red light at 0.2 mW/mm² (~4% of maximum power level). When gradually increasing the applied power, we observed that heart arrest occurred at ~2.0 mW/mm² and higher for larvae, and at ~0.7 mW/mm2 and higher for early pupae (Fig. 4b). Drosophila resting heart rates change during development, and larvae have higher RHR than pupa17. Our results reflect these physiological differences: it takes less light power to stop the pupae’s heart because it already beats at slower pace. The red-light optical exposure was well below the maximum permissible exposure (MPE) recommended by ANSI for safe use of lasers on the human skin (ANSI Z136.1)38.

## Bi-directional, wide range optogenetic cardiac control
Having optimized red (617 nm) and blue light (470 nm) power levels, we further characterized the controllable HR range for Hand > UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry; UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP 3rd instar larvae (n = 12) and early pupae (n = 17). After determining the RHR for each animal, we designed the red and blue light illumination schedules and performed series of heart rate modulation experiments aiming to gradually reduce the heart rate to 0% RHR (heart arrest) and increase to 200% RHR during 5 s stimulation. HR reduction was achieved by long pulses of red light (617 nm) and HR increase—by 20 ms pulses of blue light (470 nm) applied to the same animal. Figure 5 and Supplementary Data 1 summarize the heart rate modulation limits for 3rd instar larvae (Fig. 5a) and early pupae (Fig. 5b). Hand > UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry; UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP 3rd instar larvae and early pupae can tolerate gradual HR reduction down to a complete stop. Heart pacing with higher than RHR frequencies resulted in faithful HR increase to a roughly 125% of RHR level for larvae and pupae (Fig. 5c). The Supplementary Data 1 provides statistical analyses of HRs during initial 5 s (pre-pacing), illumination, and 5 s after pacing for multiple animals subjected to heart stimulation. Further increase of blue light pulse frequency did not result in linear increase of the heart rates; average HR for larvae remained at the same level and average HR for pupae exceeded the 125%.

## Discussion
We utilized Drosophila genetic tools and created a UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry; UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP double transgenic line through several rounds of genetic crosses; the opsins heart-specific expression was achieved by crossing this stock to the Hand-GAL4 driver. The progeny opsin expression pattern was verified by visualizing the fluorescent markers mCherry and YFP associated with ChR2-H134R and eNpHR2.0 transgenes, respectively. This is the first reported cardiac optogenetic instrument encompassing two opsins with opposite functions in Drosophila.
Notably, H134R-ChR2 and eNpHR2.0 respond to different light colors, blue and orange, well separated in the spectrum. In our experiments we used 470 nm and 617 nm LED sources, efficiently stimulating the heart without any crosstalk interference: 470 nm light did not activate NpHR2.0 mediated bradycardia and 617 nm did not induce ChR2-H134R mediated tachycardia. The ability to induce both bradycardia and tachycardia greatly expands the types of arrythmia that can be modeled using this system.
We determined the physiological limits of Hand > UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry; UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP animals heart rate manipulations. 3rd instar larvae and early pupae can tolerate bradycardia episodes consistently, responding to red light long pulses reducing the heart rate. We have demonstrated gradual heart rate reduction to 75%, 50%, and 25% of the RHR. Stimulating the heart arrest for 5 s by red-light illumination is also quite feasible; after the light was turned off, the heart beating re-started and eventually (outside of the post-stimulation period of 5 s) restored to the RHR level. Figure 2b (iii) and 2d (iii) shows notably increased HR compared to the initial RHR, 3.6 Hz from ~2.4 Hz for larva, and ~2.4 Hz from ~2.0 Hz for pupa, immediately after the light stimulus was removed. The extent of the HR increase and the recovery timing from the eNpHR2.0 mediated heart arrest was previously observed and characterized17. Men et al.17 expressed eNpHR2.0 opsin using 24B-GAL4 driver which directs broader UAS-eNpHR2.0 transgene activation. Nevertheless, 5 s heart arrest caused 30% HR increase and required on average 4.62 s to return to the RHR. Drosophila can sustain and recover from the heart arrest; studying the recovery processes may shed light on the basic mechanisms preserving the heart functionality and neuronal regulation. Optical tachypacing at 125%, 150%, 175% to 200% of RHR, with optimized blue light power, helped to determine the highest possible pacing frequencies for Hand > UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry; UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP 3rd instar larvae and early pupae. The highest pacing physiological limit is approximately 125% of their RHR. Subsequent increases in the pulse frequency did not produce an equal heart rate increase. The average heart rate remained at ~125% of RHR for both developmental stages. The demonstrated pacing limits are consistent with the levels previously shown for 24B > UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry, ~127% of RHR for larvae and ~121% of RHR for pupae under comparable blue light power conditions15. However, tachypacing of larvae and pupae carrying red activatable channelrhodopsin (ReaChR) driven by 24B-GAL4 resulted in significantly wider pacing window, up to ~167% of RHR for larvae and ~218% of RHR for pupae17. These results seem to be counterintuitive, since the H134R-ChR2 opsin can generate higher amplitude photocurrent with rapid inactivation (important for neuromodulation); ReaChR generates lower current and has slower kinetics39,40, but apparently, this is more beneficial for cardiac pacing, based on the experimental data available. However, blue light delivery in live tissues is obstructed by stronger absorption of endogenous chromophores and greater scattering41 than for red light, which may lead to lower light irradiance at the heart level. Furthermore, Drosophila heart can tolerate electric tachypacing resulting in ~250% of RHR in w1118 pupae for at least 30 s without visible detrimental effects34. Therefore, most likely the 25% increase over the RHR is determined by H134R-ChR2 opsin specifics in Hand > UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry; UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP system, and not the Drosophila heart physiological limits.
Our double H134R- ChR2; eNpHR2.0 transgenic Drosophila system and dual-color light stimulation (470 nm and 617 nm LED) integrated OCM system can induce and detect the increasing heart rate and restorable heart arrest in the same animal. The new tool has the potential to provide important insights of cardiovascular system regulation mechanisms and greatly facilitate the research efforts to identify new heart disease therapeutic candidates. The complex nature of the heart disorders includes genetics, diet, age, environment, and other risk factors42. A substantial number of investigations are focusing on screens to identify genes affecting cardiomyocyte remodeling, functional drug screenings, metabolic studies43–47. Identification of positive hits is also based on the response to electric pacing of Drosophila heart. The procedure results in specimen destruction and requires a separate module for heart imaging and data processing. Our optogenetic approach offers tunable, non-invasive heart rate manipulations. Heart pacing and imaging data collection are not labor-intensive and time-consuming. Experimental flexibility and automatic data processing open broad application possibilities for this new optogenetic cardiovascular research platform.
Further development of new optogenetic tools is not limited by our selection of the opsins. For example, red light sensitive excitatory opsin (e.g., ReaChR) and blue light triggered inhibitory opsin (e.g., stGtACR248) can be combined using similar procedures described in this study to achieve successful performance within the same light power density range. For those labs without access to OCM/OCT devices, it is possible to incorporate an optical reporter such as GCaMP to visualize the calcium gradients during the heart function alteration. There is also a wide range of optogenetic tools available, allowing the selection with the trade-off between sensitivity, light wavelength, and kinetics. Although this study demonstrates an application in cardiology, our double-transgenic model can be also easily translated into neural experiments, just by crossing UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry; UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP to neuron specific GAL4 drivers.
In conclusion, we have developed and characterized a Drosophila model with bi-directional cardiac optogenetic control by expressing ChR2-H134R and NpHR2.0, allowing precise heart rate manipulation without interference. We explored the limits of heart rate control, finding that larvae and pupae can tolerate heart arrest, various levels of bradycardia, and exhibit a maximum sustainable pacing frequency of approximately 125% of RHR. This instrument provides a versatile platform for investigating a wide range of cardiac pathology models in Drosophila. The majority of the existing models reviewed in ref. 49 were built on the knowledge of the disease cause (e.g., human homologous gene or a pathway, metabolic conditions, age) and the resulting heart abnormality. Our approach allows to create and tune up the abnormal heart conditions (somewhat similar to ischemia-reperfusion injury in mammals) and investigate the nature and molecular mechanism of the recovery processes, potentially leading to discovery of new cardioprotective therapies.

## Methods

## D. melanogaster husbandry and genetic crosses
D. melanogaster stocks were obtained from Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center: w[*]; P{w[+mC]=UAS-H134R-ChR2}2 (#28995), w[*]; P{y[+t7.7] w[+mC]=UAS-eNpHR-YFP}attP2 (#41752), w[1118]; P{y[+t7.7] w[+mC]=GMR88D05-GAL4}attP2 (#48396). yw; CyO; TM3/ T(2; 3)ap[Xa] stock is a gift from Sarah Elgin. All stocks were kept on standard cornmeal media at room temperature. Crosses were set at 25 °C, 70% humidity; progeny development was protected from light.
P{y[+t7.7] w[+mC]=GMR88D05-GAL4}attP2 (designated as Hand-GAL4) is segregating with TM3 Sb[1] chromosome. We replaced the TM3 balancer with TM6 Sb Tb16. Genetic crosses scheme used for ChR2-H134R; eNpHR2.0 transgenic line generation is shown in Fig. 1a. Genetic crosses to generate Hand-GAL4 > ChR2-H134R; eNpHR2.0 progeny were set in vials with standard cornmeal media; after 48 h the parents were transferred to vials with all-trans-retinal (ATR) containing media and kept in the dark16.

## Transgene confirmation by PCR
UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry and UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP presence in the double transgenic line in was confirmed by PCR. H134R -ChR2 primers were: 5’-CAGCATTTTGCTGCTGATGT-3’ and 5’-TACCCCAGCTCACGAAAAAC-3’ producing 488 bp band. eNpHR2.0 primers were 5’-TGCTGGGAGGAGAAGAGGTA-3’ and 5’-GCCACGATATCCAGGAAAGA-3’ resulting in 442 bp product. DNA was extracted from 2 flies as described in GD Protocols/Transgene verification of VDRC (Vienna, Austria). GoTaq® Green Master Mix (Promega, Cat #M7122) was used for DNA amplification.

## Two-photon imaging
The expression of mCherry and YFP fluorescent markers in UAS-H134R-ChR2-mCherry and UAS-eNpHR2.0-YFP transgenes was visualized by two-photon (2P) imaging. The 2P images were obtained using Leica Sp8-DIVE (Deep In Vivo Explorer) Multiphoton Microscope system (Biology Imaging Facility, Washington University) with 10X HC PL APO CS2 objective. The whole pupae image acquisition and processing was done by LAS X software, integrated with the imaging system. YFP was excited by 1045 nm light and mCherry was excited by 1130 nm light. The emission wavelength for YFP was set between 510–550 nm and for mCherry at 590–650 nm. The field of view was set as 512 × 512. Two sequences for YFP and mCherry channels were set to scan between frames. The step size was set at 3 micrometers, and the total number of frames ranged from 50 to 130, depending on the specimen size and thickness. The output was saved in a.tiff file; post-sequence image processing (image stitching, brightness change, contrast change, and image labeling) was done by ImageJ.

## Specimen preparation
3rd instar larvae or early (‘white’) pupae with desirable genotype (non Tb, non Sb, fluorescent markers present) were removed from a vial, cleaned with a wet painter’s brush, wiped by the Kimwipe tissue, and attached to the microscope slide dorsal side up and perpendicular to the long side of the slide using the double-sided Scotch tape. If necessary, the larvae can be gently pressed by the brush or tweezers to ensure immobility.

## Optogenetic control of the Drosophila heart
Supplementary Fig. 1 outlines the OCM system. Current OCM setup uses a super-luminescent diode (SLD) as the light source (center wavelength: ~850 nm, FWHM: ~165 nm). Using a 10x objective lens, the OCM imaging system can achieve an axial resolution of ~3.3 µm in tissue and a lateral resolution of ~2.8 µm. Interference signals of reflected light from the reference arm and the sample arm are detected using a spectrometer with a 2048-pixel line scan camera with a max line rate of 80 kHz. The measured system sensitivity is ~95.1 dB. 617 and 470 nm LEDs (Migtex System, BLS-LCS-0617-03-22; Migtex System, BLS-LCS-0470-03-22) were synchronized with image acquisition using high-speed multichannel I/O device (National Instruments, USB-6343). The LEDs were focused onto the sample, obtaining a spot size with a radius of ~1.7 mm and power density of ~6 mW/mm2.
The specimen was fixed on a glass slide and placed on the adjustable stage. Specimen position was adjusted by micromanipulators to focus on the beating heart. The OCM light beam projection on the larva/pupa’s body covers the area between A5 and A8 segments. Imaging parameters were set as 128 A-scans, 2000 B-scans, and a 12.5 µs exposure time to achieve a frame rate of ~125 frames per second. The initial 15 s recordings were used to determine the resting heart rate (RHR) of each animal (Fig. 2a-i–e-i).

## Pacing procedure
Heart pacing was conducted by 470 nm blue light pulses, 20 ms wide (Fig. 2a-ii). Each recording consisted of a 5 s waiting period, 5 s pacing period, and 5 s recovery period. The pacing stimulation frequency was set for each animal by adding 0.5 Hz to the RHR determined initially. Heart arrest and bradycardia stimulations were performed using 617 nm red light (Fig. 2a-iii). The heart arrest illumination schedule combined 5 s waiting period, 5 s continuous illumination, and 5 s recovery period. The reduction of the heart rate (bradypacing) was achieved by substituting the single 5 s long pulse by the series of light pulses with RHR/2 or RHR/4 frequency during 5 s; the duty cycle for each pulse was 80%.

## Light power optimization
To determine the optimal light power level for pacing experiments, we designed six to seven light power settings ranging from the maximum power value and to the level when we stopped observing heart pacing effects. Specific power settings were established based on consideration of the developmental stage and the wavelength of light used for pacing. Following the imaging protocol, we acquired at least three recordings per power setting on the same animal, starting with lower power intensities and progressively increasing them. We repeated this procedure on multiple animals of each developmental stage.

## Pacing limits
To establish the limits of heart rate control, we incrementally adjusted the pacing frequency (0%, 25%, 50%, 100% = RHR, 125%, 150%, 175%, 200% of RHR) at the same optimal power level determined previously. Pacing procedures at each light frequency level were repeated at least three times before moving on to the next level. Each animal was first subjected to blue light tachypacing from 100% to 200% of the RHR and continued with red-light bradypacing decreasing the HR from 100% to 0% of the RHR.

## Data processing
Imaging data was processed with our custom FlyNet2.0+ program32,50, which segmented the heart region and extracted the heart rate, heart dimensions and fractional shortening values. During the data analyses, we defined successful pacing as three consecutive captures, with a heart rate difference of less than 0.1 Hz observed among the three recordings for each light intensity. Pacing probability refers to the likelihood of the individual animal achieving successful pacing under a specific power level. Inhibition probability represents the likelihood that cardiac arrest or bradycardia will be induced by the given light pulses. Both probabilities were determined by the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE), following a binomial distribution. MLE is calculated as the number of successfully paced animals divided by the total number of individuals in these two probability analyses. Error bars were shown as standard error of the mean (SEM) in Fig. 4 and standard deviation (SD) in Fig. 5c.

## Statistics and reproducibility
Statistical parameters (Mean, SD, SEM) were calculated using customed code in MATLAB (The MathWorks Inc., USA). Total sample size ranges from 12 to 17 per group. No statistical methods were used to determine sample sizes, we collected animals with desired genotypes. Figures 2 and 3 demonstrate representative results that were selected based on the image quality from the repeated experiments. For the light power density optimization experiment, 12 3rd instar larvae and 15 early pupae were included, as shown in Fig. 4. Similarly, for the experiment testing the extent of controllable heart rate, 12 3rd instar larvae and 17 early pupae were included, as illustrated in Fig. 5c. Data are represented as mean ± SEM in Fig. 4 and mean ± SD in Fig. 5c. T-tests in the Supplementary Data 1 were performed as two-sided student’s t-tests. Results were assigned as significant when p < 0.05.
