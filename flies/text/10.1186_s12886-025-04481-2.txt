AmblyoPlay-based multimodal visual therapy in pediatric anisometropic amblyopia: a pilot study of visual, oculomotor and sensorimotor outcomes.

# AmblyoPlay-based multimodal visual therapy in pediatric anisometropic amblyopia: a pilot study of visual, oculomotor and sensorimotor outcomes

## Abstract
AbstractBackgroundThis pilot exploratory study aimed to assess the impact of a 6-month app-based multimodal visual therapy program (AmblyoPlay®) on visual acuity (VA), stereopsis, oculomotor control, motor proficiency, and postural stability in children with anisometropic amblyopia.MethodsThis study, non-randomized controlled study included 29 children aged 7–13 years. Group I (n = 14) had anisometropic amblyopia and performed daily 20-minute gamified dichoptic therapy using AmblyoPlay® for 6 months. Group II (n = 15) were age- and sex-matched typically developing controls without intervention. Assessments included VA (LogMAR), stereopsis (Titmus Fly), oculomotor parameters (saccadic latency, smooth pursuit gain, optokinetic gain), motor proficiency (BOT-2 subtests), and postural stability (Sensory Organization Test). Group I was evaluated at baseline, 1, 3, and 6 months; controls at baseline and 6 months. Friedman and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for within-group comparisons; Mann–Whitney U tests for between-group differences.ResultsIn Group I, amblyopic eye LogMAR VA improved from 0.23 ± 0.10 to 0.08 ± 0.05 at 6 months (p < 0.001), and stereopsis improved from 494.3 ± 874.5 to 60.7 ± 28.9 arcsec (p < 0.001). Significant reductions in saccadic latency and increases in smooth pursuit and optokinetic gains were observed (all p < 0.05). Motor proficiency and postural stability composite scores also improved significantly (p < 0.05). No significant changes occurred in controls.ConclusionsAmblyoPlay-based multimodal visual therapy led to significant improvements in visual and oculomotor function, with secondary benefits in motor coordination and balance, in pediatric anisometropic amblyopia.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04481-2.Keywords: Anisometropic amblyopia, AmblyoPlay, Dichoptic therapy, Oculomotor function, Hand-eye coordination, Balance

## Background
Amblyopia is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by reduced best-corrected visual acuity in one or both eyes, in the absence of any structural ocular abnormalities [1, 2]. The primary etiologies of amblyopia include strabismus, visual deprivation, and notably anisometropia a refractive imbalance between the two eyes that disrupts normal binocular integration [3, 4]. In anisometropic amblyopia, the more ametropic eye continuously receives a blurred retinal image, which over time leads to cortical suppression and abnormal development of binocular visual pathways [4–6].
Traditional therapeutic strategies for amblyopia have primarily focused on monocular VA restoration, often through occlusion therapy (patching) or pharmacological penalization (e.g., atropine drops). These methods aim to stimulate the amblyopic eye (AE) by functionally suppressing the dominant eye, thus encouraging neural activation in underutilized visual pathways [7]. Clinical evidence indicates that the success of these interventions is highly age-dependent, with outcomes significantly more favorable when treatment is initiated before the age of five [8].
Despite their longstanding use, conventional approaches exhibit notable limitations. Prolonged occlusion can be uncomfortable and socially stigmatizing for children, leading to poor adherence and suboptimal outcomes [9, 10]. Psychosocial consequences such as reduced self-esteem and social withdrawal are frequently reported, particularly in school-aged populations [10]. Furthermore, traditional therapies predominantly target acuity deficits, while often neglecting broader functional impairments namely binocular vision, stereopsis, oculomotor control, and visuomotor coordination [6, 11]. These residual deficits may persist even after acuity normalization, thereby affecting motor competence and quality of life.
Considering these challenges, contemporary treatment modalities have increasingly turned toward digital health innovations and gamified interventions. These newer methods are designed not only to improve acuity but also to enhance binocular integration and visual–motor function through interactive, child-friendly platforms [12]. One such approach, dichoptic training, involves presenting complementary stimuli to each eye under conditions that necessitate binocular cooperation, thereby reducing interocular suppression and promoting functional binocularity [13, 14]. Studies suggest that such app-based or virtual reality therapies can increase compliance and engagement by transforming treatment into an immersive, rewarding experience [12]. To date, there have been a limited number of studies investigating the effect of multimodal visual rehabilitation on motor movements, oculomotor skills, and balance performance (static–dynamic) in children with anisometropic amblyopia, especially those that are practice-based and delivered through gamified visual exercises, assessed across extended follow-up durations [5, 15, 16].
Beyond visual deficits, amblyopia is now recognized as a multisystem neurodevelopmental disorder that extends beyond a decline in visual acuity and may affect multiple aspects of motor and perceptual functioning [17–19]. When it occurs in early childhood during the critical phase of visual system maturation—it may exert long-lasting effects on cortical plasticity and binocular integration [20, 21]. Functional impairments associated with amblyopia are not confined to visual parameters alone; deficits have been reported in oculomotor control, postural stability, hand–eye coordination, and motor planning [5, 18, 19, 21, 22]. The lack of functional binocularity can reduce a child’s ability to respond to environmental stimuli, maintain balance, and coordinate complex motor behaviors, particularly during key developmental periods [23, 24]. These findings support the concept that amblyopia affects not only vision but also sensorimotor integration, underscoring the rationale for comprehensive evaluation across visual, oculomotor, and balance domains in the present study.
This study aimed to evaluate the effects of a six-month, gamified, app-based multimodal visual therapy program (AmblyoPlay®), incorporating oculomotor, selective attention, and hand–eye coordination exercises based on dichoptic principles, on visual, oculomotor, and sensorimotor functions in children with anisometropic amblyopia.
To the best of our knowledge, the present work constitutes the first peer-reviewed clinical investigation assessing the AmblyoPlay® digital vision-training platform in a pediatric anisometropic amblyopia cohort. Given the absence of prior published outcome data, the current study should be interpreted as an exploratory pilot trial aimed at characterizing preliminary therapeutic effects of this multimodal digital intervention.
By addressing amblyopia as a multisystem condition rather than a purely visual deficit, this work provides preliminary clinical evidence that may inform future research and guide the integration of digital multimodal approaches into pediatric amblyopia management.

## Materials and methods

## Study design and ethical approval
This prospective, controlled study was conducted at the Department of Audiology, XXX University, between August 2023 and May 2025, in collaboration with the Ophthalmology Outpatient Clinic of Medipol University Hospital. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Istanbul Medipol University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval number: E-10840098-772.02-5860; date: 15 September 2023). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants prior to inclusion.

## Participants
Children aged 7 to 13 years were recruited and assigned to two groups: an anisometropic amblyopia group (Group I) and an age- and sex-matched control group (Group II).
The diagnosis of anisometropic amblyopia was established according to the criteria of the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO). Each participant underwent a comprehensive ophthalmological evaluation conducted by a single board-certified pediatric ophthalmologist to ensure diagnostic consistency. The Cover–Uncover Test was performed to rule out strabismus, and a dilated fundoscopic examination was used to evaluate the optic nerve head, macula, and retinal integrity.
Anisometropic amblyopia was defined as an interocular difference of at least two lines in best-corrected visual acuity (VA) on the Snellen chart and a refractive error difference of ≥ 1.00 diopter in either spherical or cylindrical power between the two eyes.
A priori power analysis was performed using GPower 3.1.9* for a repeated-measures ANOVA (within–between interaction). Assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.5), α = 0.05, and power (1–β) = 0.90, a minimum of 12 participants per group was required to achieve adequate statistical power.
A total of 29 children were enrolled: 14 in Group I and 15 in Group II.
Inclusion criteria for Group I were as follows: age between 7 and 13 years; diagnosis of anisometropic amblyopia in accordance with AAO guidelines; absence of vestibular or neurological pathology within the previous six months; no cognitive or developmental impairments; and ability to comply with the treatment protocol. Participants had not received prior amblyopia therapy other than refractive correction.
For those requiring new or updated spectacle prescriptions, full optical correction was prescribed, and the AmblyoPlay®-based digital rehabilitation program was initiated promptly thereafter, once stable binocular vision under correction was confirmed.
Baseline (T0) measurements were obtained after the new correction had been dispensed, ensuring that observed improvements primarily reflected the effects of the digital therapy rather than early optical adaptation.
Inclusion criteria for Group II were as follows: age between 7 and 13 years; normal ocular health with no evidence of amblyopia, strabismus, or refractive error; absence of vestibular, neurological, or cognitive impairments; and visual acuity of 20/25 (≤ 0.1 logMAR) or better in each eye without the need for optical correction. Participants who failed to meet any inclusion criterion were excluded.
The flow of participant recruitment, allocation, intervention, and follow-up is presented according to the CONSORT 2010 guidelines (Fig. 1). No participants were lost to follow-up, and data from all enrolled subjects were included in the final analyses.

## Diagnostic procedures
All diagnostic evaluations were performed under standardized illumination and environmental conditions.
During the AmblyoPlay® digital training, participants wore their spectacle correction with clip-on red–green anaglyph dichoptic filters to enable binocular image separation.
For VNG testing, recordings were obtained without spectacles, as the infrared video-oculography goggles are not compatible with corrective lenses.
Conversely, CDP and BOT-2 evaluations were performed with full spectacle correction, ensuring that all postural and coordination assessments were carried out under optimal visual conditions.
Visual acuity was measured monocularly using a paper Snellen chart (6 m/40 cm) and converted to logMAR values for analysis. Crowded line presentation was used, and a line was credited when ≥ 50% of its letters were correctly identified. Cycloplegic refraction was performed for all participants, and refractive errors were recorded as spherical equivalents (SE).
Stereopsis was assessed using the Titmus Fly Stereo Test (Stereo Optical Co., Chicago, USA), which provides a three-level evaluation of depth perception via the Fly (gross stereopsis; 3500 arcsec), Animals (intermediate; 400–100 arcsec), and Circles (fine stereopsis; 100–40 arcsec) subtests. The test was administered at 40 cm with polarized glasses, and the smallest correctly identified disparity was recorded as the stereoacuity threshold in arcseconds.
Oculomotor performance was evaluated using the Micromedical Technologies VisualEyes Spectrum videonystagmography (VNG) system. The test battery included gaze, saccade, pursuit, and optokinetic subtests. Fixation stability and microsaccades were assessed during the gaze test; latency, peak velocity, and accuracy were recorded during saccades; pursuit gain (eye velocity/target velocity) was calculated for smooth tracking of horizontally and vertically moving targets; and optokinetic responses were analyzed for gain symmetry. In Group I, both eyes were tested separately (first the amblyopic eye, then the fellow eye under alternate occlusion), while in Group II both eyes were recorded binocularly. All VNG procedures followed validated and published device protocols.
Postural control was examined using the NeuroCom SMART EquiTest Balance Master computerized dynamic posturography (CDP) system. Balance performance was measured with the Sensory Organization Test (SOT) at baseline and at the 1st, 3rd, and 6th months of follow-up. Each of the six progressively challenging conditions lasted 20 s and was repeated three times. The SOT quantified sensory integration across visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive systems, with participants secured in safety harnesses. Composite equilibrium scores were compared with age-appropriate normative data provided by the manufacturer.
Motor proficiency was evaluated using the Bruininks–Oseretsky Test of Motor Proficiency, Second Edition (BOT-2) [25, 26], a standardized instrument assessing gross and fine motor performance in individuals aged 4–21 years. Due to the comprehensive nature of the full test, only the balance and upper-limb coordination subtests were administered. The balance subtest (maximum score: 37) and upper-limb coordination subtest (maximum score: 39) were performed following the standardized BOT-2 manual. Since age- and sex-based normative data were not applied, intergroup comparisons between amblyopia and control participants served as the reference for interpretation.
All ophthalmological evaluations were conducted by the same pediatric ophthalmologist, and all VNG, CDP, and BOT-2 assessments were performed by the same experienced audiologist/clinician to ensure methodological consistency and eliminate inter-rater variability.

## Intervention program
Participants in the amblyopia group underwent a six-month visual rehabilitation program using the AmblyoPlay® platform (Smart Optometry, Slovenia), which employs dichoptic therapy principles via interactive, gamified exercises. AmblyoPlay® is a commercially available vision-training system that delivers binocular visual stimulation but is not cleared by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or CE-certified for amblyopia treatment; hence, the present study represents a pilot clinical evaluation of its feasibility and short-term outcomes.
The training program included interactive games such as Collect Shapes,Balloon Pop,Flying Candy,Catch the Birdy,Tasty Tiles,Cross the Road,Catch Monsters,Flappy Bird,Planet Shooter,Jumping Ball,Maze, and Blocks. Before each session, the system’s built-in alignment and luminance-balancing procedure was performed using red–green anaglyph glasses to ensure accurate dichoptic separation. The amblyopic eye was presented with high-salience (high-contrast) stimuli, while the fellow eye viewed attenuated input through the red filter. Interocular balance was automatically adjusted by the device’s calibration algorithm, which was executed before every session to maintain stable binocular stimulation.
The therapy was conducted at home five days per week, with each session lasting 30 min. Compliance was monitored through the application’s cloud-based tracking system, and progress reports were reviewed weekly by the research team. Participants who failed to demonstrate binocular fusion during pre-assessment were excluded.
The program incorporated a proprietary adaptive algorithm that dynamically modified game content and difficulty according to the participant’s performance metrics (accuracy, reaction time, and consistency).
Upon first login, participants entered their age, sex, and the affected eye (right, left, or bilateral), followed by a short self-guided contrast calibration task in which they adjusted the interocular contrast until a target object (e.g., a fish or cat) became equally visible to both eyes on the screen.
This process established the initial binocular balance, which the algorithm subsequently fine-tuned during therapy.
As training progressed, the system continuously analyzed performance indicators accuracy, completion rate, and earned points—and automatically adjusted stimulus contrast, spatial frequency, and game speed to maintain an optimal challenge level.
Difficulty levels increased as performance improved and decreased in response to performance decline, maintaining engagement and motivation. This individualized adaptation ensured optimal cognitive load and maximized neuroplastic stimulation throughout training.

## Outcome measures
The primary outcome measure was improvement in best-corrected visual acuity (logMAR). Secondary outcomes included changes in stereopsis, oculomotor function (VNG parameters), postural stability (CDP composite scores), and motor coordination (BOT-2 subtests).

## Statistical analysis
All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v26. Data normality was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Since most variables were non-normally distributed, non-parametric tests were applied. Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and median (min–max) for continuous variables and frequencies (%) for categorical variables.
Within-group comparisons were analyzed using the Friedman test, followed by Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction (p < 0.0125). Between-group comparisons (baseline vs. 6th month) were evaluated with the Mann–Whitney U test.

## Discussion
This 6-month AmblyoPlay®-based multimodal visual therapy program produced statistically and clinically meaningful gains across visual and sensorimotor domains in children with anisometropic amblyopia. In the amblyopic eye, mean visual acuity improved by 0.15 logMAR (from 0.23 to 0.08), which corresponds to approximately 1.5 lines on a logMAR chart (noting that Snellen “lines” are not directly equivalent). Stereopsis improved from 494 arcsec to 61 arcsec, representing a ~ 3-octave gain in binocular depth perception. These changes were accompanied by reductions in saccadic latency and increases in smooth-pursuit and optokinetic gains, alongside improvements in BOT-2 balance and upper-limb coordination subtests, whereas controls remained stable. Collectively, the findings indicate that structured, gamified dichoptic training can enhance both acuity and functional binocularity, with concurrent benefits to oculomotor and motor performance.
Numerous studies have evaluated dichoptic and binocular therapies across different age groups and control designs, including comparisons with glasses-only or patching protocols. However, only a few have specifically addressed school-age children with anisometropic amblyopia using extended follow-up periods. In our cohort (7–13 years, 6-month training), the 0.15-logMAR mean improvement aligns with the magnitude reported in similar trials. For instance, Holmes et al. observed modest but significant visual acuity gains in children aged 7–12 years undergoing 4 weeks of dichoptic gaming [27], while Hsieh et al. demonstrated superior outcomes following structured vision therapy in 7–10-year-olds with unilateral refractive amblyopia [28]. Likewise, Hernández-Rodríguez et al. documented significant enhancements in both VA and stereopsis after 6 months of digital visual training combined with patching in children of comparable age [29]. A systematic review by Hernández-Rodríguez and Piñero further corroborated the general efficacy of active vision therapy in anisometropic amblyopia [30]. Together, these findings support that our results are broadly consistent with the existing dichoptic literature, with slightly greater gains likely attributable to our multimodal design incorporating visual-motor and oculomotor components to promote cortical integration.
Our findings are consistent with controlled trials evaluating binocular and vision-therapy–based interventions in children of comparable age and amblyopia subtype. In a multicenter randomized trial, Holmes et al. evaluated the Dig Rush binocular game in children aged 7–12 years and reported modest but significant improvements in visual acuity, highlighting the feasibility of dichoptic gaming in this age group [27]. Similarly, Hsieh et al. demonstrated meaningful acuity gains in children aged 7–10 years with unilateral refractive amblyopia following structured vision therapy compared with standard optical correction [28]. Extending these findings, Hernández-Rodríguez et al. observed enhanced visual and binocular outcomes after a 6-month digital-platform–based visual training combined with patching, reinforcing the benefit of multimodal digital rehabilitation in school-age children [29]. Furthermore, the systematic review by Hernández-Rodríguez and Piñero summarized evidence supporting the efficacy of active vision therapy in anisometropic amblyopia, providing additional theoretical backing for multimodal rehabilitation frameworks [30]. While Kelly et al.’s younger cohort (< 8 years) exhibited larger short-term improvements with iPad-based dichoptic games, such differences likely reflect age-dependent neuroplasticity rather than methodological inconsistency [31]. Collectively, these studies—and our results—support the effectiveness of structured, gamified, and multimodal binocular training approaches in anisometropic amblyopia, with treatment response modulated by age, amblyopia type, and training duration.
Participants performed AmblyoPlay®-based dichoptic training at home under parental supervision. The platform automatically sent daily reminders to prompt gaming sessions and recorded user activity logs, including date, duration, and task completion. Clinicians could monitor adherence remotely via a color-coded dashboard (green = completed, yellow = partially completed, red = missed), providing qualitative tracking of engagement. Although this monitoring ensured consistent participation, a quantitative compliance index was not available, which limits our ability to correlate individual adherence with visual outcomes. Therefore, our results reflect improvements achieved under supervised and structured usage rather than uncontrolled real-world condition.
In this cohort, a 6-month digital dichoptic therapy program using AmblyoPlay® was associated with statistically significant and potentially clinically meaningful improvements in visual acuity (VA) in children with amblyopia. VA gains were observed at 1, 3, and 6 months, with marked improvements from baseline to the 3rd and 6th months, suggesting a cumulative therapeutic effect and being consistent with the plastic potential of the developing visual system. The present findings provide preliminary clinical evidence suggesting that multimodal dichoptic digital training may enhance visual and oculomotor outcomes in pediatric anisometropic amblyopia.
In line with previous evidence, our findings further support the efficacy of structured home-based and feedback-driven dichoptic interventions in pediatric amblyopia. Piñero et al. demonstrated that 6 weeks of home-based dichoptic visual training produced significant improvements in both visual acuity and stereopsis among children with amblyopia, highlighting the practicality and short-term efficacy of supervised digital programs [32]. Similarly, Caoli et al. developed a dichoptic feedback-based oculomotor training paradigm that successfully manipulated interocular alignment and improved binocular cooperation, emphasizing the potential contribution of oculomotor recalibration to functional visual recovery [33]. These studies complement our results, where a longer-term (6-month) multimodal dichoptic rehabilitation protocol integrating perceptual, oculomotor, and visual-motor tasks yielded clinically meaningful gains in visual acuity and stereopsis. Collectively, the evidence suggests that both home-based dichoptic practice and feedback-mediated oculomotor adaptation can strengthen binocular integration and visual plasticity in children with anisometropic amblyopia.
Beyond visual gains, several studies have explored the impact of dichoptic and feedback-based paradigms on oculomotor behavior, providing further insight into the mechanisms underlying binocular rehabilitation.
Chow et al. examined saccadic latency, velocity, and amplitude in 10 individuals with amblyopia (4 anisometropic, 6 strabismic/mixed) using a mirror haploscope system [34]. They found a significantly longer latency in saccades made with the AE. Interestingly, no group differences were observed in amplitude or peak velocity values. Moreover, the study revealed marked delays in attentional focus and target engagement mechanisms in the AE, suggesting that increased latency may be more closely associated with attentional control deficits than with motor output dysfunction.
In a study conducted by Niechwiej-Szwedo et al. [35] involving 13 individuals with amblyopia, the average saccadic latency in the AE was reported as 220 ms (compared to 180 ms in controls), with an accuracy rate of 83% (controls: 93%). Additionally, the rate of corrective saccades was significantly higher in the amblyopic group. These findings indicate delays in visual targeting and motor execution, particularly among the anisometropic subgroup.
Raashid et al. [36], in a study including 11 individuals with anisometropic amblyopia and 14 controls, measured smooth pursuit initiation times. The initiation delay in the AE was 206 ± 20 ms, whereas it was 183 ± 17 ms in healthy individuals. Although there was no significant difference in velocity gain during the open-loop and steady-state phases, greater variability was observed in the amblyopic group, which may suggest impaired initiation of pursuit, despite preserved sustainment capability.
In the present study, a 6-month digital dichoptic visual rehabilitation program using the AmblyoPlay® system was associated with improvements in oculomotor functions. Notably, from the third month onward, we observed improvements in fixation stability, reductions in saccadic latency (pre-treatment: 218 ms, post-treatment: 184 ms), and increases in smooth pursuit accuracy. Additionally, the rate of corrective saccades during pursuit decreased from 30% to 12%. These findings suggest that the oculomotor system may be responsive to treatment and highlight the importance of monitoring these functions throughout the rehabilitation process.
Hand–eye coordination is a complex process requiring the timely perception of visual input and its integration with precise motor responses, particularly essential for upper extremity fine motor skills. In early-onset visual disorders such as amblyopia, disruptions in this integration can lead to deficits in reaching, grasping, and spatial orientation [37–39]. In our study, the impact of digital-based exercises on hand–eye coordination in children with amblyopia was assessed using the BOT-2 upper limb coordination subtest, which showed significant improvement, especially between baseline and the 6th month.
Previous research aligns with these findings. Hernández-Andrés et al. [40] found that visual rehabilitation combined with patching produced greater improvements in VA and stereopsis than patching alone or with perceptual learning. Our structured digital protocol similarly may emphasize goal-directed visual–motor tasks, which could have contributed to the observed motor gains. Likewise, Niechwiej-Szwedo et al. [41] reported enhanced processing speed and attention following perceptual learning–based hand–eye training, supporting the possibility of improved cognitive–motor integration.
Other studies have shown parallel effects. Birch et al. [42] demonstrated functional visual and motor improvements in amblyopic children following a short-term, home-based dichoptic gaming intervention. Lagrèze et al. [43] reported that task-based therapies significantly enhanced hand–eye coordination, attention, and bimanual control, whereas patching alone did not yield comparable motor benefits. The digital exercises in our study, which involved continuous feedback and bimanual tasks, align with these active learning principles.
Significant improvements observed in the BOT-2 hand–eye coordination subtest are consistent with the possibility that such disruptions may be reorganized through neuroplasticity-driven interventions. Targeted visual–motor exercises may stimulate relevant brain networks, particularly those involved in timing, tracking, and spatial coordination [44–47]. These findings suggest the potential value of a broader approach to amblyopia treatment one that not only addresses visual symptoms but also may reinforce the underlying neural architecture through structured motor engagement.
To our knowledge, this is among the first studies in the literature to integratively assess multiple domains VA, stereopsis, oculomotor function, hand–eye coordination, and postural balance in children with amblyopia, and to track these parameters over a medium-term follow-up period. In this regard, the study presents a novel and comprehensive model for evaluating both visual rehabilitation and broader sensorimotor development.
The findings support that children with amblyopia may benefit from monitoring not only of visual outcomes but also of broader developmental domains. Critical skills such as oculomotor control, fine-motor coordination, and balance directly linked to quality of life and daily functioning may warrant a multidisciplinary approach that goes beyond traditional visual acuity focused treatments.
Importantly, the digital, application-based therapy used in this study offered a gamified, interactive, and engaging experience, which appeared to contribute to high adherence over the six-month intervention period. These results suggest that digital therapies may be effective and feasible, representing a promising adjunct for long-term amblyopia management.

## Limitations
This study has certain limitations. First, the absence of a randomized controlled design limits the strength of causal inferences regarding treatment efficacy. While an age- and sex-matched control group was included, a parallel treatment arm (e.g., patching or another conventional therapy) could have provided a more comprehensive comparison and allowed the multidimensional effectiveness of the digital program to be assessed more directly.
Second, although the program targeted oculomotor and cognitive functions, no task-specific neuropsychological assessments (such as the Stroop test) were applied. This was a deliberate decision, as prolonged cognitive test batteries can be burdensome for children, potentially leading to fatigue, loss of attention, and reduced test validity. Instead, the study focused on performance-based functional measures that were feasible in a pediatric population (Saccade random, optokinetic an pursuit test).
Third, the follow-up period was limited to six months, which restricts conclusions regarding the long-term sustainability of treatment effects. Longer-term follow-up (e.g., one year) would provide additional insight into the durability of the observed improvements. Moreover, post-intervention reassessment was not performed, and thus the persistence of the visual and functional gains remains uncertain. Future extensions of this study will include delayed follow-up evaluations to examine the retention of benefits over time.
Additionally, the visual acuity data were obtained using a Snellen chart and converted mathematically to logMAR units for analysis. While this approach is common in clinical studies, the non-linear scaling and unequal letter counts across Snellen lines limit its precision for amblyopia research. Likewise, the Titmus stereotest used for stereopsis assessment includes monocular cues and non-uniform step size increments, which may reduce measurement accuracy. Both factors should be regarded as methodological limitations when interpreting the magnitude of visual and binocular improvements observed. Future research employing standardized ETDRS logMAR charts and cue-free stereo tests (such as Randot or TNO) would improve measurement validity.
Finally, the relatively modest sample size and single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future multicenter randomized studies with extended follow-up and inclusion of cognitive as well as subjective measures will be important to validate and expand upon these preliminary results.
Despite these limitations, the study’s multimodal design, integration of visual, oculomotor, and sensorimotor domains, and medium-term monitoring provide valuable preliminary evidence supporting the potential of digital dichoptic therapy as a feasible and comprehensive rehabilitation approach for pediatric anisometropic amblyopia.
