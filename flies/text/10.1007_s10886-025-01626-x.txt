Extinction of Innate Floral Preferences in the Generalist Solitary Pollinator Eristalis tenax.

# Extinction of Innate Floral Preferences in the Generalist Solitary Pollinator Eristalis tenax

## Abstract
AbstractInnate behaviours allow solitary animals to complete essential tasks in the absence of social learning. However, we know little about the degree to which ecologically relevant innate preferences can change, and a complete extinction of innate preferences has rarely been shown. The hoverfly Eristalis tenax, a solitary generalist pollinator, is an ideal model for studying innate behaviour in a naturalistic context because its survival depends on the innate ability to identify flowers across many habitats, which could necessitate both learning and unlearning floral objects. Innate behaviour in E. tenax has previously been considered inalterable, but we hypothesised that E. tenax could modulate their innate behaviour after training to a multimodal object derived from chemical and visual cues previously shown to be attractive to hoverflies in field and laboratory assays. To test this, we examined whether E. tenax can extinguish an innate proboscis extension response (PER) to a floral object after undergoing aversive absolute conditioning with quinine, and if flies can acquire PER to an innately unattractive object using sucrose as reinforcement. Finally, we assessed long-term memory retention of these learned behaviours. Here, we report a complete extinction of the PER to an innately attractive floral object following aversive training. Eristalis tenax can also acquire PER to an innately unattractive object after appetitive training. Flies can retain these memories for days after training, and aversive memories last longer than appetitive memories. Ultimately, these findings improve our understanding of how animals integrate innate and learned behaviours to navigate the uncertainties of dynamic objects found in their natural environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10886-025-01626-x.Keywords: Learning, Multimodal, Vision, Olfaction, Insects

## Introduction
In general, animals must rely on innate behaviour to find food, mates, and avoid predators in the complex natural world in the absence of learning (Ewert & Burghagen, 1979; Dolev and Nelson 2014). Fishes (Dixson et al. 2012; Gall and Mathis 2010; Scheurer et al. 2007) and rodents (Watanabe et al. 2022) are known to have an innate ability to identify cues from predators from birth. Furthermore, many social behaviours in vertebrates like birds (Diez et al. 2019) and mammals (Sakata et al. 2022; Wei et al. 2021) are innate, though flexible. Even the ability of humans to perceive faces developed from an interaction between innate and learned behaviour (Wilkinson et al. 2014). Innate behaviours in insects are especially critical for survival because most insects are solitary with little to no parental care (Gilbert and Manica 2015) or social learning (Arenas et al. 2009; Farina, Grüter, & Díaz, 2005). However, modulation of innate preferences is also essential in a dynamic natural world. Inflexibility in innate preference can even be maladaptive, as observed in insects like jewel beetles (Hawkeswood et al., 2005), some polarotactic insects (Horváth et al. 2010), and the butterfly Pieris virginiensis (Augustine and Kingsolver 2018). Nevertheless, a lasting extinction of innate preferences is rarely seen. Several innate characteristics are considered largely inalterable, such as the colour preference of Xenopus tadpoles (Hunt et al. 2020), choices of floral colour in bumblebees Bombus terrestris (Gumbert 2000), and visual attraction to tree-like objects in the wood ant Formica rufa (Buehlmann and Graham 2022). Solitary pollinators like monarch butterflies (Danaus plexippus) have been shown to exhibit loss of PER to an innately attractive object where rapid colour appetitive learning (Blackinston et al., 2011), avoidance of rewarding flowers with noxious stimuli, and also flexibility in foraging preferences has been documented, with foraging preferences aligned to rewarding cues regardless of innate preferences (Rodrigues et al. 2012). There is some evidence for flexibility in innate preferences from literature on this species that suggests a realignment of foraging preferences with rewarding cues regardless of innate preferences. However, when the rewards were not paired with a training cue, the butterflies reverted to their innate preference (Rodrigues et al.2012 2012). Although there is also a growing body of literature revealing the plasticity of innate behaviours, such as the modulation of innate attraction to food by host plant gustatory cues in moths (McCormick et al. 2016) or the reduction of innate aversion to CO2 in D. melanogaster in the presence of fruit odour (Faucher et al. 2006), these examples feature modifications in response intensity rather than complete extinction of innate behaviour itself. Long-term modulation of innate preferences is still relatively understudied.
Our limited understanding of the extent to which insects can extinguish innate preferences could stem from two factors. First, many studies focus on social organisms such as honeybees, in which innate behaviour and social learning are difficult to distinguish (Arenas et al. 2009; Farina, Grüter, & Díaz, 2005). Secondly, much research has also focused on the preferences of insects towards unimodal sensory cues rather than complete multimodal objects like those found in nature. Cross-modal interactions can change the valence of stimuli and alter thresholds for learning and memory retrieval (Guo and Guo 2005). For instance, fruit flies (D. melanogaster) avoid small moving targets unless they are coupled with food odour (Cheng, Colbath, & Fyre 2019 Wasserman et al. 2015).
The hoverfly Eristalis tenax is an ideal model for studying innate behaviour in a naturalistic, multimodal context. As solitary generalist pollinators (Nordström et al. 2017), E. tenax hoverflies rely on the innate ability to identify flowers for food and survival. Furthermore, ecologically relevant visual and volatile chemical cues attractive to hoverflies have been identified by sampling flowers that are attractive to hoverflies in field studies (Nordström et al. 2017). Several of the volatile chemical and visual floral cues identified from the field study have also been shown to be attractive to naïve hoverflies post-eclosion in a lab setting as well (Mishra et al. 2025). Importantly, these previous studies emphasised that hoverflies are attracted to the gestalt of these odour and visual cues, rather than to each individual sensory modality itself (Nordström et al. 2017; Mishra et al. 2025; Carnero and Navarro 2022). As a consequence, multiple volatile chemical and visual cues work in tandem for the innate attraction of hoverflies to floral objects (Nordström, et al., 2017, Mishra et al. 2025). This is similar to Gestalt principles employed by other insects that perceive objects as a whole rather than as a series of independent cues (Hertz 1929; Cook 1993).
Proboscis Extension Response (PER) has been standardised to assess olfactory, visual learning, and innate behaviours in several pollinators like honeybees (Takeda 1961; Smith and Burden 2014) and hoverflies (Neimann 2018; Lunau et al. 2018; Lunau et al. 1994). Eristalis tenax hoverflies exhibit an innate PER to yellow colour stimuli (Lunau and Wacht 1994; Neimann et al., 2018) and to appetitive chemical stimuli like sucrose (Lunau et al. 2018). This behaviour is triggered by photo and gustatory receptors on the labellum and tarsi of E. tenax (Wacht et al. 2020; Lunau and Wacht 1994), and helps the hoverflies access pollen sources on a flower (Wacht et al. 1996). Previous research has suggested that the innate preference of E. tenax for floral cues such as the colour yellow is largely inalterable (Lunau et al. 2018). However, these experiments focused on unimodal artificial visual stimuli, such as painted yellow dots (Lunau et al. 2018), that do not recapitulate the multimodality of natural floral objects from which hoverflies forage (Nordström et al. 2017). We hypothesised that E. tenax hoverflies could alter their innate behaviour towards a multimodal object containing both visual stimuli and volatile chemicals derived from flowers and shown to be attractive in field and laboratory assays to various hoverfly species.
To test this hypothesis, we examined whether the hoverfly E. tenax can alter its innate PER to a floral object when trained with quinine in an aversive conditioning paradigm. We also evaluated how quickly E. tenax can learn novel food objects using sucrose as a reinforcement. Finally, we assessed memory retention after training. Notably, the attractiveness of these multimodal floral objects was established based on responses of hoverflies to these objects in the wild (Nordström et al. 2017), and in the laboratory (Mishra et al. 2025). Exploring the extinction of innate preferences in hoverflies can help us understand how animals navigate the uncertainties of dynamic resources in the natural world.

## Methods and Materials
Study Design. To assess the plasticity of innate behaviours, we first identified an object with both volatile chemical and visual cues that elicited innate PER from untrained E. tenax flies. The protocol for assessing the innate behaviour of E. tenax flies to the yellow flower model is delineated in Fig. 1. Prior work (Nordström et al. 2017; Mishra et al. 2025) had identified relevant multimodal features (including radial symmetry, reflectance, and specific volatiles) for floral identification by hoverflies in field and lab. Since both studies found that a scented yellow flower model was more attractive than a scented blue flower model, in this present study we focused only on testing these two models rather than studying additional shapes, colours, and brightness levels. This was because we were interested in the plasticity of behaviour towards the previously identified innately attractive and non-attractive objects, not in parsing the necessary and sufficient stimuli required for innate behaviour to occur, which had already been examined in a related species (Mishra et al. 2025).

The model derived from these analyses was attractive to multiple species of hoverflies (including E. tenax) across continents in the field (Nordström et al. 2017) and in the lab (Mishra et al. 2025). Insect maintenance. Eristalis tenax pupae were obtained from Polyfly Almeria, Spain, and hatched in the lab on autoclaved vermiculite. Flies were sex-sorted upon emergence and maintained using protocols adapted from Nicholas et al. (2018), as follows: Up to forty hatched adult flies were housed together in white aphid-proof nylon mesh cages (32.5 cm × 32.5 cm × 32.5 cm) (BugDorm-4F3030) in a climate-controlled chamber maintained at 24 º C, 12 h day and night cycle, and relative humidity between 60 and 70%. Adult flies were provided distilled water, 10% w/v sugar in distilled water solution, and mustard pollen mixed with powdered sugar (1:1) ad libitum without exposure to flowers or plants. Since hoverflies lack red-sensitive photoreceptors (An et al. 2018), the pollen-sugar mixture was mixed with carmoisine red food colouring to avoid any food-colour cue associations in the hoverflies. Further, there is evidence from honeybees that while pollen can trigger PERs, it does not reinforce PER (Nicholls and Hempel de Ibarra 2013). Only flower-naïve hoverflies between the ages of 3 and 30 days following eclosion were used for experimentation. The experiments were conducted between 10 am and 7 pm, coinciding with the natural foraging times for E. tenax.
Construction of artificial floral models. The artificial floral models used in the study were 3D printed using WoL 3D printer, silk white PLA filament (WOL3D, India) and a Raised N2 3D printer (Raise3D, USA) at 40% fill density to ensure that the floral models were all identical. The innately attractive yellow flower model with odour (Fig. 1a) was based on a previously standardised model (Nordström et al. 2017) with a blend of five floral volatiles and peak reflectance in the 500–700 nm (Fig. 1d), which appears yellow to humans. The blue floral model (Fig. 1b) and the negative control (Fig. 1c) are not innately attractive to hoverflies and have been previously standardised by Nordström et al. (2017) and Mishra et al. (2025). The blue flower model has peak reflectance in 400–500 nm (Fig. 1f), and the negative control has low reflectance (400–700 nm) (Fig. 1e). The 3D-printed floral models were coated with Camlin acrylic paints (KOKUYO CO., LTD.; Japan); a white base was used, followed by paints in specific ratios (Table S2) to achieve the reflectance as mentioned above.
The reflectance of all artificial floral models was recorded using a portable spectrophotometer (Ocean Optics Jaz-200 spectrophotometer with an optic fibre tip of 25-cm, 600-µm Premium Fibre, UV/Visible, FL, USA). The spectrophotometer was calibrated to a white balance (Labsphere certified reflectance standard USRS-99-010 AS-01158–060 S/N 99AA08-0615-3676) under the 5000 lx LED lights used in the experimental setup. The instrument was then used to record the hue (the band of peak wavelengths of the reflectance spectrum) and the percent reflectance of the dominant reflected wavelength of the artificial floral models used in the study (Fig. 1).
All scented models contained the same odour blend (Tables 1 and 2) made by adding 2.5 uL of the volatile solution onto a small strip of filter paper placed underneath the surface of the 3D-printed flower so that the flies could detect the volatiles, but the filter paper strip was hidden from view (Fig. 1h). During experiments, all flower models were mounted on an artificial green stalk 20 cm high to mimic what E. tenax would encounter in the wild.
The volatiles emitted from the models were sampled using a 100 μm PDMS (Polydimethylsiloxane) SPME (solid-phase microextraction) fibre (model no:548575-U, SUPELCO, USA) at 5-minute intervals to check for contamination and release rate. The fibre was placed for 30 s within 10 cm of the models with the o + volatile blend in a cleaned and heated 500 ml beaker covered with aluminium foil. The fibre was injected into the inlet of an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph in splitless mode, and volatiles were desorbed at 270 °C. An HP-5 MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness) coupled with a 5977 A MSD mass spectrometer was used to separate volatiles. The following temperature sequence was used: column oven 40 °C for 1 min, increased to 150 °C at 25 °C/min, and then to 270 °C at 100 °C/min and held for 5 min. Compounds were identified by mass spectroscopy using Masshunter Qualitative Analysis software (B.07.00) and the mass spectra library NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology and libraries created from authentic standards) (Table 2). To avoid contamination after use of appetitive or aversive stimuli, all models used in the study were cleaned with 70% ethanol and heated at 60ºs Celsius for 15 min before and after experimentation (higher temperatures would damage the polymer models). The volatile chemicals in the odour blend used in our study were not found in the headspace of the pollen fed to the flies (fig. S4).
Electroantennography (EAG). E. tenax between 4 and 10 days of eclosion were selected for EAG and prepared as described in Batra et al. (2019). Data was acquired using EAG2000 software, and flies were exposed to air, mineral oil solvent blank, and the odour blend pipetted onto filter paper inserted in Pasteur pipettes, as described in Batra et al. (2019). A Syntech intelligent data acquisition controller (IDAC-2, SYNTECH, Netherlands) was used to collect data, and stimuli were presented as pulses of 0.5 s for 10 s of recording using a custom-built odour delivery system, standardised by Tait et al. Assessing innate behaviour. Flies used for aversive conditioning were starved for 24 h to establish a baseline PER motivation before introduction to the yellow model with odour. Experiments were conducted in a black Nylon mesh cage of side 50 cm (width × height × length: 50 cm ×50 cm ×50 cm) under diffused lighting from two 100 W flicker-free white LED flood lights covered with waxed paper (Fig. 1). The illumination of the experimental arena was 5000 ± 50 lx (mean ± s.e.) as measured using a lux meter (LX-101 A HTC instrument). Freely moving flies were given a cotton plate with distilled water for a minute to ensure proper hydration, and then placed on the yellow flower model with odour using a wooden dowel. The flies were allowed up to a minute to extend their proboscis to the yellow model and the negative control. Only flies that extended their proboscis on three different placements upon the yellow model were retained for aversive conditioning. Any flies that exhibited PER to the negative control were excluded. For appetitive conditioning, flies were starved for 12 h to ensure motivation, and then their innate behaviour towards the blue model with odour was assessed using the same procedure as mentioned above. Only flies that did not show innate PER to the blue flower with odour were used for subsequent appetitive training, which constituted 85% of all the flies screened. The wooden dowel and models were cleaned using 70% ethanol and heated for 15 min at 60º C before and after experiments.
Aversive conditioning with quinine. Flies that exhibited innate PER to the yellow flower model with odour were then repeatedly exposed to the same yellow model along with 0.05% quinine w/v solution (2–3 droplets per petal) (fig. S2a). Flies were allowed to explore the yellow model until they touched a quinine droplet with either their legs or proboscis. One such interaction counted as a single training trial (MOV S1). Most flies exited the yellow model after touching the quinine by jumping or flying off it. After two successive training trials, the flies were tested for PER to an identical copy of the yellow model without any quinine. The flies were placed on the flower for three 1-minute testing trials, and if the flies did not exhibit PER during any of these three trials, they were considered trained. If any fly exhibited PER during these trials, the flies were provided two additional training trials with quinine and tested again with three testing trials. Training trials continued in this fashion until the fly did not exhibit PER to the yellow flower with odour or until they completed 20 training trials, whichever happened first. Between each training trial and testing trial, the flies were given a paper towel to walk on to remove traces of quinine solution from their body. Flies that successfully completed the aversive training were provided with a 10% w/v sucrose solution food plate as a positive control to ensure that they could still exhibit PER to a surface that is not the yellow flower model with odour. All trained flies exhibited PER to the 10% w/v sucrose solution food plate.
Appetitive conditioning with 10% sucrose solution. Flies were exposed to an innately unattractive model (the blue flower model with odour) with 10% w/v sucrose solution placed in droplets atop the floral model (fig. S2b). All flies had been starved for 12 h prior to training to establish PER motivation, and none of the flies exhibited innate PER to the models used for appetitive conditioning. Flies were allowed to explore the innately unattractive models until they touched a sucrose droplet with either their legs or proboscis, which counted as a single training trial. After two successive training trials, flies were tested for PER using an identical unrewarded model. During this testing phase, the flies were placed on the flower for three 1-minute testing trials, and the flies were considered successfully trained if they exhibited PER during all three consecutive trials. Flies were given a paper towel to walk on between the training and testing trials to remove any traces of sucrose from their body.
Retention of aversive conditioning. Flies with innate PER to the yellow flower model with odour but not to the negative control were starved for 12 h and subsequently given 20 successive training trials with 0.05% quinine w/v solution atop the yellow model with odour (20 trials were chosen as the training threshold for retention experiments because more than 90% of flies were trained by 20 training trials) and then were evaluated for PER on an identical yellow model without any quinine for three testing trials, each lasting one minute (fig. S3a). Flies that did not exhibit PER during any of the testing trials were then tested for PER to the same yellow model without quinine on subsequent days. Flies that were trained at 0 h were then tested at several time points following training: 24 h (t1), 48 h (t2), 72 h (t3), 96 h (t4), and 120 h (t5). In between each time point, the flies were given ground pollen mixed with sugar and tinted with red food colouring for 12 h and starved for 12 h after this feeding period.
During the retention testing phase, flies were placed on the yellow flower with odour for three 1-minute periods (fig. S3a). Flies that did not exhibit PER during any of these placements were considered to have successfully retained the aversive memory. Only flies that retained aversive conditioning at any given time point were tested again at the subsequent time point.
Retention of appetitive conditioning. We chose three trials as the training threshold for appetitive retention experiments because 50% of the flies acquired the appetitive conditioning within 2–4 training trials. Flies were given three training trials with 10% w/v sucrose on the blue flower model and then tested for three testing trials lasting one minute each for PER to an identical unrewarded blue model (fig. S3b). Flies that exhibited PER in all three testing trials were then tested at: 24 h (t1), 48 h (t2), 72 h (t3), 96 h (t4), and 120 h (t5). In between each time point, the flies experienced 12 h of feeding and 12 h of starvation. Only flies that retained appetitive conditioning at any given time point were tested again at the subsequent time point. Since flies were given 20 training trials for aversive conditioning and three training trials for appetitive conditioning, we accounted for the difference in number of exposures to the object by exposing a separate set of 32 flies to 20 training trials using a 10% w/v sucrose solution placed atop the blue model with odour. These flies were tested for retention of appetitive memory using the same protocol described above.
Statistical analyses. Exponential plateau curves were fitted using GraphPad PRISM 9 to visualise the data using the least-squares method and R2 values greater than 0.95. The cumulative extinction of memory between different treatments was compared using repeated measures ANOVA and Fisher’s least square distance post hoc test. Paired t-tests were used to evaluate electroantennogram responses to the odour blend.

## Results

## Innate Attraction in E. Tenax Can Be Extinguished after Aversive Conditioning with Quinine
Based on previous free-flight field and laboratory studies parsing the multimodal floral object attractive to various hoverfly species (Nordström et al. 2017; Mishra et al. 2025), we first confirmed innate PER to the same object by flower-naive Eristalis tenax flies between the ages of 3 and 30 days following eclosion. Previous experiments showed that the E. tenax can detect the yellow and blue colours in the floral objects used (Lunau and Wacht 1944). We confirmed through electroantennography (EAG) that the flies could also detect the floral volatiles used in the object. We also showed that the flies had not been pre-exposed to these volatiles when feeding on pollen (Fig. 2.l, Table S1). For the conditioning experiments, we used flies that exhibited robust innate attraction to the yellow floral object, i.e. flies that performed PER to the object three times in a row (Fig. 1), for conditioning experiments. Once we confirmed innate PER to the yellow flower model with odour, we used aversive conditioning with 0.05% w/v quinine solution. We excluded flies (n = 2) that did not stop exhibiting PER after 50 trials, as well as any flies that exhibited innate PER to the negative control (gray disk), because these flies tended to extend their proboscis indiscriminately to random objects.
Fifty percent (50%) of the flies that received aversive conditioning with 0.05% w/v quinine on the yellow flower model with odour stopped extending their proboscis to the model in the absence of quinine after 4–6 training trials, despite exhibiting innate PER to the yellow model at the beginning of the experiment. Ninety four (94)% of flies were trained within 20 training trials; n = 69 (Fig. 3a). To account for the effects of habituation, we included a negative control experiment in which we repeated the aversive training protocol for 20 training trials without any quinine on the yellow model with odour (n = 30 flies). We also included a positive control experiment in which we repeated the aversive training protocol with 10% w/v sucrose solution atop the yellow model with odour (n = 30 trials). In both controls, flies did not stop exhibiting PER to the yellow flower with odour after 20 training trials (Fig. 3a).

## E. Tenax flies can learn to exhibit PER to an innately unattractive object after appetitive conditioning with sucrose
In appetitive conditioning, we used hoverflies that did not exhibit innate PER to the blue flower with odour. Fifty (50)% of the flies that underwent appetitive conditioning with 10% w/v sucrose on the blue flower model with odour started extending their proboscis to the unrewarded blue model with odour within 2–4 training trials, despite showing no innate attraction to the model (Fig. 3b). The fraction of the population that successfully learned plateaued at 85% of all flies tested (n = 75), and 11 flies did not learn to exhibit PER to the unrewarded blue model with odour (Fig. 3b).

## E. Tenax flies can retain aversive training memories longer than appetitive training memories
Fifty (50) % of flies lost their appetitive training memory to the blue flower with odour within 24 h (Fig. 3c, Table S3, n = 86), while 50% extinction in aversive training to the yellow flower with odour only occurred at 72 h (Fig. 3c, Table S3). At 96 h, all surviving flies showed extinction to the appetitive conditioning to the blue flower model, but 28% of flies retained aversive conditioning to the yellow flower model at the same time point (Fig. 3c; Table S2). In the batch of flies that received 20 appetitive training trials on the blue flower model, 40% of the flies lost the appetitive conditioning memory by 24 h (n = 32, fig. S5, Table S3). At 48 h, 85% of the flies lost the appetitive training to the blue flower model, and all the surviving flies lost PER to the blue model by 96 h (fig. S6, Table S3). The difference between the retention curves for the appetitive and aversive conditioning was statistically significant (p < 0.0001, repeated measures ANOVA).

## Discussion
Here, we identified an extinction of innate PER by a solitary pollinator to a floral model containing both volatile chemical and visual cues in 94% of Eristalis tenax (n = 69) after 20 aversive training trials (Fig. 3a). This training was retained for at least 48 h in more than 50% of the hoverflies (64%, n = 40, Fig. 3c). With appetitive training, more than 50% of tested E. tenax learned to exhibit PER to an innately unattractive floral object after only four training exposures, with ~ 80% of tested E. tenax exhibiting PER to this object after ten training exposures (Fig. 3b). Finally, the extinction of appetitive conditioning occurred significantly more quickly, with 50% extinction at t1 = 24 h, as compared with aversive conditioning (50% extinction at t3 = 72 h; Fig. 3c, Table S3). Our results show that innate object preferences to important appetitive food cues can be completely extinguished in a solitary pollinator, and can persist much longer than learned appetitive cues. The model used in our study incorporated multiple features detected by hoverflies. Results from electroantennography showed that the odours used in our study are detected by hoverfly antennae, and previous literature shows that E. tenax has photoreceptor sensitivity in regions that overlap with the reflectance spectra of the yellow flower model (Horridge et al. 1975). Furthermore, the model used in our study was derived from in situ sampling of odour and visual cues from flowers visited by hoverflies in multiple natural environments (Nordström et al. 2017) and thus incorporates ecologically relevant cues.
Our work sets the stage for future research into how neural circuitry changes occur during the extinction of innate behaviour in E. tenax. Other studies have shown that unlearning initially attractive stimuli involves the activation of aversive circuits (Jacob et al. 2021) rather than simply attenuating a reward circuit. Such neurophysiology might be apparent in E. tenax as well. Multisensory stimuli have also been shown to augment memory expression by binding different types of modality-specific neurons, essentially increasing the range of Kenyon cells expressing the memory engram (Okray et al. 2023). A similar mechanism could also apply to hoverflies, through which the extinction of innate preferences for multimodal stimuli could persist. Globally, hoverflies are known to visit 72% of all food crops and are robust to changes in land-use patterns and colony collapse (Doyle et al. 2020). In light of the drastic decline in insect populations (Powney et al. 2019; Rhodes 2018), hoverflies are important in pollination (Rader et al. 2015). We can take a step towards mitigating the looming pollinator crisis by understanding how generalist pollinators such as hoverflies alter their innate preferences to navigate dynamic environmental challenges. In particular, the difference in retention curves between aversive and appetitive memories (Fig. 3c) may point towards the resilience of hoverflies as pollinators since they can persistently avoid noxious stimuli while quickly taking advantage of dynamic and short-lived appetitive food sources (Fig. 3c). For example, high potassium salt levels, potentially arising from fertiliser use (Mazaka and Khiari, 2023), are aversive to other pollinators (Lau and Nieh 2016). Hoverflies in nature might learn to avoid such noxious stimuli by modifying their preferences. Similar flexibility in foraging behaviour has been documented in lepidopteran pollinators like Danaus plexippus as well, where individuals consistently avoid floral resources with noxious deterrents (Rodrigues et al. 2010).
In addition to playing an important role in evoking foraging behaviour (Balamurali et al. 2020; Wilmsen et al. 2017; Nordström et al. 2017), gustatory cues can also act as a safeguard against toxins that impair modality-specific learning (Muth et al. 2019). As seen in our experiments, quinine is behaviourally relevant to pollinators, and when coupled with suitable multimodal stimuli such as volatile chemical and visual cues, it can modulate innate preferences without severe physiological or learning impairment. Quinine has also been observed to deter pollen collection and cause switching in the foraging behaviour of other pollinators, such as bees (Tedjakumala and Giurfa 2013; Muth et al. 2016; Buatois et al.2017).
Moreover, rapid appetitive learning and rapid extinction of appetitive memory, as seen in our results (Fig. 3b and c), suggest that E. tenax can exploit short-lived flowers as food sources, which is consistent with the temporary floral constancies observed in some hoverflies (Goulson and Wright 1998). The frugal innate search template of hoverflies, coupled with their ability to unlearn the innate preference for the yellow floral model, could help E. tenax avoid sub-optimal, maladaptive, or noxious flowers, thus making them resilient generalist pollinators. This flexibility of innate behaviour gives hoverflies an adaptive advantage over other pollinators, such as honey bees, which continue to forage from unpalatable, quinine-laced flowers regardless of their energy budget (Desmedt et al. 2016).
Given this adaptive advantage, aversive learning via unpalatable reinforcement and modulation of innate preferences could be a robust safeguard against suboptimal or toxic food objects in hoverflies. For example, several pesticides, such as neonicotinoids, are tasteless (Raine and Gill 2015) and undetectable except at very high concentrations (Nagloo et al. 2023; Parkinson et al. 2023). Pollinators can, therefore, potentially consume large quantities of such toxins (Kessler et al. 2015; Arce et al. 2018; Nagloo et al. 2023). These pesticides often contaminate pollen and nectar (Zhang et al. 2023), and are detrimental even at sub-lethal doses to pollinators (Wu-Smart and Spivak 2016; Singla et al., 2020; Siviter et al. 2021). Coupling bitter compounds like quinine with tasteless contact-poison pesticides like neonicotinoids (Anadón et al. 2020) could be a viable strategy to reduce the exposure of pollinators to such pesticides and should be explored further. This approach holds promise not only because quinine can be detected by bees (Wright et al. 2010) and flies (Sellier et al. 2011) at concentrations much lower than the lethal dose, but also because the lethal dose itself for quinine (LD50 = 10mM quinine at 24 h for bees; Wright et al. 2010) is much higher than that of neonicotinoid pesticides like clothianidin (LD50 = 0.53 ppm or 25.4 ng clothianidin per bee; Yao et al. 2018). The slow extinction of aversive conditioning (Fig. 3c) is promising and suggests that aversive training could be long-lasting in protecting hoverflies against pesticides in an agricultural context.
Future experiments can move beyond our minimal flower model and assess the effects of more complex sensory cues on learning. Conducting these experiments in the field using the expansive range of sensory cues from natural flowers may offer a more comprehensive picture of the degree to which innate preferences can change in other solitary species. Countering such well-worn adages as “a leopard cannot change its spots” and “a tiger cannot change its stripes,” we show here that a generalist insect can extinguish something as fundamental as the food preference with which it was born. Moreover, a multimodal sensory context may facilitate this extinction of innate behaviour. Ultimately, these findings improve our understanding of how animals navigate uncertainties in natural environments that are constantly changing and pave the way for uncovering common principles about the flexibility of innate preferences to multimodal objects.
