ABSTRACT

11

To guide locomotion, animals control their gaze via movements of their eyes, head, and/or body,

12

but how the nervous system controls gaze during complex motor tasks remains elusive. Notably,

13

eye movements are constrained by anatomical limits, which requires resetting eye position. By

14

studying tethered, flying flies (Drosophila) in a virtual reality flight simulator, we show that

15

ballistic head movements (saccades) reset eye position, are stereotyped and leverage elastic

16

recoil of the neck joint, enabling mechanically assisted redirection of gaze. Head reset saccades

17

were of proprioceptive origin and interrupted smooth movements for as little as 50 ms, enabling

18

punctuated, near-continuous gaze stabilization. Wing saccades were modulated by head

19

orientation, establishing a causal link between neck signals and execution of body saccades.

20

Furthermore, we demonstrate that head movements are gated by behavioral state. We propose a

21

control architecture for biological and bio-inspired active vision systems with limits in sensor

22

range of motion.

INTRODUCTION

26

Animals move their eyes, head and/or body to stabilize and shift gaze. In particular, eye

27

movements display a variety of dynamic modes, consisting of interacting slow- and fast-phases

28

(Land, 1992). In response to continuous motion, human eyes will alternate between periods of

29

slow stabilizing movements and rapid, anti-directional shifts in gaze called “saccades”, a motor

30

pattern generally referred as optokinetic nystagmus (Purves et al., 2004). Periods of slow eye

31

movements and saccades are widespread in the animal kingdom, observed in primates, birds, fish,

32

crabs, and a variety of insects, although the physiological mechanisms enabling eye movements

33

vary across phyla (Land, 2019). In insects, the eyes cannot move independently of the head, thus

34

saccades are performed via both head and body movements (Collett and Land, 1975; Land, 1973).

35

Revealing the strategies insects use to control gaze, specifically how and why they perform

36

saccades, can provide unique insights into how eye movements are coordinated in locomoting

37

animals.

38

Analogous to humans, in flying flies a rotating cylinder with vertical stripe elicits smooth

39

movement and rapid reset saccades of the body and head (Collett and Land, 1975; Land, 1973;

40

Mongeau and Frye, 2017). Similarly, as in humans, fly vision is also punctuated, combining

41

continuous and discrete movements (Cellini and Mongeau, 2020a; Collett and Land, 1975; Land,

42

1973; Mongeau et al., 2019; Mongeau and Frye, 2017). Functionally, reset saccades prevent the

43

eyes from reaching their anatomical limits, which is a fundamental constraint on gaze stabilizing

44

systems across phyla. Healthy humans can move their eyes ~±45° about yaw (Shin et al., 2016),

45

whereas in Drosophila head movements about yaw have a limit of about ~±15° from body midline

46

(Cellini and Mongeau, 2020b; Fox and Frye, 2014). A visual control system combining smooth

47

movement and reset saccades could therefore overcome these classes of strict anatomical limits.

48

Whereas the interaction between smooth body movements and body saccades in flies has been

49

revealed, how head movements contribute to gaze stabilization remains poorly understood. Head

50

saccades are generally coupled with body saccades in flies and in bees and may aid in reducing

51

motion blur during a body saccade (Boeddeker et al., 2010; Schilstra and van Hateren, 1998),

52

however the dynamics and control of head saccades during gaze stabilization remain elusive. How

53

are head smooth movements and saccades coordinated and how are head reset saccades triggered?

To what extent are head and wing saccades coupled? Revealing these interactions are critical to

55

understanding the punctuated nature of fly vision and coordination in flight, and ultimately its

56

underlying neural control circuitry.

57

We investigated saccadic head and wing movements in rigidly tethered Drosophila during gaze

58

stabilization. Because the head and wings displayed distinct smooth and saccadic dynamics we

59

modeled the visuomotor response as a hybrid (switching) system (Cellini and Mongeau, 2020b;

60

Goebel et al., 2012). We confirm that during continuous visual motion, a stereotyped reset saccade

61

is triggered when the head nears its anatomical limit, wherein the head moves in the opposite

62

direction of the visual stimulus. Flies leveraged passive elastic forces of the neck joint, providing

63

an instantaneous acceleration at the start of a head reset saccade. In between saccades, flies reduced

64

retinal slip by up to 70% via smooth head movements. This stabilize-and-reset strategy enabled

65

flies to stabilize gaze quasi-continuously, overcoming neck joint anatomical limits. By correlating

66

saccade rate with the head’s excursion, we uncovered the role of proprioceptive feedback from the

67

neck sensory system in generating saccades. Head and wing saccades were triggered concurrently

68

but fixing the head reduced the wing saccade rate, further implicating proprioceptive feedback.

69

Finally, we demonstrate that head movements are gated by behavioral state. We outline a

70

framework detailing how the stabilize-and-reset strategy may apply to any noncontinuous active

71

vision system, such as mammal eyes. Altogether, our results reveal the function and mechanism

72

of head reset saccades in an active vision system.

Head reset saccades are stereotyped and punctuate smooth movement stabilization

77

Free flight body saccades in flies appear stereotyped (Muijres et al., 2015; Tammero and

78

Dickinson, 2002a). We hypothesized that head saccades would be similarly stereotyped because

79

head and body movements are generally coordinated (Van Hateren et al., 1999). To test this

80

hypothesis, we elicited an optomotor response in rigidly tethered flies by presenting constant

81

velocity visual stimuli (position ramp), which has been shown to trigger head reset saccades

82

(Figure 1A, Video 1) (Duistermars et al., 2012; Land, 1973). We developed a method to detect

83

head saccades based on the dynamics of head position and velocity and culled saccades across

84

experimental trials (Figure Supplement 1) (see Methods). Head saccades were interspersed

between bouts of smooth movements and had a significant influence on head trajectories (Figure

87

We examined saccade dynamics by extracting each saccade and aligning it to the time it

88

reached peak velocity (Figure 2A). The angular position and velocity of head saccades were

89

strongly stereotyped across all stimulus speeds, consisting of a near-symmetric velocity spike

90

lasting ~50 ms (Figure 2A). By examining the duration, amplitude, and peak velocity of saccades,

91

we found that none of the visually elicited head saccade dynamics were robustly influenced by

92

stimulus speed (Figure 2B). Overall, there was more variation in saccade rate. Specifically, flies

93

performed more saccades with increasing stimulus speed up to 120°s-1, at which point saccade rate

94

began to decrease. The difference in saccade rate across stimulus speeds suggests that the saccade

95

trigger mechanism may be dependent on visual motion. Based on these findings, we conclude that

96

head reset saccade dynamics are stereotyped but visual motion may influence their trigger.

Head saccades are anti-directional with respect to visual motion

99

Head saccades were triggered predominantly in the direction opposite of visual motion (Figure

100

2A,C). This anti-directional characteristic has been observed in previous work studying the head

101

optomotor response in flies, locusts, and other invertebrates (Duistermars et al., 2012; Land, 2019,

102

1973; Shepheard, 1974). Anti-directional head saccades appear akin to “return” eye saccades in

103

mammals that underlie optokinetic nystagmus, wherein the eyes will smoothly move in one

104

direction before rapidly returning to the center position (Purves et al., 2004). Here we refer to anti-

105

directional head saccades in flies as “reset” saccades, to emphasize the beginning of a new smooth

106

gaze stabilization period after each saccade. Head reset saccades were of opposite direction to body

107

saccades measured in a magnetic tether in previous work, wherein co-directional saccades

108

accounted for 70% of all saccades (Mongeau and Frye, 2017), although this behavior could be

109

influenced by restricting body motion in the rigid tether. Surprisingly, co-directional head saccades

110

were exceedingly rare, making up less than 2% of all saccades (Figure Supplement 2). Previous

111

work showed that anti-directional body saccade dynamics are less sensitive to visual motion speed

112

than their co-directional counterparts, which is consistent with the head saccade tuning described

113

here (Mongeau and Frye, 2017).

Another metric to characterize how head saccades may be initiated is the angular orientation

117

of the head relative to the body midline. Unlike the body, which is unconstrained and able to move

118

freely through space (unless tethered), in Drosophila the head has hard anatomical constraints that

119

limit its range of motion to ~±15° about yaw (Cellini and Mongeau, 2020b; Fox and Frye, 2014).

120

Therefore, the angular orientation of the head could play an important role in the initiation of head

121

saccades. Head reset saccades were triggered when the head was oriented near its anatomical limit,

122

i.e. head saccades triggered during counterclockwise rotation initiated near the left-side anatomical

123

limit and vice-versa (Figure 2C). On average, flies engaged in smooth gaze stabilization until the

124

head reached ±9° from body midline, then triggered a reset saccade (Figure 2C, Video 1). Head

125

orientation at the end of a saccade was similarly clustered near the opposing side (~7.5°), moving

126

the head through nearly its full range of motion. This is notably distinct from return eye saccade

127

in primates, which end near the visual center (Purves et al., 2004). Taken together, these results

128

demonstrate that head reset saccades are triggered when the head nears its anatomical limits.

129

However, the exact sensory trigger mechanism, whether of visual or proprioceptive origin, is

130

unclear.

Spontaneous head saccades are distinct from visually elicited head reset saccades

133

We hypothesized that head saccades performed in the absence of visual motion might display

134

different characteristics than visually elicited head saccades, as the role of the head is task

135

dependent about the yaw axis (Fox and Frye, 2014; Geiger and Poggio, 1977). We presented flies

136

with a static panorama and compared the dynamics of spontaneous and visually elicited reset

137

saccades (Figure 2A–D). Compared to visually elicited saccades, the peak velocity, amplitude, and

138

duration of spontaneous head saccades were reduced overall and were slightly more symmetric

139

due to the similar pre- and post-peak durations (ANOVA, DOF = 5, p < 0.001) (Figure 2A,B). The

140

reduced spontaneous head saccade dynamics were likely due to the more central trigger position

141

during saccade initiation (Figure 2C). Because flies were not stabilizing a moving panorama, the

142

head maintained a more neutral orientation. Therefore, the head was limited to a smaller range of

143

motion amplitude when performing a saccade. For spontaneous and visually elicited saccades,

144

amplitude and peak velocity were highly correlated (r ≈ 0.7 for both, p < 0.001) therefore it follows

145

that the peak velocity of spontaneous head saccades was also reduced compared to visually elicited

146

saccades (Figure 2D). Amplitude and duration were correlated to a lesser extent (r = 0.4, p < 0.001)

Head roll during head reset saccades is feedforward

154

In addition to head turns in yaw, flies also rapidly rolled their head during reset saccades

155

(Figure Supplement 4A, Video 2). Prior work demonstrated that compensatory head yaw

156

movements are present during body mechanical roll perturbations, suggesting that yaw and roll

157

coupling may be inherent to head control (Nalbach and Hengstenberg, 1994). The body banks

158

during free flight saccades, thus flies simultaneously roll their head to compensate for body roll

159

and maintain a level gaze (Schilstra and van Hateren, 1998; Van Hateren et al., 1999). Because

160

head roll is present in rigidly tethered flies—where body movement is abolished—head roll during

161

a saccade may consist largely of a feedforward (open-loop) motor command (Figure

162

Supplement 2B,C). It was previously thought that compensatory head roll is reflexive, initiated

163

primarily via feedback from flies’ mechanosensory organs—e.g. the halteres or prosternal

164

organs—however our findings suggest that this may not be the case during saccades

165

(Hengstenberg, 1988; National et al., 1986; Van Hateren et al., 1999). Magnetically tethered flies

166

also roll their head during a body saccade, although body motion is restricted to yaw; however,

167

minute body roll movement or feedback from the body yaw axis could have triggered head roll via

168

mechanosensory feedback (Kim et al., 2017). Our results provide strong evidence that head roll is

169

part of a feedforward motor command that could anticipate body roll during a saccade.

Flies leverage passive elasticity of the neck joint to initiate head reset saccades

172

In free and magnetically tethered flight, to terminate a body saccade a fly actively brakes

173

(decelerates) by applying a counter torque (Bender and Dickinson, 2006; Fry et al., 2003; Mongeau

174

and Frye, 2017; Muijres et al., 2015). The profile of a head saccade displays a similar rapid

175

deceleration that could also be indicative of active braking (Figure 3A). However, flies could rely

176

on passive mechanics of the neck joint to damp out head motion. Furthermore, if the neck were

177

sufficiently elastic, flies could also take advantage of spring forces to rapidly execute head

179

180

In short, we displaced the head from its neutral position (0°) to near its anatomical limit, where

181

head saccades typically begin (Figure 2C). We then rapidly released the head, allowing us to

182

evaluate the passive response of the head-neck system (Figure 3B, Video 3). The observed passive

183

response began at the initial displacement and rapidly moved back towards the neutral position

184

(0°), indicative of a natural stiffness within the neck joint (Figure 3C). Interestingly, the response

185

decayed slowly with no overshoot, thus pointing to a significant amount of damping (Figure 3C).

186

To reveal the contribution of passive mechanics during head saccades, we compared the

187

passive head response and actively controlled head saccade trajectories (Figure 3D). For

188

comparison, we only used passive response data for initial displacements less than 12° from the

189

neutral position to maintain consistency with the typical head reset saccade trigger position (Figure

190

2C). Strikingly, the passive response and active saccade trajectories were similar within the first

191

10–15ms, suggesting that flies may exploit the elasticity of the neck joint when initiating head

192

saccades (Figure 3D). Indeed, at the onset the initial acceleration could be generated passively as

193

opposed to directly by muscle activation (Figure 3D). However, once past this initial time frame,

194

active control would be necessary to continue moving the head, as all passively generated forces

195

would be damped out (Figure 3D). The passive mechanics accounted for ~65% of the peak velocity

196

achieved during head saccades (Figure 3D). Interestingly, the passive and active (saccade) peak

197

velocity scaled similarly with the initial head orientation (passive: r = -0.8, p < 0.001, active: r = -

198

0.89, p < 0.001), further implicating passive elasticity as a mechanism for the initial acceleration

199

in saccades (Figure Supplement 3).

200

To reveal the role of passive breaking as flies terminate a head saccade, we approximated the

201

damping in the system by fitting a first-order exponential to the passive response (Figure 3B). The

202

time constant τ was ~25 ms, roughly half the duration of an active head reset saccade (Figure 3D,E,

203

Figure 2B). The initial displacement had little effect on the time constant (r = 0.1, p = 0.4), typical

204

of a linear system (Figure Supplement 3). Remarkably, we found no significant difference between

205

the time constants of the passive response and active saccades (t-test, p = 0.3) (Figure 3E). The

206

similar time constants suggest that flies may rely primarily on passive damping to terminate a head

207

saccade, although there may still be an active braking component (Figure 3D,E). Taken together,

208

Head reset saccades enable flies to reduce retinal slip by up to 70% during smooth intervals

215

To elucidate the functional role of head saccades during gaze stabilization and gain further

216

insights into their trigger, we analyzed the smooth component of head movements for moving

217

visual stimuli (Figure 4A). Flies actively compensated for visual motion via smooth head

218

movements, as expected during the optomotor response (Duistermars et al., 2012; Land, 1973).

219

However, the head could only stabilize gaze for less than one second before reaching its anatomical

220

limit (Figure 4A,B, Video 1,4). The head had a stereotyped response during inter-saccade

221

intervals: after a preceding saccade, the head would rapidly accelerate until reaching a peak

222

velocity, then decelerate at a lesser rate until reaching near-zero velocity, signifying the head had

223

neared its anatomical limit (Figure 4A,B). The decreasing velocity over the length of the interval

224

could be a result of the passive mechanical properties of the neck joint, where the restoring force

225

at the neck increases as the head orientation deviates from the neutral orientation (Figure 3C,D).

226

By the time the head neared the anatomical limit, a reset saccade was generally triggered (Figure

227

4A, 2B). Interestingly, retinal slip speed (or velocity error) had little influence on saccade

228

initiation, as the retinal slip at the start of each saccade was approximately equal to the stimulus

229

speed due to the attenuation of stabilizing head movements (ANOVA, DOF = 4, p < 0.001) (Figure

230

4A,C). We also calculated the built-up velocity error over time, or the integrated error, and found

231

that this too scaled with stimulus speed (ANOVA, DOF: 4, p < 0.001) (Figure 4C). Unlike co-

232

directional body saccades in a magnetic tether that were triggered in response to wide-field

233

stimulus, the integrated error was not constant across stimulus speeds, and therefore may not serve

234

as a trigger for head reset saccades (Figure 4C) (Mongeau and Frye, 2017). These findings

235

therefore favor a proprioceptive-based trigger determined by the orientation of the head relative to

236

the thorax.

237

Head intervals were tuned to stimulus speed and head saccades enabled flies to quasi-

238

continuously stabilize gaze. During stabilization, the initial acceleration of the head correlated with

239

stimulus speed and reached peak speeds near the actual stimulus speed (Figure 4B). Because flies

increased the speed of head movements for faster stimuli, the time the head took to reach saturation

241

was decreased (Figure 4B). This result provides an explanation for the increasing saccade rate

242

observed with increasing stimulus speed at lower stimulus velocities (Figure 2B), as flies would

243

have to perform more reset saccades to continue performing stabilizing smooth movements.

244

Without reset saccades at the end of each interval, gaze stabilization would quickly become futile,

245

as the head would simply reach its anatomical limit and remain there. However, the hybrid,

246

saccadic control scheme allowed flies to solve this problem by rapidly resetting their head

247

orientation, enabling gaze stabilization to begin anew during the next smooth interval.

248

Occasionally, the head reached its anatomical limit and remained there for a longer period (more

249

than 1 s), thus a head saccade was not immediately triggered (Figure 4A, D). This suggests that

250

flies may be able to inhibit the saccade reflex or that the trigger may be inherently stochastic.

251

To understand the extent to which flies could reduce retinal slip error during saccade-enabled

252

smooth intervals, we computed the mean head velocity during stabilization, defined from the onset

253

of stabilizing smooth movements to saturation (head velocity = 0 °s-1) (Figure 4A). We then

254

divided the mean speed by the stimulus speed to compute a (unitless) compensatory gain (Figure

255

4E). On average, flies reduced retinal slip by up to 70% at low speeds and 30% at high speeds.

256

This is in line with prior work showing that flies can compensate for oscillatory stimuli via smooth

257

head movements by up to 60% (Cellini and Mongeau, 2020b), although the exact gain was

258

dependent on stimulus speed and head inertial delays could have a greater influence during

259

oscillatory motion. Compensatory head movements that reduce retinal slip have been shown to

260

double the range of visual motion speeds encoded by the flies active vision system (head + visual

261

system) and map the effective dynamic range of the stimulus onto the optimum of the motion

262

vision pathway (Cellini and Mongeau, 2020b). Effectively, head movements shape and slow down

263

inputs to the eyes. However, the effects of head movements were previously thought to be limited

264

to stimuli moving within the head’s anatomical limits. Here we show that flies can still slow down

265

inputs to their visual system via a combination of smooth head movements and head saccades,

266

even for stimuli that move well beyond the anatomical limits of the head. Taken together, although

267

reset head saccades do not directly contribute to gaze stabilization, they allow flies to stabilize

268

gaze effectively during punctuated smooth intervals.

Intriguingly, in the presence of a static stimulus, flies exhibited smaller oscillatory head

272

movements between spontaneous head saccades (Figure Supplement 5A, Video 5). These head

273

movements contained notable frequency components between 11–18 Hz with a peak around 15

274

Hz, possibly part of an internal “search” strategy flies executed in the absence of visual motion

275

(Figure Supplement 5C) (Geiger and Poggio, 1977). We measured the amplitude of these head

276

movements to be ~2° in the 11–18 Hz frequency band across spatial wavelengths of 22.5, 30, and

277

60° (Figure Supplement 5D). These head movements were absent when flies were presented with

278

a uniform background, suggesting that this search program is dependent on visual features (Figure

279

Supplement 5B–D, Video 6)(t-test, p < 0.001). In contrast, wing movements displayed no such

280

oscillations (Figure Supplement 5C). This is consistent with prior work showing that head and

281

wing movements are less correlated in the absence of visual motion (Cellini and Mongeau, 2020b).

282

The oscillatory head movements we observed are distinct from head “jitter”, which has been

283

reported primarily in the pitch plane (Rosner et al., 2009; Van Hateren et al., 1999). Jitter

284

frequencies in the studies were concentrated near wingbeat and haltere stroke frequencies, which

285

are greater than 120 Hz for fruit flies and blowflies, and far away from the frequency band of the

286

head movements we observed (Rosner et al., 2009; Van Hateren et al., 1999). Spontaneous head

287

saccade frequency also moderately decreased when flies were presented with a uniform

288

background, consistent with previous work (Wolf and Heisenberg, 1980) suggesting that overall

289

background luminance and/or visual features influence the trigger of spontaneous head saccades

290

(Figure Supplement 5E). Taken together, we show that even in the absence of visual motion, fly

291

eyes are not still and are punctuated by rapid spontaneous movements. These movements are

292

consistent with illusory motion in the absence of external motion (Salem et al., 2020). Although

293

we cannot infer the functional role of these head movements with confidence, they may be part of

294

a search strategy for active vision systems and may even serve to refresh visual inputs in a way

295

similar to human micro-saccades (Martinez-Conde and Macknik, 2017).

359

The head and wings switch between smooth and saccadic control simultaneously

374

If the origin of head reset saccades is truly based on proprioceptive information from the neck

375

sensory system, how does this trigger influence body saccades (via wing saccades) and are head

376

and body saccades always coupled? Indeed, a head-based proprioceptive trigger might suggest that

377

the head could perform reset saccades independently of the body. However, prior work showed

378

that head and body saccades are coupled in free flight (Schilstra and van Hateren, 1998) and in

379

magnetically tethered paradigms (Kim et al., 2017), although these studies analyzed spontaneous

380

saccades exclusively. Smooth head and wing movements in rigidly tethered flight are also coupled

381

when presented with a moving panorama (Cellini and Mongeau, 2020b; Duistermars et al., 2012;

382

Kim et al., 2015), but can be decoupled about yaw for simultaneous small- and wide-field motion

383

(Fox and Frye, 2014), thereby suggesting that a parallel visual pathway may elicit wing steering

384

responses. To elucidate head-wing interactions, we tracked wingbeat kinematics and compared

385

them to head kinematics. In the rigid tether paradigm, the difference between left and right

386

wingbeat amplitude (ΔWBA) is roughly proportional to the yaw torque acting on the body

387

(Tammero et al., 2004) (Figure 1A). Therefore, saccadic wing movements, i.e. hitches in ΔWBA,

388

indicate fictive body saccades.

389

We developed an algorithm to detect and extract wing saccades from ΔWBA traces (see

390

Methods) and then classified head saccades as either 1) having an accompanying wing saccade

391

within a 100 ms window or 2) not having an accompanying wing saccade (Figure 7A, Video 13).

392

About 50% of head reset saccades had an accompanying wing saccade, but some head reset

393

saccades occurred without a clearly identifiable wing saccade (Figure 7A, Figure Supplement 7A).

394

However, wing movements around head saccades without an accompanying wing saccade still

396

405

Interestingly, head and wing saccades lasted for a similar duration of ~50 ms, as evidenced by

406

408

rigidly tethered flies (Heisenberg and Wolf, 1986; Wolf and Heisenberg, 1980). This discrepancy

409

spawns from conflicting definitions of the time scale of wing saccades. Prior studies have classified

410

spontaneous wing saccades as long duration spikes in ΔWBA, consisting of an initial spike in

411

ΔWBA followed by a slow decay back baseline. We observed these same ΔWBA spikes in flies

412

presented with a static background (Figure 7C, Figure Supplement 5A, Video 5). However, using

413

raw ΔWBA to classify fictive body saccades in rigid tether paradigms is problematic, as the lack

414

of reafferent feedback—presumably from the halteres—could artificially exaggerate the transient

415

response (Bender and Dickinson, 2006; Dickinson and Muijres, 2016). We contend that wing

416

saccades should be classified by the rate of change of ΔWBA (see Methods), therefore only the

417

initial spike above baseline ΔWBA should define saccade duration. This is a more precise method

418

of classifying flight into smooth and saccadic bouts, in addition to being more consistent with the

419

duration of body saccades in free flight (Muijres et al., 2015). If wing saccades did truly last 500

420

ms, then this would imply that the head and wings were often operating under different control

421

(smooth vs. saccadic), which did not seem to be the case here. Taken together, head and wing reset

422

saccades are under simultaneous control and have similar durations in the rigid tether.

Proprioceptive feedback influences both head and wing saccade initiation

15

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.12.16.422884; this version posted December 16, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint

Thus far our results suggest that 1) head saccades are triggered primarily by proprioceptive

426

feedback and 2) head and wing saccades are triggered concurrently. But does proprioceptive

427

head feedback directly influence wing saccade control? For a purely vision-based saccade

428

trigger, one might predict that head-fixed flies perform saccades more frequently, due to the lack

429

of stabilizing smooth head movements resulting in increased retinal slip error. However, based

430

on our findings, we hypothesized that wing saccade rate in head-fixed flies would be less than

431

that in head-free flies, due to the head never reaching its anatomical limit. To test this hypothesis,

432

we presented head-fixed flies with the same ramp stimuli presented to head-free flies and

433

compared the overall ΔWBA response and wing saccade rate (Figure 8, Video 13). Head-fixed

434

flies exhibited a greater mean wing response with less intra- and inter-trial variance than head-

435

free flies (t-test, p < 0.001)(Figure 8A,B). Without reset saccades, we would expect the wing

436

response to be larger and less variable, thus these results suggest that wing saccades in head-

437

fixed flies occur less frequently, if at all, and/or with reduced amplitude. To confirm this, we

438

culled wing saccades from head-free and head-fixed ΔWBA traces and found that head-fixed

439

flies did indeed perform reset saccades less frequently (t-test, p < 0.001) (Figure 8C). Wing

440

proprioceptive feedback could also influence the reset saccade trigger. In the rigid tether,

441

restricting body motion causes the ΔWBA signals to operate in a broader range than in free flight

442

(Dickinson and Muijres, 2016)(see Video 4).

443

We hypothesized that a threshold in wing angular excursion could provide a signal to initiate

444

a reset saccade. To uncover the possible role of wing proprioceptive feedback, we compared the

445

ΔWBA distributions at the start and end of a saccade in head-free flies (Figure Supplement 8). In

446

addition, we compared saccade start and end distributions for the left and right WBA separately

447

(Figure Supplement 8). Compared to head saccade start and end distributions (Figure 2C), the

448

wing start and end distributions significantly overlapped in all cases and were not distinct from

449

the ΔWBA distribution, suggesting that neither absolute WBA nor ΔWBA may provide a robust

450

trigger signal for saccade initiation. Indeed, absolute wingbeat amplitude can vary substantially

451

in flies and therefore may not provide a salient trigger signal (Duistermars et al., 2007). In

452

conjunction with our prior findings, these results strongly implicate proprioceptive feedback of

453

head orientation as the primary origin for the trigger of both head and wing reset saccades.

456

By combining smooth head movement and reset saccades, flies stabilize gaze in a punctuated

457

manner, overcoming anatomical limits in a way analogous to vertebrate eye reflexes. Head reset

458

saccades were strongly stereotyped and interrupted smooth movement gaze stabilization for as

459

little as 50 ms, representing less than 5% of total flight time (Figure 2). Flies leveraged the

460

elasticity of the neck joint to initiate head reset saccades, demonstrating the role of passive

461

mechanics in head control (Figure 3). Head saccades enables flies to quasi-continuously stabilize

462

visual motion, reducing retinal slip by up to 70% between head saccades (Figure 4). Reset saccades

463

were initiated when the head was near its anatomical limits and their rate was highly dependent on

464

the operating angular range of the head, implicating proprioceptive feedback in saccade initiation

465

(Figure 2C, 6). Visually and spontaneous head saccades were triggered at different absolute head

466

positions, suggesting different trigger origins (Figure 2C). Higher stimulus speeds elicited a

467

landing response, which suppressed head smooth movements and saccades, demonstrating that

468

behavioral state gates head visuomotor responses (Figure 5). Lastly, the head and wings switched

469

between smooth and saccadic control concurrently but fixing the head significantly reduced wing

470

saccade rate (Figure 7,8). These results further support the role of proprioceptive feedback in

471

triggering reset saccades. To our knowledge, we demonstrate for the first time that wing control

472

can be directly influenced by head movements. Taken together, our work demonstrates the critical

473

function of a hybrid strategy for visuomotor control that provides a robust solution to overcome

474

sensor saturation limits.

476

Reset saccades as a hybrid control solution for systems with saturation nonlinearities

477

In the task studied here, the dynamics of the head are inherently nonlinear due to the anatomical

478

range of the joint. In control theory, this class of nonlinearity is categorized as a saturation

479

nonlinearity. Essentially, when the head is saturated (oriented at anatomical limit; Figure 4A), no

480

matter how much control effort (torque at neck joint) is applied, the head will remain in this state.

481

Interestingly, if a fly’s stabilization control strategy were purely linear, the head would saturate

482

quickly and remain at its anatomical limit, rendering further efforts to stabilize gaze futile (Figure

483

4A,B). We confirm that flies solve this problem by performing a rapid head and wing reset saccade

484

when the head nears this saturated region, allowing stabilizing movements to begin anew (Figure

485

2C, Video 1). Because reset saccades lasted ~50 ms, flies experienced only a momentary

486

disruption of smooth gaze stabilizing movements. Overall, flies were able to spend ~95% of the

17

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2020.12.16.422884; this version posted December 16, 2020. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.

487

time performing stabilizing head movements. From a design perspective, it would be optimal for

488

flies to reset their head orientation as fast as possible so as to minimize the periods where gaze is

489

not stabilized, which is consistent with the constant rapid saccade dynamics we observed across

490

stimulus speeds. The switch from smooth to saccadic control comprises a hybrid control scheme

491

that allows flies to deal with the head’s saturation nonlinearity while still performing the given

492

task (gaze stabilization) (Figure 9A). This concept can be illustrated by artificially removing

493

saccades from a fly’s head and wing trajectories (see Methods) and observing the continuous

494

stabilization response (Figure 9B). Altogether, the hybrid mechanism allows the smooth controller

495

to operate in the region where it is most effective (i.e. not saturated). Crucially, this strategy only

496

applies to a velocity sensitive control system like the gaze stabilization reflex, as opposed to a

497

position sensitive one like object localization, where reset saccades would result in swiftly

498

increasing error. Flies seldom perform reset saccades during object tracking tasks, thus supporting

499

this notion (Mongeau and Frye, 2017).

Proprioceptive mechanisms

502

The consistent head orientation at the time of saccade initiation, the influence of the head’s

503

operating range on saccade rate, and the decrease in saccade rate in head-fixed flies strongly

504

suggest that reset saccades are triggered by proprioceptive feedback (Figure 2C, 6, 8). Flies most

505

likely monitor the orientation of their head and initiate a reset saccade when the head reaches some

506

predefined threshold near the anatomical limit (Figure 8). Neural recording within the neck motor

507

system of soldier flies showed that a pair of mechanosensory hair fields located on the prothorax,

508

the prosternal organs (PO), encode information regarding head posture about yaw, pitch, and roll

509

(Paulk and Gilbert, 2006). However, in blow flies, unilaterally shaving the PO results in a postural

510

bias primarily about the roll and pitch axes, with less of an influence observed in yaw (Preuss and

511

Hengstenberg, 1992). This result suggests that there may be other mechanosensory mechanisms

512

involved in the control of head yaw. Flies have additional mechanoreceptors that may be involved

513

in head control, including the prothoracic chordotonal organs (CO) and several larger hairs on the

514

back the head (Strausfeld et al., 1987). Altogether, reset saccades are most likely triggered by the

515

stimulation of mechanosensory organs at the base of the fly neck. Our work provides testable

516

hypotheses concerning the physiological mechanisms involved in triggering reset saccades.

Potential for a conserved mechanism across species

519

In addition to fruit flies, blow flies (Land, 1973), hover flies (Collett and Land, 1975), locusts

520

(Kien and Land, 1978), beetles (Varju and Bolz, 1980), and mantids (Lea and Mueller, 1977; Liske

521

and Mohren, 1984) perform reset saccades of the head and/or body during visuomotor tasks.

522

Presumably, other insects have a similar proprioceptive mechanism that enables this behavior. The

523

locusts’ neck sensory system contains the myochordotonal organs (Shepheard, 1973), similar to

524

the CO in flies, which have a strong influence on head saccade during yaw gaze stabilization.

525

Interestingly, reset saccades virtually disappear when these organs are cut, suggesting that a

526

proprioceptive mechanism may be necessary (Shepheard, 1974). A similar concept applies to

527

mantids, where the reset saccade rate in response to a moving target is nearly cut in half when hairs

528

at the base of the neck are shaved, and saccade speed and amplitude increase (Liske and Mohren,

529

1984). Intriguingly, the idea of proprioceptive triggered reset saccades may span multiple

530

taxonomic classes, as eye movements in crabs (Horridge, 1967) and primates (Cheng and

531

Outerbridge, 1974) display remarkably similar movement patterns. Indeed, mammal eyes have

532

multiple proprioceptive mechanisms that could influence saccade control during optokinetic

533

nystagmus (Donaldson, 2000), however, to our knowledge the mechanisms underlying the trigger

534

of reset saccades during optokinetic nystagmus is not known in humans and non-human primates.

535

Altogether, a proprioceptive saccade trigger may be a conserved characteristic of active vision

536

systems.

Head-wing interactions

539

To our knowledge, we demonstrated for the first time that head orientation influences wing

540

saccades (Figure 8), establishing a causal link. Prior work has shown that 93% of saccades in free

541

flight can be linked to salient visual features in a fly’s environment (Censi et al., 2013). The

542

remaining saccades were attributed to unobservable internal states or spontaneous behavior.

543

Intriguingly, the reset saccades studied here could provide a possible explanation for a portion of

544

these “spontaneous” saccades. We propose that some of these saccades could be triggered by head

545

saturation, presumably measured by the PO or CO, or even by stochastic processes underling

546

proprioceptive feedback from hair plates. Smooth wing steering gain and coordination is also

547

influenced by head movements during gaze stabilization tasks (Cellini and Mongeau, 2020b).

548

Furthermore, fixing the head of a fly at an angle in complete darkness results in a wing steering

Neural control of saccades

555

Our results pose testable hypotheses concerning how reset saccades might be initiated in the

556

fly nervous system. The first possibility is that neck proprioceptors (PO, CO, etc.) sense when the

557

head is near its anatomical limit which triggers coordinated head and wing reset saccades via direct,

558

descending pathways to the head and wing motor centers. Head and wing neuropils are innervated

559

by many mutual descending neurons (DN) and head position influences wing steering efforts in

560

flies, thus the head-neck sensory system could directly influence both head and wing control

561

(Cellini and Mongeau, 2020b; Liske, 1977; Namiki et al., 2018). At present, it is not known

562

whether the saccade motor program is initiated peripherally or from more central brain processes.

563

Our results suggest that head angular position is linked to the trigger of reset saccades, but because

564

wing saccades precede head saccades, the saccade motor program may be located more centrally

565

(Figure 9A). We believe this is the more likely scenario because wing saccades precede head

566

saccades by ~5 ms (Figure 7). If saccades were triggered via direct pathways from the neck that

567

bypass the central brain, it would be reasonable to expect that head saccades would precede wings

568

saccades due to the shorter distance signals would have to travel. Furthermore, motor-related

569

efference copies act on peripheral visual interneurons during fictive saccades, suggesting that these

570

saccade-related signals originate from more central brain processes (Kim et al., 2017, 2015). Taken

571

together, our work suggests that saccades may originate from a motor program located more

572

centrally within the nervous system of Drosophila.

575

We found that coordinated smooth and saccadic head and wing movements are an integral

576

component of the gaze stabilization system but that these sensorimotor processes are not always

577

active (Figure 5). For high retinal slip speeds flies shifted to a landing state which significantly

578

reduced head smooth movements and saccades (Figure 5A). Flies rarely performed reset saccades

579

during landing and instead maintained a persistent head orientation biased towards one side, in the

Effects of restricting body motion

594

Our results show that rigidly tethered flies regularly perform reset saccades (Figure 2), but it

595

remains unclear whether head reset saccades are common in free flight where the body is free to

596

move. It could be that reset saccades are less prominent in freely flying flies due to body

597

movements that reduce the probability of the head reaching a saturated state. Furthermore, body

598

movements can rapidly reduce retinal slip, thereby in free flight head movements might be very

599

small to compensate for similarly small visual drift (Cellini and Mongeau, 2020b; Van Hateren et

600

al., 1999). However, over time, the accumulated drift may be sufficient to generate a reset saccade

601

via spatiotemporal integration of visual and/or mechanical origin, as demonstrated in a magnetic

602

tether (Mongeau and Frye, 2017). Unlike the head, the body has no anatomical constraints

603

restricting its range of motion, thus flies could stabilize gaze via a combination of head and body

604

movements that would seldom require a reset saccade. Nevertheless, reset body saccades have

605

been observed in freely flying hover flies, demonstrating that this behavior is not limited to rigidly

606

tethered flight (Collett and Land, 1975). Furthermore, reset body and head saccades are performed

607

by magnetically tethered flies, where the body is constrained to yaw but the head is free to move

608

(Land, 1973; Mongeau and Frye, 2017). In response to wide-field visual motion, magnetically

609

tethered flies performed reset saccades with a rate of ~0.15 Hz, more than five times less than the

610

rate reported here, although this result was dependent on stimulus speed (Mongeau and Frye,

(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made

2017). This suggests that reset saccades are activated less often in flies with unrestricted body

612

dynamics. Nevertheless, the consistent head orientation of magnetically tethered blowflies at the

613

instance of reset saccades (~10–15° from midline) supports our proposed proprioceptive-based

614

trigger (Land, 1973)(Figure 9A). Moreover, prior studies showed that co-directional body saccades

615

in magnetically tethered and freely flying fruit flies are immediately followed by a rapid anti-

616

directional head movement. Although these studies did not interpret this anti-directional

617

movement as a separate saccade, the dynamics suggest that it is indeed a reset saccade, presumably

618

executed to recenter the head to body midline (Kim et al., 2017; Van Hateren et al., 1999). An

619

exciting avenue for future research will be to elucidate the role of the head and its influence on

620

flight control in paradigms with unrestricted body dynamics.

Bio-inspired applications
With a growing interest in bio-inspired technology, many recent studies have focused on the

624

development of vision sensors inspired by insect eyes for autonomous vehicles (Serres and

625

Viollet, 2018). A particular focus has been placed on understanding and instantiating the

626

physiological and motion processing mechanisms that make insects such proficient flyers.

627

However, less emphasis has been placed on one of the most defining features of insect vision:

628

insect eyes move via head movements, and are thus active visual sensors (Bajcsy, 1988). As

629

current micro-air vehicles have recently begun to incorporate movable sensors, roboticists and

630

engineers should consider saccade-inspired hybrid control for vision systems with saturation

631

nonlinearities (Iyer et al., 2020). For example, a recent study developed a wireless steerable

632

vision sensor for insect-scale flying systems (Iyer et al., 2020). This sensor can be actuated

633

through ~60° in yaw via a piezo-based actuator, and thus has saturation constraints analogous to

634

the mechanics of an insect head. In order to continuously stabilize gaze, this sensor’s movement

635

could be programmed to perform reset “saccades”. A proprioceptive sensor, analogous to the PO

636

or CO in flies, would be all that is required to implement this control scheme. Altogether, this

637

hybrid control strategy has important applications for active vision systems in insect-scale robots

638

and beyond that must overcome limits in sensor range of motion.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

642
643

Animal preparation

644

We prepared the animals for each experiment according to a protocol that has been previously

645

described (Reiser and Dickinson, 2008). Briefly, we cold-anesthetized 3–5 day old females flies

646

(wild-type Drosophila melanogaster) by cooling them on a Peltier stage maintained at

647

approximately 4°C. We fixed flies to tungsten pins (A-M Systems) by applying a small drop of

648

UV-activated glue (XUVG-1, Newall) to the thorax. The pin was placed on the thorax projecting

649

forward at an angle of approximately 90°. For head-fixed experiments, a small drop of glue was

650

placed atop the neck joint. After a minimum 30-minute period of recovery, flies were used for

651

experiments.

652
653

Experimental protocol

654

The rigid-tether arena has been described elsewhere (Reiser and Dickinson, 2008). Briefly, the

655

display consists of a cylindrical array of 96 × 32 light emitting diodes (LEDs, each subtending

656

3.75° on the eye) that wrap around the fly, subtending 330° horizontally and 112° vertically (Figure

657

1A). Flies were rigidly mounted to a rod inside the arena and illuminated from above with a single

658

940 nm LED (Figure 1A). Flies were attached to a rigid rod and placed in the arena at an angle

659

near the natural flight angle (~45° in pitch). The position of the fly was adjusted until the fly was

660

in the frame, and in focus of the camera. Flies then underwent a closed-loop bar fixation period of

661

approximately ten seconds to ensure that the wings were being accurately measured and that the

662

flies were not visually impaired. Flies that failed to fixate the bar were not used for experiments.

663

All experimental trials were interspersed with closed-loop bar fixation bouts of five seconds to

664

ensure attentiveness throughout the length of the experiment. We recorded videos of the fly from

665

above at 200 frames per second (fps) with an infrared-sensitive camera (acA640-120gm, Basler).

666

During trials, video frames were collected using an external trigger, and synced to the start of

667

visual motion. We acquired signals (stimulus position and camera trigger) at 5 kHz.

Head saccade detection

721

To detect flight saccades, we used an Angular-Velocity based Saccade Detector (AVSD), which

722

works by analyzing the angular heading of the body/head (Censi et al., 2013). We implemented an

723

AVSD with parameters tuned specifically to Drosophila head movements (Figure Supplement 1).

724

For each trial, we computed the velocity of the head using the central difference formula (Figure

725

Supplement 1A) and then applied a 10 Hz low-pass filter to the velocity signal (Figure

726

Supplement 1B). We pooled the filtered velocity data points across all trials and computed the

727

standard deviation (Figure Supplement 1G). For each trial, we selected a saccade threshold equal

728

to 1.5 of the overall standard deviations from the trial’s median velocity. For experiments with a

729

static background and sinusoidal stimuli, we used the median of the absolute value of the velocity

730

signal. We used the MATLAB findpeaks routine to detect spikes in the filtered velocity signal

731

which were considered putative saccades (Figure Supplement 1B). We also ensured that all spikes

733

734

735

736

737

Supplement 1G). We determined the start and end of a saccade by finding when the velocity

Wing saccade detection

753

Prior work has demonstrated that flies attempt to perform body saccades while rigidly tethered

754

(Wolf and Heisenberg, 1980). These fictive saccades manifest as torque spikes which are produced

755

by hitches in ∆WBA. We refer to these hitches as wing saccades. While head saccades are

756

relatively straightforward to detect, wing saccades were much less discernible due to the larger

757

measurement noise floor. This was exacerbated for trials with a high saccade rate (e.g. Video 4).

758

We found that manually detecting wing saccades was not feasible, as wing saccades were not

759

nearly as visually distinct as head saccades overall. Prior studies have attempted to classify wing

760

saccades using algorithms that focus on the entire above-baseline spike in ∆WBA (Aptekar et al.,

761

2012; Bender and Dickinson, 2006), however here we show that head and wings saccades may be

762

best classified by analyzing the change in ∆WBA (Figure 7). We found that we could use the same

1090
