# One-year clinical outcomes of four-dose induction versus immediate treat-and-extend faricimab regimens in pretreated neovascular age-related macular degeneration

## Abstract
Background/objectivesThis study aimed to compare one-year clinical outcomes of two faricimab treatment strategies in patients with previously treated exudative neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD): (1) immediate switch to treat-and-extend dosing (T&E), and (2) initiation with a four-dose induction (“loading”) regimen.Subject/methodsRetrospective comparing two cohorts of pretreated nAMD patients with 12 months of follow-up who were either switched to Faricimab with immediate T&E dosing (Lucerne protocol, “on-the-fly”), or initiated with a four-dose induction phase (Lugano protocol,“re-loading”). Both cohorts were subsequently managed according to a T&E regimen. Demographic and clinical data, including prior intravitreal agents, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), central subfield thickness (CST) on optical coherence tomography (OCT), and length of treatment intervals were analysed. Multivariable models were adjusted for baseline differences.ResultsBoth cohorts (Lucerne, 183 eyes; Lugano, 33 eyes) were similar in baseline characteristics. BCVA improved by 0.81 ETDRS letters in Lucerne and by 2.42 in the Lugano cohort (p = 0.250). CRT decreased by -38.3 μm in Lucerne and by -57.8 μm in Lugano (p = 0.053). Treatment intervals increased by 2.2 weeks in Lucerne and 3.4 weeks in Lugano (p = 0.092). At 12 months, 15.3% of Lucerne patients and 27.3% of Lugano patients achieved intervals ≥ 12 weeks, with significantly higher odds of extension in Lugano (OR 3.87; p = 0.013).ConclusionsBoth cohorts showed anatomical and functional improvements after switching to faricimab. The Lugano cohort, using a four-dose induction phase, showed longer treatment intervals, suggesting better disease stability.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

## Introduction
The recent approval of faricimab (Vabysmo®, F. Hoffmann-La Roche AG, Basel, Switzerland) introduces a novel therapeutic option for the clinical management of patients with exudative neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), diabetic macular oedema (DMO), and retinal vein occlusion (RVO). Faricimab is the first bispecific antibody targeting both angiopoietin-2 (Ang2) and vascular endothelial growth factor-A (VEGF-A) [1, 2]. The pivotal randomized double-masked controlled phase 3 non-inferiority trials, TENAYA and LUCERNE, evaluated the efficacy, durability, and safety of intravitreal faricimab in treatment-naïve nAMD patients [3]. In both trials, most patients were able to maintain extended dosing intervals of 12 to 16 weeks throughout the 2-year follow-up period [4]. However, these trials included only treatment-naïve participants [3], providing no clear guidance on how to initiate faricimab in patients who have already undergone treatment. Following approval, clinicians and retinal specialists worldwide began using faricimab for patients who did not achieve adequate disease control with traditional anti-VEGF compounds and who required intensive anti-VEGF treatment. Over the past 2 years, multiple real-world case-series have been published worldwide and there has been ongoing debate among physicians and retinal experts on the best approach to switching pretreated patients in routine clinical practice.
It is well known that while clinical trials follow strict protocols, clinicians in real-world settings rely on their professional judgement alongside relevant local policies. Commonly, in pretreated patients two switching strategies can be applied: Patients are either switched to faricimab while maintaining their existing planned intervals following the treat-and-extend (T&E) regimen, so that the decision about the next treatment interval is not altered by the switch (“on-the-fly” switching) Alternatively, a re-loading regimen is used, in which faricimab is administered as four consecutive monthly injections during an induction phase (“re-loading”), mirroring the approach taken in pivotal phase 3 clinical trials. The on-the-fly approach offers several logistical advantages and does not impose any additional treatment burden on patients. The re-loading strategy, on the other hand, aligns the use of the new drug with the protocols of the registration studies, but it results in a substantial, albeit temporary, increase in injection frequency for patients previously treated at potentially longer intervals. Whether re-loading upon switching leads to increased long-term effectiveness remains unclear.
Recent real-world studies have begun to examine faricimab switching protocols with varying approaches. Ng et al. [5] investigated three distinct switching regimens in treatment-refractory nAMD patients: four monthly re-loading doses, an accelerated schedule with fewer than four doses, and an on-the-fly approach. However, their study did not assess potential differences in clinical outcomes based on the chosen switching strategy. Similarly, Raimondi et al. [6] compared 2 versus 3 monthly re-loading doses in aflibercept-resistant patients, finding no significant differences in anatomical or functional outcomes between the two loading regimens. While these studies provide valuable initial insights, important gaps remain in our understanding of optimal switching protocols. Notably, neither study directly compared full four-dose induction versus on-the-fly switch protocols, included only single-agent switchers, or evaluated long-term interval durability critical to real-world practice.
To address this gap, we conducted a comparative study of two cohorts of pretreated nAMD patients, who were switched to faricimab using two differing strategies, to evaluate differences in functional and anatomical outcomes and treatment intensity at 12 months follow-up time.

## Methods
This was an observational, retrospective, two-centre study which was conducted at the Department of Ophthalmology at the Cantonal Hospital Lucerne and at the Department of Ophthalmology at the Ospedale Regionale of Lugano, Switzerland. The study received approval from the relevant Ethics Committees (EKNZ 2024 − 00243 and EC Ticino 2023 − 00653) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments. All participants provided written informed consent.

## Population
Consecutive nAMD patients who were switched from a T&E therapy regimen with either intravitreal aflibercept or ranibizumab to faricimab due to high injection burden (“high-maintenance disease”) were eligible for this study. Patients requiring injection intervals of 8 weeks or less were classified as having “high-maintenance disease”, indicating the need for frequent treatment to maintain disease stability. This definition aligns with patient-reported burden literature demonstrating that injection frequencies ≤ 8 weeks are associated with significant treatment burden, impaired quality of life, and substantial economic impact on patients and caregivers [7]. This approach does not distinguish between patients with durability challenges (requiring frequent injections to maintain control) versus true treatment resistance (persistent disease activity despite optimal therapy). The inclusion criteria were a minimum treatment duration of six months before the switch and a follow-up period of at least 12 months after the switch. Patients were not stratified by nAMD subtype as per Consensus on Neovascular Age-Related Macular Degeneration Nomenclature (CONAN) due to the retrospective nature of this study and the absence of systematic multimodal imaging protocols (i.e. Indocyanine green angiography) required for definitive subtype classification [8].
Patients were excluded in case of incomplete clinical documentation before the switch, inability to obtain high-quality retinal imaging or in case of refusal to provide informed consent. For patients receiving bilateral treatment with faricimab, only one eye per patient was included in the study; if both eyes were eligible, the eye with the longer follow-up time was selected [9].

## Study and treatment protocols
At baseline (T0 = prior to switching) and all follow-up visits, patients underwent spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) using the Heidelberg Spectralis® device (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Additionally, a comprehensive ophthalmic examination was conducted, including best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (IOP), slit-lamp examination, and dilated fundoscopy at both centres.
Post-switch treatment protocols varied between the two centres. In Lugano, all patients received a re-loading dose of four faricimab intravitreal injections (IVT) every four weeks. Subsequently, at week 12, patients who demonstrated a complete clinical response, as evidenced by OCT-biomarkers, were extended to an eight-week interval, followed by a T&E regimen with ± 2 weekly intervals, as detailed in a previous study from our group [10]. In case of fluid recurrence requiring shortening of the dosing interval, the interval was maintained three times before attempting further extensions. Conversely, patients in Lucerne switched while continuing with planned intervals according to a T&E regimen, allowing for treatment interval adjustments of ± 2 weeks.

## Outcomes
The study evaluated four outcomes at 12 months of follow-up: changes in BCVA, changes in central subfield thickness (CST) defined as the mean retinal thickness between internal limiting membrane and Bruch’s membrane of the circular area within 1 mm diameter around the centre of the fovea measured in micrometres (µm), changes in treatment interval from baseline (weeks), and the proportion of patients treated at intervals equal to or greater than 12 weeks.

## Statistical analysis
Dichotomous variables were summarized with percentages while continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations (SD). Baseline characteristic differences were statistically tested using distribution-free tests. BCVA in Lucerne was measured in ETDRS letters, while in Lugano BCVA was obtained in decimals and converted to ETDRS for the purpose of this analysis. Outcomes at 12-month follow-up were assessed using multivariable models that accounted for potential confounders, including patient age, sex, mean pre-switch interval, pre-switch BCVA, and mean pre-switch CST. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 16.1 (Stata Statistical Software: Release 16 College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC), with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

## Results
A total of 183 eyes (183 patients) were included in the Lucerne cohort, while the Lugano cohort comprised 33 eyes (33 patients). Patient characteristics and clinical parameters before the switch are displayed in Table 1. The two cohorts were comparable in terms of age, sex, number of previous intravitreal injections, mean interval before switching and CST. However, the Lucerne cohort had higher BCVA compared to the Lugano cohort before switching to faricimab (69.3 Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study [ETDRS] letters (standard deviation [SD] 14.1) vs. 63.8 ETDRS letters (SD 14.6); p = 0.041).

## Changes in clinical parameters
BCVA improved by 0.81 ETDRS letters (SD 7.0) in the Lucerne cohort, while the Lugano cohort saw a slightly higher improvement of 2.42 ETDRS letters (SD 7.9). After adjusting for potential confounders, the Lugano cohort exhibited a marginally, though not significantly, greater improvement than the Lucerne cohort (1.60 ETDRS letters; 95% confidence interval (CI): -1.14 to 4.34; p = 0.250).
Regarding CST, the Lucerne cohort experienced a decrease of -38.3 μm (SD 78.6), whereas the Lugano cohort had a reduction of -57.8 μm (SD 57.0). In multivariate analysis, the Lugano cohort showed a greater but not statistically significant decrease in CST compared to the Lucerne cohort (-24.78 μm; 95% CI: -49.91 to 0.35; p = 0.053).

## Changes in treatment intervals
Compared to baseline, the treatment intervals increased on average by 2.2 weeks (SD 2.8) in the Lucerne cohort and by 3.4 weeks (SD 3.5) in the Lugano cohort. Multivariate analysis showed no significant difference in the extent of interval prolongation (0.93 weeks; 95% CI: -0.15 to 2.01; p = 0.092) after adjusting for cohort differences.
Over the 12-month follow-up period, treatment intervals were extended to 12 weeks or more in 28 out of 183 eyes (15.3%) in the Lucerne cohort and in 9 out of 33 eyes (27.3%) in the Lugano cohort. Multivariate comparison indicated that the odds of extending treatment to 12 weeks or more were significantly higher in the Lugano cohort compared to the Lucerne cohort (odds ratio (OR) 3.87; 95% CI: 1.33 to 11.21; p = 0.013).

## Discussion
High maintenance nAMD disease requires closely spaced anti-VEGF injections to effectively manage neovascular activity, creating a substantial socio-economic burden for both patients and the healthcare system. Therefore, over the past years a trend towards optimizing treatment regimens has emerged to enhance patient well-being and alleviate pressure on healthcare resources, leading to more effective disease control. In this context, exploring whether different switching treatment regimens might play a role in disease control represents a compelling area of investigation. Specifically, while a “loading dose” is widely adopted during treatment induction in treating naïve patients, it is not universally applied in real-world clinical practice for pretreated patients.
There is limited evidence in the literature regarding the benefits of re-loading when switching patients between therapeutic agents. Kamao et al. compared outcomes after switching eyes with nAMD and persistent exudation from aflibercept every 8 weeks to brolucizumab, with- versus without a loading phase and subsequent T&E regimen [11]. Although the authors concluded that switching to brolucizumab was an effective treatment option regardless of the induction regimen, a statistically significant reduction in CST was observed only in the re-loading group. This suggests that administering a re-loading phase to pre-treated patients may be beneficial for improving anatomical outcomes in those with significant retinal exudation.
Real-world studies involving patients with pre-treated nAMD receiving faricimab encompass a broad range of therapeutic switch strategies. While some authors have opted to maintain the interval of the last injection [12–16], others have chosen to initiate a re-loading phase, consisting of four monthly injections [17–20] as seen in the TENAYA or LUCERNE trials, or a shorter loading phase of 2 or 3 monthly injections [21–23].
To the best of our knowledge, no study has yet directly compared a re-loading with an on-the-fly switching strategy in pretreated nAMD patients switched to faricimab therapy.
In our study, both cohorts demonstrated improvements in BCVA and reductions in CST following the switch to faricimab, emphasizing its effectiveness and aligning with data obtained from other real-world studies [24]. Although the Lugano cohort exhibited slightly greater improvements in both BCVA and CST, these differences were not statistically significant, suggesting a broadly similar effectiveness profile across cohorts. Furthermore, treatment intervals were extended more in the Lugano cohort compared to the Lucerne cohort, with a significant proportion achieving intervals of 12 weeks or more. This may suggest better disease stability or a more robust response to treatment in the Lugano cohort, possibly attributable to the re-loading regimen, which could enhance initial disease control and subsequently permit longer intervals between treatments.
These data suggest that a re-loading phase of four monthly injections when switching to faricimab from another anti-VEGF agent may provide benefits in terms of extension of the interval between injections, thereby improving disease control and reducing the long-term therapeutic burden for the patient. However, it is important to note that our study assessed patients with high maintenance nAMD disease, encompassing a heterogenous group of patients including those with poor treatment response and persistent disease activity, where satisfactory disease control is difficult to achieve despite monthly injections, as well as those facing durability issues, who can only maintain control of the disease with frequent injections. This represents a potential limiting factor, as the group from Lucerne had a significantly higher BCVA at baseline making it is possible that these two subgroups of patients may not be entirely comparable due to possible selection mechanisms and may not equally benefit from a loading dose. An additional limitation arises from the differing protocols implemented by the centres in Lucerne and Lugano in their T&E approach. It should be noted that, in the Lugano cohort, treatment intervals were maintained thrice upon shortening, in case of fluid recurrence, which could have potentially let to more sustained disease control. Furthermore, the substantial difference in cohort sizes may reflect unmeasured selection factors that could bias outcome interpretation. The favourable interval extension rates observed in the smaller Lugano cohort therefore warrant cautious interpretation. The smaller number of studied eyes in the Lugano- compared to the Lucerne cohort limited the extent of statistical analysis. The heterogeneous nature of our patient population, encompassing different AMD subtypes without systematic stratification, limits our ability to determine whether specific neovascular phenotypes respond differentially to re-loading versus on-the-fly protocols. This represents a crucial area for future investigation, particularly given evidence suggesting that different neovascular subtypes may demonstrate distinct treatment responses to anti-VEGF therapy. Future prospective studies should incorporate systematic subtype classification using the contemporary CONAN classification system [8].
Separately, the absence of detailed prior anti-VEGF switching histories, including the sequence of previous agents, treatment durations, and reasons for prior switches represents a significant confounding factor, as patients with multiple prior switches may have more treatment-resistant disease phenotypes that could respond differently to re-loading versus on-the-fly protocols. Furthermore, the lack of systematic lens status documentation represents another uncontrolled confounding factor that may have influenced our BCVA outcomes. An additional methodological limitation is the differential BCVA measurement approaches between centres, which may have introduced systematic bias in BCVA comparisons that could not be adjusted for statistically. Lastly, we acknowledge that the lack of standardized imaging protocols represents a limitation that may introduce measurement variability. However, this reflects real-world clinical practice where technical parameters may vary while maintaining quality standards through systematic quality assessment.”
The clinical relevance of optimizing faricimab switching protocols is underscored by the ongoing prospective Light-Touch Study (ISRCTN10012824), a multicentre randomized controlled trial specifically designed to compare standard induction (four monthly injections per the Summary of Product Characteristics [SmPC]) versus a “light touch” approach (single injection followed by personalized intervals) in patients switching to faricimab. While NICE technology appraisal TA800 recommends faricimab as an option for exudative nAMD and defers dosing to the SmPC (without mandating re-loading after switching) local treatment pathways often extrapolate the SmPC’s initial induction schedule to switching scenarios, resulting in considerable heterogeneity in clinical practice [25]. Given faricimab’s dual inhibition of VEGF-A and angiopoietin-2, initial optimization through a full loading protocol may be necessary to maximize its unique mechanism of action, yet definitive guidance will depend on robust prospective data. This Phase III trial, expected to complete in 2027, will provide definitive evidence on the optimal switching strategy. In the interim, our retrospective findings offer valuable preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility and potential benefits of modified switching protocols, while highlighting the need for comprehensive anatomical assessments and patient-reported outcomes to fully understand the clinical impact of different loading strategies.
In conclusion, our study provides valuable data on the clinical utility of faricimab, supporting its use in extending treatment intervals while maintaining or improving clinical outcomes in pre-treated AMD patient cohorts. When analysing treatment outcomes, performing a complete treatment induction of 4-monthly injections at the time of switching as per label may lead to a higher odd of extended treatment intervals in patients with nAMD, over a 12-month follow-up.
Further prospective trials comparing different switching regimens on larger cohorts are needed.
Specifically, it would be interesting to highlight markers to identify the subset of patients who would benefit the most from a re-loading approach at the time of switching.
